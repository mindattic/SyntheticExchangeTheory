# Research notes: the free will and morality paper (SET-T38)

Verification record for the sources new to `submissions/free-will-and-morality/manuscript.md`. Sources already in the dissertation's reference list are verified as Appendix C records and are not repeated here; the paper uses them only for the claims the dissertation already supports with them.

Every source below was opened and the cited passages read on 2026-11-04. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entries were fetched from plato.stanford.edu as full HTML and read as text; the edition named in each reference is the one SEP's own citation tool (`archinfo.cgi`) gives for the entry, and each archive URL was checked to resolve (HTTP 200). The SEP is cited as the source of the views it reports; no primary work it discusses (Nozick, Wertheimer, Zimmerman, McGregor, Olsaretti, Pettit, Matthias, Strawson, Frankfurt) was opened, and the paper says "as Anderson reports" or equivalent wherever it relies on one. Verification level: **read in the cited passages**.

## Anderson, S. (2023). Coercion. SEP, Spring 2023 ed. (substantive revision 23 January 2023)

- Section 2.1: Nozick's conditions for "P coerces Q" (Nozick 1969, 441–445, as reported). Supports the paper's Section 3.1, first sentence.
- Section 2.2: threats and offers distinguished by a baseline; same conditional structure; "your money or your life" from a robber or a pharmaceutical company. Supports Section 3.1.
- Section 2.3: Nozick's baseline as the "normal or natural or expected course of events", predictive vs moral readings, the slave case, divergence "especially in contexts of entrenched injustice"; Wertheimer's two-pronged moralized test (worse off than the recipient ought to be, often rights violation; no reasonable choice but to succumb). Supports Section 3.1.
- Section 2.4: Zimmerman's coercive offers (island case; baseline without the proposer's interference); Anderson's remark that the coercion seems vested in the means of keeping the recipient from better terms, and that a strong bargaining position used to keep the gains may be exploitation rather than coercion; McGregor's bargaining-power account with its dependence and significant-harm conditions. Supports Sections 3.2 and 3.4.
- Section 3.3: Olsaretti's distinction between acting freely and voluntarily; a choice is voluntary iff not made because there is no acceptable alternative (Olsaretti 2004, 139, as quoted by Anderson); credible threats make alternatives unacceptable; similarity to choices forced in market conditions. The same section reports Pettit's republican freedom; the paper cites Lovett for that instead. Supports Section 3.3.

## McKenna, M., & Coates, D. J. (2024). Compatibilism. SEP, Summer 2024 ed. (substantive revision 16 April 2024)

- Opening: compatibilism is the thesis that free will is compatible with determinism. Section 1: determinism as the inevitable result of the laws of nature and the past; incompatibilists hold our self-conception as free and responsible agents would be misguided if determinism were true. Supports Section 2.1.
- Section 2.1: classical compatibilism, freedom as the ability to do what one wishes without impediments; compelled action as the benchmark of unfreedom. Supports Section 2.2.
- Sections 4.2 and 4.4: Frankfurt's hierarchical (mesh) account; reasons-responsive compatibilism. Supports Section 2.2.
- Section 3.3: Strawson's reactive attitudes (resentment at ill will; indignation as its vicarious analogue; guilt). Supports Section 5.1.

## O'Connor, T., & Franklin, C. (2026). Free will. SEP, Fall 2026 ed. (substantive revision 3 November 2022)

- Opening: "free will" as the canonical designator for a significant kind of control over one's actions; questions of whether it requires the freedom to do otherwise or self-determination, and whether it is necessary for moral responsibility. Supports Section 2.1.
- Section 2.5: libertarians united in requiring that action not be causally determined by factors beyond one's control; non-causal, event-causal and agent-causal options; libertarians not committed to denying that reasons-responsiveness is necessary, and nearly all hold that exercises of free will require it. Supports Sections 2.1 and 2.2.

## Talbert, M. (2026). Moral responsibility. SEP, Summer 2026 ed. (substantive revision 3 June 2024)

- Opening paragraphs: judging someone responsible attributes powers and capacities and views behavior as arising from their exercise; normal adult humans may have them, non-human animals and very young children are generally taken to lack them. Supports Section 5.1.

## Lovett, F. (2026). Republicanism. SEP, Fall 2026 ed. (substantive revision 12 August 2026; the archive copy carries the same revision date)

- Section 1.2: political liberty as non-domination, independence from arbitrary or uncontrolled power; Pettit's formulation (capacity to interfere on an arbitrary basis; Pettit 1999, 165, as quoted by Lovett). Supports Section 4.2.
- Section 1.3: republican freedom is negative (absence of structural dependence on arbitrary power), differs from non-interference; the slave with a non-interfering master; no slave fully free until slavery is abolished, however benevolent the master; freedom constituted by laws, institutions and norms (Pettit 1997, 107, as quoted). Supports Sections 4.2 and 4.3.
- Section 2.2: arbitrariness is not randomness (the slave who learns to predict his master is not freer); procedural and democratic accounts of non-arbitrary power. Supports Sections 4.2 and 4.3.

## Müller, V. C. (2026). Ethics of artificial intelligence and robotics. SEP, Fall 2026 ed. (substantive revision 27 March 2026)

- Section 2.7.4 (Responsibility): distributed agency and distributed responsibility; Sparrow's responsibility gap; meaningful human control; the suggestion (Simpson and Müller 2016) that the real issue may be the distribution of risk; open question whether these systems truly challenge allocations of responsibility. Supports Sections 5.2 and 5.3.

## Christman, J. (2025). Autonomy in moral and political philosophy. SEP, Fall 2025 ed. (substantive revision 22 August 2025)

- Section 3.3 (Relational Autonomy): relational accounts developed largely to analyze self-government under oppressive conditions; criticism of procedural accounts for counting as autonomous those forced to embrace the identities and value structures of oppressive environments; reference to adaptive preference formation (Khader 2011; Terlazzo 2016, not opened). Supports Section 3.5. The paper marks the claim that a person's preferences may adapt to deprivation as its own premise, not as a cited finding.

## Santoni de Sio, F., & Mecacci, G. (2021). Four responsibility gaps with artificial intelligence. *Philosophy & Technology, 34*(4), 1057–1084.

- Open access; read from the publisher's HTML full text (link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-021-00450-x). Bibliographic data from the page's citation metadata (volume 34, issue 4, pages 1057–1084).
- Abstract and Section 1: Matthias's (2004) responsibility gap for learning automata, as a dilemma between learning systems and human responsibility (Matthias not opened). Abstract and Section 2: four gaps (culpability, moral accountability, public accountability, active responsibility), several sources, also with non-learning systems. Section 3: fatalism, deflationism, solutionism. Section 4: meaningful human control. Supports Section 5.2.
