## A. Draft citations to verify

### Acquisti2015
- **APA 7:** Acquisti, A., Brandimarte, L., & Loewenstein, G. (2015). Privacy and human behavior in the age of information. *Science, 347*(6221), 509–514. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa1465
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa1465
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Crossref record and abstract; the science.org page returned 403).
- **What it actually establishes:** This is a review article, not a single study. It organizes behavioral privacy research around three themes:
  - people's uncertainty about the consequences of their privacy behavior and about their own preferences;
  - the context-dependence of privacy concern;
  - the malleability of that concern by commercial and governmental actors.

  It closes with implications for public policy.
- **Use in dissertation:** Supports the claim that the consent condition is hard to meet because preferences are uncertain, context-dependent and manipulable. The draft's paraphrase in Ch. 2 is accurate. "Lack the information" is a fair gloss of the uncertainty theme. Do not attribute specific experimental numbers to this paper without reading the full text.

### Solove2013
- **APA 7:** Solove, D. J. (2013). Introduction: Privacy self-management and the consent dilemma. *Harvard Law Review, 126*(7), 1880–1903.
- **Link:** https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-126/introduction-privacy-self-management-and-the-consent-dilemma/ (SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2171018)
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (HLR landing page and introduction). Page range 1880–1903 comes from the prior verification record.
- **What it actually establishes:** Solove names the dominant model "privacy self-management": rights of notice, access and consent that let individuals weigh costs and benefits themselves.
  - **Cognitive problems:** people cannot make informed, rational tradeoffs.
  - **Structural problems:** too many entities to manage one at a time; harms that emerge from aggregation over time; downstream uses that are unknowable at the moment of consent; and privacy treated as a series of isolated transactions when it should be assessed cumulatively and holistically.
  - **The "dilemma":** consent is often not meaningful, yet the obvious fix, paternalism, denies people the freedom to choose.
- **Use in dissertation:** Supports the claim that individual agreement becomes a formality. The draft's "does not scale" is right but narrow, since scale is one of several structural problems. The paternalism horn is a real objection to any framework that adds stricter consent requirements. The dissertation should name it.

### Solove2024
- **APA 7:** Solove, D. J. (2024). Murky consent: An approach to the fictions of consent in privacy law. *Boston University Law Review, 104*, 593–639.
- **Link:** https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2024/04/SOLOVE.pdf (SSRN doi:10.2139/ssrn.4333743)
- **Verification:** Full text read (publisher PDF: abstract, Part III, and the table of contents for the duties). I did not confirm the issue number.
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Both US notice-and-choice and GDPR-style express consent fail. Evidence of actual consent is "nonexistent" under notice-and-choice. Express consent "cannot scale," and people are pressured or manipulated.
  - Solove proposes "murky consent," a middle category between consent and non-consent. It should authorize only a "very restricted and weak license" to use data.
  - Murky consent is paired with duties on organizations (Part III.B, pp. ~632–635): a duty to obtain consent appropriately, a duty to avoid thwarting reasonable expectations, a duty of loyalty, and a duty to avoid unreasonable risk.
- **Use in dissertation:** This is the strongest recent legal-scholarship ally for the move from "consent as the legitimizer" to "terms of the exchange as the legitimizer." The duties work as the institutional half of the consent condition. It also complicates the draft: Solove concedes that most consent is fiction, so the dissertation's consent condition must say which parts rest on consent and which rest on imposed terms.

### Nissenbaum2010
- **APA 7:** Nissenbaum, H. (2010). *Privacy in context: Technology, policy, and the integrity of social life*. Stanford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1515/9780804772891
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1515/9780804772891
- **Verification:** Record located only in this session (Crossref/De Gruyter record and Open Library: Stanford Law Books, 288 pp.). An earlier session checked it against a PDF. The contextual-integrity framework is also restated in Barocas & Nissenbaum (2014), whose full text I read.
- **What it actually establishes:** Privacy is appropriate information flow. A flow is appropriate when it conforms to the context-relative informational norms of the setting where the information was shared. Those norms are specified by actors, information types and transmission principles. In the 2014 chapter, the authors use the framework to argue that consent and anonymity are the wrong tools for big data.
- **Use in dissertation:** Gives the "understands the flow" part of the consent condition a normative standard that does not depend on individual agreement.
- **Date note:** Crossref and Open Library date the book 2009 (released late 2009). The book is conventionally cited as 2010. Check the copyright page and use one year consistently.

### Viljoen2021
- **APA 7:** Viljoen, S. (2021). A relational theory of data governance. *Yale Law Journal, 131*(2), 573–654.
- **Link:** https://yalelawjournal.org/pdf/131.2_Viljoen_1n12myx5.pdf (feature page: https://yalelawjournal.org/feature/a-relational-theory-of-data-governance)
- **Verification:** Full text read (publisher PDF: abstract plus keyword-located sections). The 82-page PDF matches 573–654.
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Data production mainly aims to relate people to one another on shared features, which creates population-level interests.
  - She distinguishes "vertical" relations (data subject to collector), which current law governs, from "horizontal data relations" among people who share features. Her examples are a shared tattoo and Venntel location data used to target undocumented immigrants.
  - Both "propertarian" reforms (data as an alienable asset, including data as labor) and "dignitarian" reforms (data as an extension of selfhood) are individualist. Individualist data-subject rights cannot represent population-level effects.
  - She calls for "data as a democratic medium" governed by democratic institutions.
  - She explicitly addresses data as labor, citing Posner & Weyl (2018) at 209 and Arrieta-Ibarra et al. (2018). She points to Hitzig, Hu & Viljoen (2019) for its limits: granting a labor or property right does not secure fair conditions of sale.
- **Use in dissertation:** This is the strongest objection to an individual-exchange framework, and the draft correctly flags it. The draft should add that Viljoen targets data-as-labor directly, so the dissertation's "reciprocity insight" must survive her critique. Her claim is that the remedy must be collective and democratic, not merely priced.

### BarocasNissenbaum2014 (preferred: the Cambridge chapter)
- **APA 7:** Barocas, S., & Nissenbaum, H. (2014). Big data's end run around anonymity and consent. In J. Lane, V. Stodden, S. Bender, & H. Nissenbaum (Eds.), *Privacy, big data, and the public good: Frameworks for engagement* (pp. 44–75). Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107590205.004
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107590205.004 (author PDF: https://nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu/papers/Big%20Datas%20End%20Run%20Around%20Consent%20and%20Anonymity.pdf)
- **Verification:** Full text read (author-hosted PDF, keyword-located sections). Cambridge Core page read.
- **What it actually establishes:** Anonymity and informed consent, the "ready-to-hand" tools, both fail under big data.
  - **Consent:** they name a "transparency paradox." Simple, clear notices unavoidably lose fidelity about complex data flows.
  - **The "tyranny of the minority":** "the volunteered information of the few can unlock the same information about the many." The few who disclose implicate everyone who shares observable correlated traits. The authors stress that this problem holds even if individuals choose well for themselves.
  - **Alternative:** they propose contextual integrity in place of consent and anonymity.
- **Use in dissertation:** This is the canonical statement of the relational-data objection. It predates Viljoen and has a quotable mechanism. Cite the chapter, which matches the draft's title. The CACM piece (next entry) has a different title and only condenses the argument.

### ArrietaIbarra2018
- **APA 7:** Arrieta-Ibarra, I., Goff, L., Jiménez-Hernández, D., Lanier, J., & Weyl, E. G. (2018). Should we treat data as labor? Moving beyond "free." *AEA Papers and Proceedings, 108*, 38–42. https://doi.org/10.1257/pandp.20181003
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1257/pandp.20181003
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Crossref record with abstract).
- **What it actually establishes:** In the digital economy, user data is typically treated as capital, which ignores users' role in producing it. That framing weakens user incentives, concentrates gains, and feeds fears of automation. - **Use in dissertation:** This is the primary source for "data is labor, services are wages." Note that the authors themselves say the shift depends on countervailing power, which in exchange-theory terms is collective action. That supports the dissertation's turn to collective institutions.

## B. Social exchange theory and power-dependence

### Homans1958
- **APA 7:** Homans, G. C. (1958). Social behavior as exchange. *American Journal of Sociology, 63*(6), 597–606. https://doi.org/10.1086/222355
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1086/222355 (JSTOR 2772990)
- **Verification:** Full text read (JSTOR scan via a course page; Crossref confirms the details).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Founding statement of exchange theory. Social behavior is an exchange of goods, material and non-material (approval, prestige) (p. 606).
  - Defines profit as reward minus cost, and develops the norm of distributive justice for exchange.
  - Illustrates with Blau's study of agents who trade advice for prestige, in which the more competent receive deference.
- **Use in dissertation:** Grounds "reciprocal value" in classical sociology. Exchange covers non-material goods, so services, care and recognition count as "wages."
- **Venue note:** Some encyclopedia entries wrongly say *American Sociological Review*. The correct venue is AJS.

### Blau1964
- **APA 7:** Blau, P. M. (1964). *Exchange and power in social life*. Wiley. (Reissued 1986 by Transaction with a new introduction; later Routledge.)
- **Link:** Open Library record (Wiley, 1964, 352 pp., ISBN 0471080306). Routledge: https://www.routledge.com/Exchange-and-Power-in-Social-Life/Blau/p/book/9780887386282
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read, plus an excerpt of Ch. 4 (pp. 88–97) on a university course page and lecture notes.
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - **Social vs economic exchange:** social exchange carries "unspecified obligations" and depends on trust built up incrementally. Economic exchange is contractually specified and legally enforced.
  - **Power from imbalance:** a person who needs a service another controls, and has nothing to offer in return, has four options. They can coerce the other, get the service elsewhere, do without, or subordinate themselves. Subordination gives the other power.
- **Use in dissertation:** Blau's four options map almost directly onto the triad. "Obtain it elsewhere" is real exit. "Do without" is the baseline that must survive withdrawal (the implant problem in Ch. 1). Subordination is what happens when both fail.
- **Caveat:** The four options come from secondary notes. Confirm the page (commonly cited as Ch. 5, ~pp. 118–119) before citing.

### Emerson1962
- **APA 7:** Emerson, R. M. (1962). Power-dependence relations. *American Sociological Review, 27*(1), 31–41. https://doi.org/10.2307/2089716
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.2307/2089716
- **Verification:** Full text read (scanned PDF, text extracted).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Power is relational: "power is a property of the social relation; it is not an attribute of the actor" (p. 32).
  - **Dependence (p. 32):** A's dependence on B is directly proportional to A's motivational investment in goals mediated by B. It is inversely proportional to the availability of those goals to A outside the A–B relation. The costs of alternatives count (fn. 8 likens this to opportunity cost, using an employee's outside job options).
  - **Power:** A's power over B is the amount of B's resistance that A can potentially overcome. The core proposition is Pab = Dba, written as a pair: Pab = Dba, Pba = Dab (p. 33).
  - **Balancing operations:** an unbalanced relation can be rebalanced in four ways: (1) motivational withdrawal; (2) extension of the power network (cultivating alternatives); (3) emergence of status; (4) coalition formation (pp. 35–40).
- **Use in dissertation:** This is the theoretical core, and the draft's statement of it is correct.
  - The "not an attribute of the actor" line is a near-exact sociological precedent for "terms of exchange, not machine character."
  - "Real exit" is Emerson's availability-of-alternatives variable.
  - Data trusts and cooperatives are operation 4 (coalition formation).
  - Data strikes are operation 1 (withdrawal) used strategically.
  - Model collapse is the claim that the AI side has a non-zero Dab on humans.

### ThibautKelley1959
- **APA 7:** Thibaut, J. W., & Kelley, H. H. (1959). *The social psychology of groups*. Wiley. (Reissued 2017, Routledge, https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315135007)
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315135007
- **Verification:** Record located only (Crossref: the Routledge reissue and a 1959 review in *American Catholic Sociological Review*, doi:10.2307/3709294). Emerson (1962, fn. 7) cites the book as giving power and dependence similar emphasis. Definitions of CL and CLalt come from secondary summaries.
- **What it actually establishes (standard reading, to be checked):** Two standards for evaluating a relationship:
  - The comparison level (CL) governs satisfaction.
  - The comparison level for alternatives (CLalt) is the lowest outcome a person will accept in light of available alternatives. It governs dependence and the decision to stay or leave.

  A person can be dissatisfied and still dependent when alternatives are poor.
- **Use in dissertation:** CLalt formalizes why exit matters independently of satisfaction. A user may dislike a platform and still not leave. Cite chapter and page only after consulting the book (CL and CLalt are introduced in Ch. 6).

### RusbultVanLange2003
- **APA 7:** Rusbult, C. E., & Van Lange, P. A. M. (2003). Interdependence, interaction, and relationships. *Annual Review of Psychology, 54*, 351–375. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.54.101601.145059
- **Link:** as DOI
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Crossref abstract).
- **What it actually establishes:** A review of interdependence theory, the Thibaut–Kelley lineage. It analyzes interpersonal situations along six structural dimensions and explains, through "transformation," how long-term goals and concern for the partner reshape interaction. It also covers how repeated interdependence patterns become dispositions and relationship-specific motives.
- **Use in dissertation:** A modern, peer-reviewed entry point to Thibaut & Kelley. Rusbult's investment model (Rusbult, 1980, *JESP* 16(2), 172–186, doi:10.1016/0022-1031(80)90007-4; Rusbult, 1983, *JPSP* 45(1), 101–117, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.45.1.101; both record located only) adds investment size to satisfaction and alternatives as a predictor of commitment. That is the psychological mechanism of lock-in.

### CookEmerson1978
- **APA 7:** Cook, K. S., & Emerson, R. M. (1978). Power, equity and commitment in exchange networks. *American Sociological Review, 43*(5), 721–739. https://doi.org/10.2307/2094546
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.2307/2094546
- **Verification:** Record located only (Crossref gives 43(5), first page 721). Some secondary reference lists give 712–739. The DOI record, deposited by the publisher, supports 721.
- **What it actually establishes (standard reading, to be checked):** In laboratory exchange networks, power use follows structural dependence: actors with better alternatives extract more favorable terms. Equity concerns constrain power use only when actors can see the inequality. Commitment to particular partners reduces power use, because committed actors stop exploring alternatives.
- **Use in dissertation:** Empirical support that structure, specifically alternatives, determines outcomes. The commitment finding is a warning: loyalty reduces exploration of exit and so increases dependence.

### Cook1983
- **APA 7:** Cook, K. S., Emerson, R. M., Gillmore, M. R., & Yamagishi, T. (1983). The distribution of power in exchange networks: Theory and experimental results. *American Journal of Sociology, 89*(2), 275–305. https://doi.org/10.1086/227866
- **Link:** as DOI
- **Verification:** Record located only (Crossref).
- **What it actually establishes (standard reading):** Extends power-dependence to networks. Power depends on an actor's position (access to alternatives) in the whole network, not on centrality alone.
- **Use in dissertation:** Supports analyzing human–AI dependence at the level of the network: many users, few frontier providers, alternative data sources.

### Molm2003
- **APA 7:** Molm, L. D. (2003). Theoretical comparisons of forms of exchange. *Sociological Theory, 21*(1), 1–17. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9558.00171
- **Link:** as DOI
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Crossref abstract).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Whether exchange is *negotiated* (terms bargained explicitly) or *reciprocal* (unilateral giving with no guaranteed return) changes the causal mechanisms of power use.
  - It also changes the relative relevance of learning versus rational-choice models.
  - And it changes the salience of exchange's cooperative versus competitive "faces."
  - A companion chapter by Molm in *Advances in Group Processes* (vol. 20, 2003; secondary report) finds that reciprocal exchange produces less power use, more trust and affective commitment, and stronger perceived fairness than equivalent negotiated exchange.
- **Use in dissertation:** Complicates "legible, contract-like terms." Explicitly negotiated exchange may yield less trust than reciprocal exchange. The dissertation could specify which human–AI exchanges should be negotiated (data licensing) and which reciprocal (ongoing care).

### Hirschman1970
- **APA 7:** Hirschman, A. O. (1970). *Exit, voice, and loyalty: Responses to decline in firms, organizations, and states*. Harvard University Press.
- **Link:** ISBN 0-674-27660-4 (Open Library and library catalogues)
- **Verification:** Full text read (Chs. 1–5, course-reader PDF).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - **Exit:** leaving, which signals decline indirectly through lost revenue.
  - **Voice:** any attempt "to change, rather than to escape from, an objectionable state of affairs." Hirschman calls it political action "par excellence."
  - Voice is the only recourse when exit is unavailable, as in family, state, church, or pure monopoly.
  - Easy exit can undercut voice. The presence of exit can "tend to atrophy the development of the art of voice" (Ch. 3).
  - When quality declines across a whole industry at once, exit becomes ineffective "flitting" between deteriorating firms (Ch. 2).
- **Use in dissertation:** Real exit is necessary but not sufficient. The triad likely needs voice (collective governance) as a fourth term or as a part of exit. The industry-wide decline case maps onto frontier AI providers with similar data practices. Switching among them is not real exit.

## C. Consent theory, manipulation, exploitation

### FadenBeauchamp1986
- **APA 7:** Faden, R. R., & Beauchamp, T. L. (with King, N. M. P.). (1986). *A history and theory of informed consent*. Oxford University Press.
- **Link:** ISBN 0195036867 (Open Library: OUP, 1986, 392 pp.). Reviews: *Ethics* 98(3), doi:10.1086/292989; *APSR* 82(1), doi:10.2307/1958076.
- **Verification:** Record located only.
- **What it actually establishes (standard reading, to be checked):** Defines informed consent as a kind of autonomous authorization. An action is autonomous when done intentionally, with understanding, and without controlling influences. They distinguish this sense of consent from institutional or legal "effective consent."
- **Use in dissertation:** Supplies the philosophical criteria behind the draft's consent condition. "Understands the flow" is the understanding criterion. "Can refuse without losing access" is non-control. Verify the chapter (theory in Part III, Chs. 7–10) before citing.

### MansonONeill2007
- **APA 7:** Manson, N. C., & O'Neill, O. (2007). *Rethinking informed consent in bioethics*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511814600
- **Link:** as DOI
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Cambridge Core page with chapter list and description).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Consent consists of "distinctive communicative transactions" that waive other obligations, prohibitions and rights in controlled ways.
  - Informed consent can never be fully specific or fully explicit, and more specific consent is not always ethically better.
  - The ethics lies in successful communication (intelligibility, relevance, accuracy, honesty), not in how much information is disclosed.
  - Ch. 2 is titled "the drift from agency." Ch. 5 covers informational privacy and data protection (pp. 97–129). Ch. 7 covers trust, accountability and transparency.
- **Use in dissertation:** A principled answer to "consent doesn't scale." The test is not disclosure of every flow but whether the waiver is bounded and communicated. This fits the draft's "terms" framing better than an informational model.

### Susser2019
- **APA 7:** Susser, D., Roessler, B., & Nissenbaum, H. (2019). Technology, autonomy, and manipulation. *Internet Policy Review, 8*(2). https://doi.org/10.14763/2019.2.1410
- **Link:** https://policyreview.info/articles/analysis/technology-autonomy-and-manipulation
- **Verification:** Full text read (open-access publisher page).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Manipulation is intentionally and covertly influencing someone's decisions by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities, in short "hidden influence."
  - It differs from persuasion, which offers reasons, and from coercion, which openly constrains options.
  - It harms autonomy through the process itself, even when the outcome is good.
- **Use in dissertation:** Specifies when apparent consent fails the consent condition. The covert-influence test lets the dissertation treat AI-mediated personalization and persuasion as a consent problem.

### Mathur2019
- **APA 7:** Mathur, A., Acar, G., Friedman, M. J., Lucherini, E., Mayer, J., Chetty, M., & Narayanan, A. (2019). Dark patterns at scale: Findings from a crawl of 11K shopping websites. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 3*(CSCW), Article 81, 1–32. https://doi.org/10.1145/3359183
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3359183 (arXiv:1907.07032)
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Crossref and arXiv abstract).
- **What it actually establishes:** The authors analyzed ~53K product pages on ~11K shopping sites. They found 1,818 dark-pattern instances across 15 types and 7 categories. 183 sites used outright deceptive practices, and 22 third-party vendors sold dark patterns as turnkey products.
- **Use in dissertation:** Empirical evidence that the choice environment is engineered at scale, and that a market supplies the engineering. Consent obtained through such interfaces fails the consent condition.

### Wertheimer1996
- **APA 7:** Wertheimer, A. (1996). *Exploitation*. Princeton University Press.
- **Link:** Reviews indexed by DOI: Goodin, *APSR* 91(3), 733–734, doi:10.2307/2952107; Brighouse, *Ethics* 110(2), 448–450, doi:10.1086/233291 (both list Princeton, 1996, xiv + 316 pp.).
- **Verification:** Record located only. Content is also confirmed through the SEP entry below, co-authored by Wertheimer.
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Exploitation can be *mutually advantageous* and *consensual*: A takes unfair advantage of B even though B gains and agrees.
  - Unfairness is judged against a "fair market value" benchmark, the price in a hypothetical competitive market without informational asymmetry or undue pressure.
- **Use in dissertation:** The key philosophical complication. An exchange can meet consent and some reciprocal value and still be exploitative. Reciprocal value therefore needs a fairness standard, not just "value flows both ways."

### SEPExploitation
- **APA 7:** Zwolinski, M., Ferguson, B., & Wertheimer, A. (2022). Exploitation. In E. N. Zalta & U. Nodelman (Eds.), *The Stanford encyclopedia of philosophy* (Winter 2022 ed.). https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation/
- **Link:** as above (first published 2001; substantive revision 3 October 2022)
- **Verification:** Full text read.
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Separates the *moral weight* of exploitation (how wrong it is) from its *moral force* (whether third parties or the state may intervene).
  - Mutually advantageous, consensual exploitation is the hard case. Under the "non-worseness claim," an interaction cannot be morally worse than no interaction if it is beneficial, consensual and free of externalities.
  - Most theorists reject that claim, as the price-gouging example shows.
  - Prohibition can leave the vulnerable worse off, so redistribution that targets the underlying vulnerability is offered as an alternative to banning transactions.
- **Use in dissertation:** Supplies the "mutually beneficial exploitation" vocabulary, and the strongest argument against simply banning lopsided data-for-service exchanges. The remedy should be to fix the background vulnerability (improve exit) rather than forbid the exchange. Edition: confirm the "Winter 2022" label against the entry's archive citation.

### Zwolinski2012
- **APA 7:** Zwolinski, M. (2012). Structural exploitation. *Social Philosophy and Policy, 29*(1), 154–179. https://doi.org/10.1017/S026505251100015X
- **Link:** as DOI
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Crossref abstract).
- **What it actually establishes:** Uses sweatshops as the test case and asks whether background structural injustice makes a mutually beneficial transaction exploitative. His answer: background injustice should play "fairly little" role in determining whether a particular transaction is exploitative, even though structural injustice matters morally in other ways.

## D. Collective leverage and data institutions

### Vincent2021
- **APA 7:** Vincent, N., Li, H., Tilly, N., Chancellor, S., & Hecht, B. (2021). Data leverage: A framework for empowering the public in its relationship with technology companies. In *Proceedings of the 2021 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT '21)* (pp. 215–227). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445885
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445885 (arXiv:2012.09995)
- **Verification:** Full text read (arXiv PDF, keyword-located sections).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Data-dependent technologies give the public potential "data leverage." People can reduce, stop, redirect or manipulate their data contributions.
  - **Three levers:**
    - *data strikes*: withholding or deleting data;

### DelacroixLawrence2019
- **APA 7:** Delacroix, S., & Lawrence, N. D. (2019). Bottom-up data trusts: Disturbing the "one size fits all" approach to data governance. *International Data Privacy Law, 9*(4), 236–252. https://doi.org/10.1093/idpl/ipz014
- **Link:** https://academic.oup.com/idpl/article/9/4/236/5579842
- **Verification:** Full text read (OUP page, about 85% of the text).
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - Individuals pool their data rights in legal trusts. The trustees owe undivided loyalty, which is stricter than a duty of care, and bear the burden of proving prudence and impartiality.
  - Data subjects are both settlors and beneficiaries, choose trusts that match their values, and can move between them.
  - Ownership and contract are rejected because individuals lack bargaining power.
  - Controllers cannot credibly be "information fiduciaries" given their duties to shareholders (a critique of Balkin).
  - Pooling increases negotiating leverage and allows consent to be negotiated collectively.
  - Open issues: uptake, exit procedures for disentangling data, trustee liability, and regulatory oversight. Trusts complement the GDPR rather than replace it.
- **Use in dissertation:** The leading peer-reviewed model of the "collective counterpart" the draft calls for. It addresses all three conditions at once: collectively negotiated consent, bargained value, and exit between trusts. The authors' worry about disentangling shared data is a version of the relational-data problem at the level of exit.

### HardjonoPentland2019
- **APA 7:** Hardjono, T., & Pentland, A. (2019). *Data cooperatives: Towards a foundation for decentralized personal data management* (arXiv:1905.08819). arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1905.08819
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08819
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (arXiv abstract).
- **What it actually establishes:** Proposes data cooperatives with fiduciary duties to their members. They would hold and protect members' data, run analytics for members' benefit, and negotiate better services and discounts. Credit unions are the institutional template.
- **Use in dissertation:** The cooperative model of collective bargaining over data. This is a preprint, so pair it with the Ada Lovelace report for a vetted description.

### AdaLovelace2021

### Longpre2024
- **APA 7:** Longpre, S., Mahari, R., Lee, A., Lund, C., Oderinwale, H., Brannon, W., Saxena, N., Obeng-Marnu, N., South, T., Hunter, C., Klyman, K., Klamm, C., Schoelkopf, H., Singh, N., Cherep, M., Anis, A. ### GDPR2016
- **APA 7:** Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation). (2016). *Official Journal of the European Union, L 119*, 1–88.
- **Link:** https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj
- **Verification:** Full text read for Arts. 7, 17 and 20 (gdpr-info.eu mirror of the official text). EUR-Lex was blocked, so confirm the OJ reference there.
- **What it actually establishes:**
  - **Art. 17 (erasure).** Grounds: data no longer necessary; consent withdrawn and no other legal basis; objection; unlawful processing; legal obligation; children's information-society data.
    - 17(2): a controller that made data public must take "reasonable steps," given available technology and cost, to inform other controllers.
    - 17(3) exceptions: freedom of expression; legal obligation and public interest; public health; archiving, scientific and statistical research where erasure would seriously impair it; legal claims.
  - **Art. 20 (portability).** Applies only to data the subject *provided*, only where processing rests on consent or contract, and only where it is automated. The data must come in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format. Direct controller-to-controller transfer applies "where technically feasible." 20(4): the right must not adversely affect others' rights and freedoms.
  - **Art. 7.** 7(3): withdrawal of consent must be as easy as giving it. 7(4): whether a contract is made conditional on unnecessary consent bears on whether consent is "freely given."
- **Use in dissertation:**
  - Art. 7(4) is the legal analogue of the draft's "can refuse without losing access."
  - Art. 20 is the legal analogue of "real exit," but its scope is narrow. It excludes inferred and derived data and is limited by others' rights (the relational-data problem again).
  - Art. 17 does not clearly reach trained model parameters; Vincent et al. (2021) note that deletion-strike effectiveness depends on this.
  - The research exception in 17(3)(d) could cover some AI training.

### DataAct2023
- **APA 7:** Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 December 2023 on harmonised rules on fair access to and use of data and amending Regulation (EU) 2017/2394 and Directive (EU) 2020/1828 (Data Act). ### FarrellKlemperer2007
- **APA 7:** Farrell, J., & Klemperer, P. (2007). Coordination and lock-in: Competition with switching costs and network effects. In M. Armstrong & R. Porter (Eds.), *Handbook of industrial organization* (Vol. 3, pp. 1967–2072). Elsevier. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1573-448X(06)03031-7

## Synthesis notes

The exchange-theory tradition gives the dissertation more than a lineage. It gives the thesis a precise mechanism.

Emerson (1962) puts power in the relation, not in the actor: "a property of the social relation; it is not an attribute of the actor." He makes it a function of dependence, and dependence a function of how much one values what the other mediates and of the alternatives available outside the relation. That is the dissertation's thesis stated in sociological form: outcomes depend on the terms of exchange, not on machine character.

The triad maps cleanly onto this:
- **Real exit** is the alternatives variable (Thibaut & Kelley's CLalt; Blau's "obtain it elsewhere"; Farrell & Klemperer's switching costs).
- **Reciprocal value** is Homans's and Blau's exchange.
- **Consent** is the condition under which dependence is entered knowingly rather than engineered (Susser et al.; Mathur et al.).

Emerson's balancing operations predict the institutions the draft wants:
- coalition formation becomes data trusts and cooperatives (Delacroix & Lawrence; Hardjono & Pentland);
- network extension becomes portability, the Data Act and conscious data contribution;
- strategic withdrawal becomes data strikes (Vincent et al.).

Longpre et al. (2024) show the withdrawal operation already happening at web scale: a sharp rise in restrictions on AI crawlers in 2023–24. The human side's leverage is real when data is needed. That ties the triad to the model-collapse premise.

The literature also sets up five serious objections.

1. **Relational data (Barocas & Nissenbaum; Viljoen).** Individual consent and individual exit cannot govern flows whose value lies in inferences about others. One person's exit does not protect them if others' data predicts them. Viljoen explicitly rejects data-as-labor as one more individualist, propertarian fix. The dissertation's best answer is to make the triad apply to collective parties: consent negotiated by fiduciary intermediaries, value bargained collectively, exit between trusts. Viljoen would still say the right frame is democratic governance, not exchange.
2. **Consent may be unrepairable.** Solove (2013, 2024) and Manson & O'Neill imply that consent cannot scale or be fully informed. The defensible version of the consent condition is therefore bounded waiver plus substantive duties, not informed agreement to every flow.
3. **Exit can undermine voice.** Hirschman shows that easy exit can starve voice. When every provider declines together, exit becomes useless "flitting." AI markets with a few similar providers fit this case, so the framework probably needs voice, through collective governance, alongside exit.
4. **Mutually beneficial exploitation (Wertheimer; Zwolinski; the SEP entry).** An exchange can be consensual and beneficial to both sides and still unfair. "Reciprocal value" therefore needs a fairness benchmark, not just two-way flow. The non-worseness argument also cautions against banning lopsided exchanges when the vulnerable party gains from them. The better fix is improving their alternatives, which happens to be Emerson's prescription too.
5. **The empirical premise is weak in two places.** Diminishing returns to data (Vincent et al.) and the small "trickle" needed to avoid collapse (Ch. 2) suggest human leverage may be real but small. If AI's dependence on humans (Dab) is low, the triad describes fair terms the human side cannot enforce. That makes legal floors (the GDPR, the Data Act, AI Act Art. 53) and coalitions necessary, not optional.
