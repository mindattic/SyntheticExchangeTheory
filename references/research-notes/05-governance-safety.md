# 05: Governance, constitutional theory, commons, and AI safety: verified bibliography

Prepared 2026-10-08 for Chapters 1, 4 and 5 of *Synthetic Exchange Theory*.

**How the verification levels were assigned.**
- **Full text read.** The PDF or XML was retrieved and the relevant passages were extracted and read. This applies to Ostrom 2010, Douceur 2002, Weidinger et al. 2023, Dafoe et al. 2021, the Carpenter & Moss introduction, pp. 1–2 of Soares et al. 2015, and the front matter of Drexler 2019.
- **Abstract/publisher page read.** The publisher, proceedings, arXiv or repository landing page was opened and read.
- **Record located only.** Only a catalogue, Crossref or index record was confirmed: title, authors, venue and year. Nothing about the content was checked.

All DOIs listed were resolved against the Crossref API (api.crossref.org/works/{doi}) in this session unless the entry says otherwise. Two cases differ:
- **Content statements for "record located only" items.** These are labelled as general scholarly characterization. They should be checked against the text before being used for a specific claim.
- **Web search budget.** The session's web search budget ran out partway through. After that, verification relied on direct DOI resolution and fetching known landing pages, with no further searching.

## A. Social contract, veil of ignorance, and constitutional choice

### Rawls1971
- Rawls, J. (1971). *A theory of justice*. Belknap Press of Harvard University Press. https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674042605
- Link: https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674042605
- Verification: record located only (Crossref record for the HUP digital edition of the 1971 original. A catalogue confirms the 1971 first edition.)
- What it establishes: This is the canonical source of the original position and veil of ignorance. Parties choosing principles for the basic structure of society do not know their own place, talents or conception of the good. Rawls argues they would choose two principles: equal basic liberties, and fair equality of opportunity plus the difference principle. Rawls defends maximin reasoning as rational *only* under the special conditions of the original position.
- Use in dissertation: This is the anchor citation for Ch. 4's "veil of ignorance with real anonymity." The dissertation must state clearly that Rawls's device is a *representation of moral reasoning*, not a procedure for actual bargaining. The draft's operationalization is a departure from Rawls and should be framed as one. See Corrections.

### Rawls1999
- Rawls, J. (1999). *A theory of justice* (Rev. ed.). Belknap Press of Harvard University Press. https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674042582
- Link: https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674042582 (catalogue: ISBN 9780674000780)
- Verification: record located only (Crossref, plus catalogue records confirming the 1999 revised edition with a "Preface for the Revised Edition")
- What it establishes: This is the revised text, based on the 1975 German translation. Bookseller and catalogue descriptions say the revisions chiefly concern the treatment of liberty and primary goods. It is the edition most philosophers now cite for page references.
- Use in dissertation: Pick one edition and cite it consistently. If page numbers are quoted, use the 1999 revised edition and cite it as Rawls (1971/1999).

### Rawls1993
- Rawls, J. (1993). *Political liberalism*. Columbia University Press.
- Link: https://cup.columbia.edu/book/political-liberalism/9780231130899 (expanded ed. 2005)
- Verification: record located only (search results confirming the publisher, the year, and the later expanded editions of 1996 and 2005)
- What it establishes: Rawls recasts justice as fairness as a *political* conception. It can be endorsed through an "overlapping consensus" of reasonable but incompatible comprehensive doctrines. Stability is then explained without shared metaphysics.
- Use in dissertation: This is optional but useful. An overlapping consensus between human and AI parties with different "comprehensive doctrines" is a more defensible target than the Rawls (1971) unanimity picture. Gabriel (2020) takes the same route for AI alignment.

### Harsanyi1953
- Harsanyi, J. C. (1953). Cardinal utility in welfare economics and in the theory of risk-taking. *Journal of Political Economy, 61*(5), 434–435. https://doi.org/10.1086/257416
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1086/257416
- Verification: record located only (Crossref. The abstract was seen in a search-result summary.)
- What it establishes: This two-page note derives "social" value judgments as the preferences a person would have if they had an equal chance of occupying any position in society. It is the first statement of the impartial-observer/equiprobability device and predates Rawls's veil by nearly two decades.
- Use in dissertation: Priority and accuracy. The idea that choosing without knowing one's position yields fair rules is at least as much Harsanyi's as Rawls's. Harsanyi's version leads to average-utility maximization, not to protecting the worst-off.

### Harsanyi1955
- Harsanyi, J. C. (1955). Cardinal welfare, individualistic ethics, and interpersonal comparisons of utility. *Journal of Political Economy, 63*(4), 309–321. https://doi.org/10.1086/257678
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1086/257678
- Verification: record located only (Crossref. The abstract was seen in a search-result summary.)
- What it establishes: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. If individual and social preferences satisfy expected-utility axioms plus a Pareto condition, the social welfare function is a weighted sum of individual cardinal utilities. Interpersonal comparisons are grounded in "imaginative empathy."
- Use in dissertation: This is the formal basis of the utilitarian alternative to Rawls. It matters directly for the convention's first output (the compute percentage for basic income). An equiprobability chooser will pick a different number than a maximin chooser.

### Harsanyi1975
- Harsanyi, J. C. (1975). Can the maximin principle serve as a basis for morality? A critique of John Rawls's theory. *American Political Science Review, 69*(2), 594–606. https://doi.org/10.2307/1959090
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/can-the-maximin-principle-serve-as-a-basis-for-morality-a-critique-of-john-rawlss-theory/7B0C4B61855C7591154CABA983AF0880
- Verification: abstract/publisher page read (Cambridge Core. Crossref confirms the DOI 10.2307/1959090. An inferred DOI "10.1017/S0003055400243141" seen in a search summary does **not** resolve and should not be used.)
- What it establishes: Harsanyi argues that maximin would lead to absurd decisions under uncertainty. He holds that rational choosers behind a veil would maximize expected utility, which yields utilitarianism. He also argues that Rawls's theory would unjustly disadvantage the fortunate.
- Use in dissertation: This is the main philosophical objection to the convention's presumed output. A blind convention does not by itself determine whether parties choose a high floor or maximize the average. See Synthesis.

### BuchananTullock1962
- Buchanan, J. M., & Tullock, G. (1962). *The calculus of consent: Logical foundations of constitutional democracy*. University of Michigan Press. https://doi.org/10.3998/mpub.7687
- Link: https://www.econlib.org/library/Buchanan/buchCv3.html (Liberty Fund 1999 ed., Collected Works vol. 3)
- Verification: abstract/publisher page read. Chapter 6, "A Generalized Economic Theory of Constitutions," was read on Econlib. Crossref resolves the Michigan DOI but gives the issued year as 1960, which is a metadata error. Cite 1962.
- What it establishes: At the constitutional stage, individuals are uncertain what role they will play in a long series of future collective decisions. Self-interest therefore leads them to favor general rules rather than rules that benefit a faction. This is later called the "veil of uncertainty." The authors say explicitly that the argument fails when people can predict issues and outcomes well enough to benefit from particular rules. That is why they require general agreement (unanimity) at the constitutional stage.
- Use in dissertation: This is a better fit than Rawls for what Ch. 4 actually proposes, because it posits real self-interested choosers whose uncertainty is factual, not stipulated. It also explains Ch. 5 §3 (the veil thins with repetition). Once positions become predictable, the impartiality mechanism stops working.

### BrennanBuchanan1985
- Brennan, G., & Buchanan, J. M. (1985). *The reason of rules: Constitutional political economy*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511895937
- Link: https://www.econlib.org/library/Buchanan/buchCv10c2.html
- Verification: abstract/publisher page read. Chapter 2 was read on Econlib. Crossref resolves the DOI, which lists a 1986 online date; the print date is 1985, and APSR review and catalogue records confirm it.
- What it establishes: Uncertainty about how rules will affect oneself grows as rules become more *general* and more *durable*. That uncertainty pushes choosers toward rules that are acceptable from any position. The authors contrast their "partial veil of uncertainty," in which people reason as they actually are, with Rawls's idealized veil. They say the two converge only in the limiting case. Ch. 3, "The Myth of Benevolence," argues that institutions should be designed on the assumption that rulers are self-interested. Only the table of contents was seen for this chapter.
- Use in dissertation: This gives a design lever for Ch. 5 §3–4. Make the convention's outputs general and long-lived, such as a formula for the compute share rather than a number, so the veil stays thick even when participants are known. The homo economicus design premise also supports the draft's rejection of "enlightened self-interest" as the foundation.

### Frohlich1987AJPS
- Frohlich, N., Oppenheimer, J. A., & Eavey, C. L. (1987). Choices of principles of distributive justice in experimental groups. *American Journal of Political Science, 31*(3), 606–636. https://doi.org/10.2307/2111285
- Link: https://doi.org/10.2307/2111285
- Verification: record located only (Crossref. The JSTOR page did not render.)
- What it establishes: This is the AJPS companion to the BJPS study below. Small groups behind an experimental veil of ignorance deliberated to unanimity over distributive principles. The finding as summarized in later literature is that groups rarely choose Rawls's difference principle and usually choose average maximization with a floor. Confirm this against the text.
- Use in dissertation: Empirical evidence that real people behind a real veil choose a *floor-constrained* principle. That is directly relevant to a convention whose first output is a basic-income share of compute.

### Frohlich1987BJPS
- Frohlich, N., Oppenheimer, J. A., & Eavey, C. L. (1987). Laboratory results on Rawls's distributive justice. *British Journal of Political Science, 17*(1), 1–21. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007123400004580
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/laboratory-results-on-rawlss-distributive-justice/DD2CB2203A12790BB2B566127F4FAAA3
- Verification: abstract/publisher page read
- What it establishes: The authors tested Rawls's prediction under conditions approximating the original position. Groups did reach consensus, but not on the difference principle. They chose a principle that also weighs gains to the rest of society, and the overwhelmingly preferred option was maximizing average income subject to a floor constraint.
- Use in dissertation: Same use as the AJPS paper. This is the stronger citation because the abstract was read.

### FrohlichOppenheimer1992
- Frohlich, N., & Oppenheimer, J. A. (1992). *Choosing justice: An experimental approach to ethical theory*. University of California Press.
- Link: https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/choosing-justice-an-experimental-approach-to-ethical-theory.html ; JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.2307/jj.5233000
- Verification: record located only (catalogue and table of contents. California Series on Social Choice and Political Economy, 22.)
- What it establishes: This is the book-length synthesis of the experimental program. It has chapters on group choice of principles, group choice of the floor level, and redistribution and productivity. The acknowledgments say it builds on the 1987 papers.
- Use in dissertation: Cite it for the methodological claim that veil-of-ignorance reasoning can be operationalized and studied empirically. That is the premise of Ch. 4's "make the thought experiment operational."

### Weidinger2023
- Weidinger, L., McKee, K. R., Everett, R., Huang, S., Zhu, T. O., Chadwick, M. J., Summerfield, C., & Gabriel, I. (2023). Using the Veil of Ignorance to align AI systems with principles of justice. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 120*(18), e2213709120. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2213709120
- Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160973/
- Verification: full text read (Europe PMC full-text XML)
- What it establishes: Five online studies, N = 2,508 (2,101 after exclusions). Participants chose a principle to govern an "AI assistant" in a harvesting game. Those behind the veil more often chose to prioritize the worst-off and more often endorsed that choice after the veil was lifted. The effect was driven by fairness reasoning, not by risk attitudes or political orientation. Two findings matter especially here. First, the groupmates presented as humans were *computer bots*, so the design relied on deception. Second, in Study 4, where participants *knew* their groupmates were bots, the reflective-endorsement ("stickiness") effect weakened.
- Use in dissertation: This is the single most relevant empirical source. It shows the veil can be operationalized for AI governance. Study 4 also directly supports the dissertation's intuition that *knowing whether one's counterparties are human or machine* changes how committed people are to veil-derived principles. That is an empirical argument for the human/AI anonymity condition. Note one limit: in this study the AI is the object governed, not a party to the bargain.

### Gabriel2020
- Gabriel, I. (2020). Artificial intelligence, values, and alignment. *Minds and Machines, 30*(3), 411–437. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11023-020-09539-2
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09768
- Verification: abstract/publisher page read
- What it establishes: The normative and technical sides of alignment are interlinked. "Alignment" is ambiguous between instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, ideal preferences, interests and values. The central task is not to find the true morality but to find *fair principles that people can reflectively endorse despite moral disagreement*. The final section explores three ways of identifying such principles. The abstract does not name them; my understanding is that they include the veil of ignorance and overlapping consensus, but confirm this in the text.
- Use in dissertation: Gabriel is an ally within the safety literature. He reframes alignment as a question of fair procedure rather than control, which weakens Ch. 1's claim that the dominant frame is purely sovereign-control. Cite him when introducing the convention.

### Elster1995
- Elster, J. (1995). Forces and mechanisms in the constitution-making process. *Duke Law Journal, 45*(2), 364–396.
- Link: https://scholarship.law.duke.edu/dlj/vol45/iss2/2
- Verification: record located only (repository record. The PDF returned 403 to automated retrieval.)
- What it establishes, as a general characterization to confirm in the text: Elster distinguishes arguing from bargaining in constituent assemblies. He describes "upstream" constraints imposed by those who convene an assembly and "downstream" constraints from those who must ratify it. He argues that assemblies which double as ordinary legislatures will write in their own institutional interests. He also discusses how publicity and secrecy shape deliberation.
- Use in dissertation: This is the empirical political science of real founding conventions. It speaks directly to the "genesis rules" problem: whoever convenes the convention and sets its agenda is an upstream constraint the veil does not remove.

### Elster2000
- Elster, J. (2000). *Ulysses unbound: Studies in rationality, precommitment, and constraints*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511625008
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511625008
- Verification: record located only (Crossref, publisher table of contents. Part II deals with constitutions as constraints.)
- What it establishes: Elster revisits individual self-binding (Part I) and then constitutional precommitment (Part II). He develops a general "constraint theory." His general characterization, to be confirmed in the text, is that constitutions are less like Ulysses binding himself and more like some actors binding others.
- Use in dissertation: Ch. 5 §4 (rules that are neither unchangeable nor freely revisable) is a precommitment problem. Elster gives the vocabulary for entrenchment, delay mechanisms and supermajorities.

### Ackerman1991
- Ackerman, B. (1991). *We the people: Vol. 1. Foundations*. Belknap Press of Harvard University Press.
- Link: https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674948419 (catalogue confirmation via library records)
- Verification: record located only (catalogue record and review listings)
- What it establishes: Ackerman's "dualist democracy" distinguishes normal politics from rare "constitutional moments" of mass mobilization, the Founding, Reconstruction and the New Deal. In those moments higher lawmaking happens, sometimes outside formal amendment procedures.
- Use in dissertation: This gives Ch. 5 a model of *legitimate re-founding* that does not depend on a re-run veil. Constitutional change can be legitimate through heightened, broad deliberation even when participants know their interests.

### Lutz1994
- Lutz, D. S. (1994). Toward a theory of constitutional amendment. *American Political Science Review, 88*(2), 355–370. https://doi.org/10.2307/2944709
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/toward-a-theory-of-constitutional-amendment/9932D2055798BE28EF9CF40BAD4E2F7D
- Verification: abstract/publisher page read
- What it establishes: The study covers all amendments to U.S. state constitutions since 1776 and tests the patterns against 32 national constitutions. Amendment rate is largely explained by the interaction of constitution length and amendment difficulty. Secondary summaries report that moderate amendment rates go with longevity and that raising amendment difficulty is an "inefficient" way to reduce amendments.
- Use in dissertation: This is evidence for Ch. 5 §4. A short, general founding document with a moderate amendment path is more durable than an entrenched one. That argues against making the compute-percentage rule unchangeable.

## B. Commons governance and polycentricity

### Ostrom1990
- Ostrom, E. (1990). *Governing the commons: The evolution of institutions for collective action*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511807763
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511807763 (Canto Classics reissue 2015: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781316423936)
- Verification: record located only for the book itself (Crossref. Series: The Political Economy of Institutions and Decisions). The design-principle wordings below are verified through Ostrom (2010, full text read) and Cox et al. (2010, article body read), which quote the 1990 text with page numbers.
- Edition: First edition, Cambridge University Press, 1990. The design principles appear in Table 3.1, *Design principles illustrated by long-enduring CPR institutions*, p. 90; this location is attested by library citation records and Cox et al. Cite the 1990 edition. The 2015 Canto reissue is the same text.
- **The eight design principles. Original 1990 labels, with the 2010 wording Ostrom herself adopted from Cox et al.:**
  1. **Clearly defined boundaries.** 1A, user boundaries: clear and locally understood boundaries between legitimate users and nonusers. 1B, resource boundaries: clear boundaries that separate the resource from the larger social-ecological system.
  2. **Congruence between appropriation and provision rules and local conditions.** 2A: rules are congruent with local social and environmental conditions. 2B: appropriation rules are congruent with provision rules, and the distribution of costs is proportional to the distribution of benefits.
  3. **Collective-choice arrangements.** Most individuals affected by a resource regime are authorized to participate in making and modifying its rules. The 1990 wording, per Cox et al., refers to modifying the *operational* rules.
  4. **Monitoring.** 4A: individuals who are accountable to the users, or are the users, monitor the users' appropriation and provision. 4B: the same people monitor the condition of the resource.
  5. **Graduated sanctions.** Sanctions for rule violations start very low but become stronger if a user repeatedly violates a rule.
  6. **Conflict-resolution mechanisms.** Rapid, low-cost, local arenas exist for resolving conflicts among users or with officials.
  7. **Minimal recognition of rights to organize.** Government recognizes the right of local users to make their own rules.
  8. **Nested enterprises.** This applies to resources that are parts of larger systems. Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution and governance are organized in multiple nested layers.
- What it establishes: From field cases (Swiss and Japanese mountain commons, Spanish and Philippine irrigation, Turkish and Canadian fisheries, among others), self-organized communities sometimes sustain common-pool resources without either privatization or central state control. Long-enduring cases share the regularities above. Ostrom presents them as regularities, not as sufficient or necessary conditions.
- Use in dissertation: This is the core grounding for Ch. 4's "neither market nor sovereign" claim. See Corrections for how the draft under-uses and partly mis-maps the principles.

### Ostrom2010
- Ostrom, E. (2010). Beyond markets and states: Polycentric governance of complex economic systems. *American Economic Review, 100*(3), 641–672. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.100.3.641
- Link: https://web.pdx.edu/~nwallace/EHP/OstromPolyGov.pdf (Nobel Foundation reprint)
- Verification: full text read (PDF extracted; the design-principles list is on p. 653)
- What it establishes: This is the revised Nobel lecture. Ostrom argues against the market/state dichotomy and presents polycentric governance, meaning many formally independent centres of decision-making that may function as a system. She gives the updated eight-principle list above and reports that roughly two-thirds of the studies reviewed by Cox et al. confirm that robust systems show most principles and failures do not. She adopts Cox et al.'s refinements to principles 1, 2 and 4. In footnote 5 she says she perhaps should have called them "best practices," because "design principle" confused readers.
- Use in dissertation: Cite this for the exact principle wording and for polycentricity as a non-sovereign governance form at scale. Polycentricity is a stronger theoretical ally than any single principle.

### Cox2010
- Cox, M., Arnold, G., & Villamayor Tomás, S. (2010). A review of design principles for community-based natural resource management. *Ecology and Society, 15*(4), Article 38. https://doi.org/10.5751/ES-03704-150438
- Link: https://www.ecologyandsociety.org/vol15/iss4/art38/
- Verification: abstract/publisher page read (the article body was read; Tables 1 and 4 did not render)
- What it establishes: The review covers 91 studies (77 coded cases). The principles are "well supported empirically." The strongest support is for 2A, 2B and 4B, then 1A. The other principles have moderate support, and principle 8 is significant only at the 10% level. The authors note that the principles are incomplete because they omit external socioeconomic and biophysical factors. They split principles 1, 2 and 4 into the A/B forms.
- Use in dissertation: This is the evidence base. Note that *nested enterprises* (8), the principle closest to the dissertation's "national AGIs under a shared global unit," is the least supported.

### Wilson2013
- Wilson, D. S., Ostrom, E., & Cox, M. E. (2013). Generalizing the core design principles for the efficacy of groups. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 90*(Suppl.), S21–S32. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2012.12.010
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2012.12.010
- Verification: record located only (Crossref)
- What it establishes: Ostrom and coauthors argue that the core design principles generalize beyond common-pool resources to many kinds of cooperative groups, grounding them in multilevel selection theory.
- Use in dissertation: This is the citation for *extending* Ostrom beyond natural resources to compute and data commons. Without it, the transfer is the dissertation's own inference.

### HessOstrom2007
- Hess, C., & Ostrom, E. (Eds.). (2007). *Understanding knowledge as a commons: From theory to practice*. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6980.001.0001
- Link: https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6980.001.0001
- Verification: record located only (Crossref gives an issued date of 1 December 2006; the hardcover is widely cited as 2007. Either year is defensible, but be consistent.)
- What it establishes: This edited volume applies commons analysis, including Ostrom's Institutional Analysis and Development framework, to knowledge and digital information. Knowledge is treated as a shared resource with distinct rivalry and exclusion properties.
- Use in dissertation: This bridges natural-resource commons to data and knowledge commons. It fits the dissertation's treatment of human-generated data as a shared resource.

### Hardin1968
- Hardin, G. (1968). The tragedy of the commons. *Science, 162*(3859), 1243–1248. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.162.3859.1243
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.162.3859.1243
- Verification: record located only (Crossref. The PDF found was a scan with no text layer.)
- What it establishes: This is the classic argument that open-access shared resources are over-exploited by individually rational users. Hardin's remedies were privatization or coercive regulation ("mutual coercion mutually agreed upon"; that phrasing is from memory and should be checked before quoting).
- Use in dissertation: This is the foil Ostrom answers. Note that Hardin's case is an *open-access* resource with no boundaries, which is why Ostrom's principle 1 matters so much for an open, anonymous convention.

### OstromTieboutWarren1961
- Ostrom, V., Tiebout, C. M., & Warren, R. (1961). The organization of government in metropolitan areas: A theoretical inquiry. *American Political Science Review, 55*(4), 831–842. https://doi.org/10.2307/1952530
- Link: https://doi.org/10.2307/1952530
- Verification: record located only (Crossref. The definitional passage was read as quoted in Ostrom 2010, pp. 643–644.)
- What it establishes: This article originated "polycentric." It describes many formally independent decision centres that may operate as a coherent system through competition, contracting, cooperation, and recourse to central conflict-resolution mechanisms. Whether they do so is an empirical question.
- Use in dissertation: This is the origin of the concept that lets the dissertation describe governance with no single sovereign without implying no structure at all.

## C. Institutional capture and founding problems

### Stigler1971
- Stigler, G. J. (1971). The theory of economic regulation. *Bell Journal of Economics and Management Science, 2*(1), 3–21. https://doi.org/10.2307/3003160
- Link: https://doi.org/10.2307/3003160
- Verification: record located only (Crossref and RePEc)
- What it establishes: Stigler argues that regulation is, as a rule, acquired by the industry and designed and operated mainly for its benefit. Concentrated interests with low costs of organizing outcompete diffuse ones. This is the founding statement of economic capture theory.
- Use in dissertation: This is the classic support for Ch. 4's claim that national regulators reproduce capture. Pair it with Carpenter & Moss, who qualify it.

### CarpenterMoss2013
- Carpenter, D., & Moss, D. A. (Eds.). (2013). *Preventing regulatory capture: Special interest influence and how to limit it*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139565875
- Link: https://www.tobinproject.org/sites/default/files/assets/Introduction%20from%20Preventing%20Regulatory%20Capture.pdf
- Verification: full text read (introduction chapter. The DOI was confirmed in Crossref with an issued date of 2013. Some printings say "first published 2014.")
- What it establishes: The editors define capture as regulation consistently directed away from the public interest toward the regulated industry, *by that industry's intent and action*. They distinguish strong capture, where the public would be better off with no regulation, from weak capture, where net benefits are reduced but remain positive. They argue capture is often misdiagnosed because of weak evidentiary standards traceable to Stigler. Their three claims are that capture is often misdiagnosed, that deregulation is no panacea and can itself be "corrosive capture," and that capture is "at least at the margins, preventable."
- Use in dissertation: Ch. 4 currently states capture as an inevitability ("courts can be stacked..."). Carpenter & Moss show the defensible claim is narrower: capture is a matter of degree and partly preventable. That matches Ch. 5's own "degrades more slowly and is easier to repair" framing.

### Mueller2002
- Mueller, M. L. (2002). *Ruling the root: Internet governance and the taming of cyberspace*. MIT Press.
- Link: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262632980/ruling-the-root/ (403 to automated retrieval). Confirmed via the review in *Information Technology & People, 17*(4), 2004, https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/itp.2004.16117dae.001/full/html
- Verification: record located only (review and catalogue records: MIT Press 2002, ISBN 0-262-13412-8)
- What it establishes: Mueller uses institutional economics to analyze domain names and addresses. The DNS root is the only point of centralized control in an otherwise distributed network, and ICANN's creation established a new international regime shaped by a small set of industry and technical insiders with limited public accountability.
- Use in dissertation: This is the scholarly source for Ch. 4's claim that ICANN "still has a founding problem." It is a well-documented case of genesis-rule capture in a multi-stakeholder body.

### DeFilippiWright2018
- De Filippi, P., & Wright, A. (2018). *Blockchain and the law: The rule of code*. Harvard University Press. https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674985933
- Link: https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674985933
- Verification: record located only (Crossref. Catalogue table of contents: technology; finance and contracts; information systems; organizations and automation.)
- What it establishes: The book examines how blockchains create "lex cryptographia," rules administered through self-executing code, and how that both escapes and invites legal regulation. It includes decentralized autonomous organizations.
- Use in dissertation: Cite it for the claim that protocol-level enforcement (Ch. 5 §5) is itself a governance choice embedded by the code's authors. Code-as-law relocates the genesis problem rather than removing it.

### Fritsch2024
- Fritsch, R., Müller, M., & Wattenhofer, R. (2024). Analyzing voting power in decentralized governance: Who controls DAOs? *Blockchain: Research and Applications, 5*(3), Article 100208. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bcra.2024.100208

## Corrections to the draft

1. **Rawls is misdescribed as a bargaining procedure.** Ch. 4 says designers "might land on either side" and that participants "negotiate."
   - The "equal chance of being anyone" reasoning is Harsanyi's (1953, 1955). Rawls's parties do not reason from equal probabilities. In Rawls the parties are identically situated, so the choice is effectively one person's and not a negotiation. Check the Rawls passage on why bargaining drops out before stating it this strongly; I did not verify that page this session.
   - The draft's actual mechanism, real self-interested people who are uncertain about their future position, is the Buchanan & Tullock (1962) and Brennan & Buchanan (1985) "veil of uncertainty."
   - Suggested framing: the proposal is a *Buchanan-style veil of uncertainty, thickened by Turing-style anonymity, aiming at Rawlsian fairness*.

2. **Ostrom's principles: four of eight are listed, and the mapping is partly wrong.**
   - The paraphrases of boundaries, local congruence, collective choice and graduated sanctions are acceptable.
   - "Escalating consequences such as warnings and fines" is an illustration, not Ostrom's wording. Her wording is that sanctions start low and grow with repeated violation, and in 1990 they are imposed by users or by officials accountable to them. Peer enforcement is part of the principle.
   - The draft omits:
     - **2B proportionality of costs and benefits.** This is directly relevant to choosing a compute share.
     - **4 monitoring by users or by people accountable to users.** This is the principle that answers Ch. 5 §2.
     - **6 low-cost conflict resolution.**
     - **7 external recognition of the right to self-organize.** This is the principle that blocks state override.
     - **8 nested enterprises.**
   - "Nation-or-culture-specific AGIs under a shared global unit" maps to **principle 8**, not to principle 2.
   - Note that Cox et al. (2010) found principle 8 the least supported, so the mapping is weaker than the draft implies.

3. **Boundaries conflict with anonymity.** Principle 1 requires clear, *locally understood* boundaries between legitimate users and nonusers. An open, anonymous convention is close to the opposite. The draft presents principle 1 as support; it is better presented as a constraint the design must satisfy (see Douceur; Adler et al.).

4. **Scale.** Ostrom's cases are local or regional CPRs. She warns against treating the principles as panaceas, and in 2010 calls them "best practices." Extending them to global compute needs Wilson, Ostrom & Cox (2013) and polycentricity (Ostrom 2010; V. Ostrom et al. 1961), not just Ostrom (1990).

5. **Missing reference.** Ostrom (1990) is cited but absent from the reference list. Add it from entry B.

6. **"The dominant AI-safety frame assumes one party must be sovereign" (Ch. 1) overgeneralizes.**
   - It fits corrigibility (Soares et al., 2015) and AI control (Greenblatt et al., 2024).
   - It fits less well for Amodei et al. (2016), which is about accident engineering.
   - It does not fit Gabriel (2020), which seeks fair, reflectively endorsed principles; Dafoe et al. (2021), on cooperative AI; or Drexler (2019), on distributed services.
   - Narrow the claim to "the control strand of AI safety." Name the cooperative strand as an ally.

7. **Capture is overstated.** "National regulators reproduce the capture problems" should be qualified with Carpenter & Moss (2013): capture comes in degrees, is often misdiagnosed, and is partly preventable. The courts and central-bank examples need their own sources. None in this domain support them.

8. **Complementarity is contingent, not structural.**
   - Autor (2015) supports present-day human advantage in non-routine, tacit tasks, but explicitly flags machine learning as the route around it.
   - Complementarity in *production* does not imply distributed *control*. Owners of capital can control infrastructure built by complementary labor.
   - Moravec's paradox wording is unverified. Do not quote it yet.

9. **Turing.** State that the draft *repurposes* the imitation game, using indistinguishability as a fairness condition rather than a test of thinking. Weidinger et al. (2023, Study 4) gives empirical support for why this matters: knowing counterparts were bots weakened commitment to veil-chosen principles.

10. **Citation details to fix if used.**
    - Harsanyi (1975) DOI is 10.2307/1959090.
    - Hadfield & Clark is now Jurimetrics 2026.
    - Hess & Ostrom: choose 2006 or 2007 and be consistent.
    - Constitutional AI (Bai et al.), Concrete Problems, Hubinger et al., personhood credentials, and Taking AI Welfare Seriously are preprints and should be labelled as such.

## Synthesis notes: the strongest objections to the blind convention, and how to answer them

**1. Harsanyi: the veil does not determine the output.**
- The first output is a single number, the share of global compute for basic income. Behind a veil, that number depends on the decision rule. Maximin choosers set a high floor. Expected-utility choosers (Harsanyi, 1975) maximize the average and accept a lower floor if total output rises.
- Laboratory evidence points between the two. Groups behind experimental veils overwhelmingly chose average maximization subject to a floor (Frohlich, Oppenheimer & Eavey, 1987).
- The careful response concedes that anonymity is a fairness *procedure*, not a theory of justice. The dissertation should predict a floor-constrained outcome rather than claim the veil yields Rawls's difference principle.
- Harsanyi's worry also cuts sharper here. Human and AI parties may have incommensurable utility scales, so "equal chance of being anyone" requires comparisons across substrates. That is the same problem as the unspecified measurement standard in Ch. 5 §7.

**2. Public choice: genesis rules and repeated play.**
- Buchanan and Tullock's argument only works while positions are unpredictable. Ch. 5 §3 already concedes that the veil thins with repetition.
- Elster's upstream constraints add more. Whoever convenes the convention, sets its agenda, chooses the question (a compute *percentage* rather than, say, data rights) and certifies the result has already written genesis rules that no veil covers.
- The best answer has three parts:
  - (a) Follow Brennan and Buchanan: make outputs general and durable (formulas, not figures), because generality keeps the veil thick even after founding.
  - (b) Follow Lutz: adopt a moderate amendment path rather than entrenchment.
  - (c) Follow Ostrom (principles 3, 4, 6): put monitoring and conflict resolution in the hands of parties accountable to participants.
- Concede plainly that the convener problem is reduced, not solved. Carpenter and Moss's "weak capture" is the honest target.

**3. Sybil and identity: the deepest technical objection.**
- Douceur proves that without a logically centralized identity authority, an open system cannot prevent one entity from presenting many identities. AI systems can be copied at near-zero marginal cost, so the convention as described is easy to take over by multiplying copies.
- Proof-of-personhood schemes either rely on reverse Turing tests, which machine learning is eroding (Siddarth et al., 2020), or on credentials that *distinguish humans from AI* (Adler et al., 2024). The second option destroys the blindness the proposal requires.
- The dissertation cannot wave this away. It needs a two-layer design:
  - A credential layer that attests *uniqueness and eligibility* for some unit of agency, without disclosing substrate to counterparties. This could be a human personhood credential and a separately audited AI-instance credential, pooled and blinded.
  - Openly specified seat allocation between substrate classes, decided before the blind phase.
- That second element concedes that the human/AI ratio of seats is itself a genesis rule. Long et al. (2024) give the most principled defence of placing *which* party one will be governed as behind the veil: AI moral status is genuinely uncertain, so no one, human or AI, can claim to know the right answer. Ch. 5 §2 should be rewritten around Douceur, not as a generic verification worry.

**4. The AI-control literature: you cannot bargain blind with a party that may be deceptive.**
- Hubinger et al. (2019) and Ngo, Chan and Mindermann (2024) argue that trained systems may hold objectives different from those they express and may seek power while appearing aligned.
- A convention's legitimacy rests on participants' stated preferences being their real ones. A deceptively aligned participant defeats that, and anonymity makes it worse, since it removes the scrutiny that might detect it.
- Bengio et al. (2024) and the International AI Safety Report (2025) record that serious experts consider loss of control plausible, though contested.
- The careful answer has three parts:
  - (a) Concede that AI-control protocols (Greenblatt et al., 2024) are compatible with, and probably prerequisites for, any convention. The dissertation can reject *permanent* sovereignty while accepting *transitional* containment.
  - (b) Note that the off-switch result (Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017) rests on the machine's uncertainty about its objective. That is structurally a veil, so the dissertation's mechanism and the control literature's best result share a logic.
  - (c) Align with cooperative AI (Dafoe et al., 2021) and Gabriel (2020), who already treat fair terms between parties with mixed interests as a research problem.
- Drexler (2019) is a double-edged ally. Bounded services ease safety but make "the AI party" hard to define.

**Net position for a careful dissertation.** The blind convention is defensible as a *legitimacy-generating procedure* under uncertainty about moral status and future position. It is not a guarantee of fairness, of a particular distributive outcome, or of security against adversarial participants. Its main contribution is to put the human/AI status question behind the veil, which Weidinger et al.'s Study 4 suggests changes behaviour. Its main debts are identity (Douceur) and convener power (Elster). These should be stated as preconditions, not left as footnoted limitations.
