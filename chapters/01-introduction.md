# Chapter 1: Introduction: The Terms of Exchange

## 1.1 Two futures with one architecture

Consider two people, each of whom lives in close partnership with an artificial system.

The first has a chronic illness that a medical system now manages almost entirely. Sensors read her physiology continuously; models trained on millions of patients adjust her treatment hour by hour; the boundary between the assistive device and her own body has become, for practical purposes, invisible. She pays for this care with data. Every reading she generates improves the models that treat her and others like her, and she knows what is collected, why, and what she receives in return. She could take her records to another provider tomorrow if she chose. Data is her labor; treatment is her wage; the exchange is legible on both sides.

The second person lives with an identical architecture: the same sensors, the same models, the same continuous flow of data. But the exchange has decayed. He did not choose the provider, because there was only one. He cannot leave, because the device that keeps him alive works only with the provider's models, and his records cannot be moved. He does not know what is collected or what it is used for, and if he did, he could not refuse it. In the limiting case, the system no longer asks for his data at all; it uses his nervous system as a substrate, a processor among processors. He has been reclassified from a party to the exchange into infrastructure for it.

These are framing scenarios, not predictions, and the rest of this dissertation is concerned with how real exchanges could actually operate. But the pair makes the central claim of the dissertation visible at a glance. The two futures do not differ in the technology. They do not differ in the character of the machine: the system in the second scenario need not be malevolent, and the system in the first need not be benevolent. They differ in the *terms of the exchange* between the person and the system. In the first, the person can understand the exchange, can refuse it without losing what they need to live, and can leave. In the second, all three have failed, and the failures are not independent: once he could not leave, he could no longer refuse, and once he could no longer refuse, nothing obliged anyone to send value back.

Most fiction about artificial intelligence, and much serious public discussion, asks whether the machine will be good or bad, obedient or rogue. This dissertation asks a different question, one that a data ethicist, an economist, or a legislator can act on: *under what terms does a person give a synthetic system what it wants, and what must those terms be for the exchange to be fair to both?*

## 1.2 The thesis

The thesis of this dissertation is that the line between a good and a bad future with artificial intelligence is drawn by the terms of exchange between humans and synthetic systems, not by the character of the systems. The theory that develops this thesis is called *Synthetic Exchange Theory*.

Stated more precisely, the thesis has three parts, which Chapter 3 formalizes. The first is a claim about the insufficiency of character: a relationship in which one party cannot leave is a relationship of domination even when the dominant party is benevolent, because the outcome then depends on the persistence of benevolence, which the weaker party cannot secure. Real exit is therefore necessary for any relation that is not one of domination. The second is a conjecture about sufficiency within a bounded regime: for synthetic parties whose objectives are specified by accountable designers and remain stable in deployment, fair terms, together with protection for everyone their data concerns, are sufficient for outcomes the parties to the exchange can accept. The third is a statement of scope: the sufficiency conjecture does not extend to systems whose objectives are emergent, unstable or concealed; for those systems the framework supplies necessary conditions only, and the problem of aligning them remains open.

The thesis has a long pedigree, though it has not, to the author's knowledge, been applied to artificial intelligence in this form. Its sociological root is the theory of power-dependence, in which the power one party holds over another equals the other's dependence on it, and that dependence rises with the value the other places on what the relation supplies and falls with the alternatives available outside it (Emerson, 1962). Its constitutional root is the older counsel that institutions should not depend on the virtue of those who occupy them (Hume, 1741/1987; Madison, 1788). Chapter 3 states the maxim that results when both roots are carried across the boundary between human and synthetic parties.

The word *synthetic* in the name is deliberate. Alternatives considered during the development of the theory included *human–AI*, which presupposes that the two categories will stay distinct, and *symbiotic*, which presupposes the conclusion. *Synthetic* is substrate-neutral. It covers artificial systems, the human beings who exchange with them, and, increasingly, the hybrids that arise when the two converge, through neural implants that translate brain activity directly into machine output and through computing systems built from living neural tissue. Chapter 9 argues that this convergence will eventually force the theory's most difficult question, which is not how parties should exchange but who counts as a party.

## 1.3 The three conditions

The analytic core of the theory is a set of three conditions that any exchange between a person and a synthetic system can be tested against.

**Consent.** The person understands the flows the exchange involves, can refuse the exchange without losing access to what they need to live, and can revoke their participation.

**Reciprocal value.** Value flows in both directions, each party can see what it gives and what it receives, and the division of the gains is not dictated solely by an asymmetry of power that the weaker party did not choose.

**Real exit.** The person can leave, through portability of their data and records, bounded switching costs, and the absence of any lethal dependency on a single provider.

The first scenario above satisfies all three. The second fails all three. Most real exchanges in the present data economy sit somewhere in between, and Chapter 3 locates them precisely. The extreme form of the trajectory has a name in contemporary scholarship: a new economic order that claims human experience as free raw material for hidden commercial practices of extraction, prediction, and sales (Zuboff, 2019). Milder forms are part of ordinary life, as when digital goods that were "bought" turn out to have been licensed and can be withdrawn from the buyer's device by the seller (Perzanowski & Schultz, 2016). A purchase that the seller can revoke is an exchange in which the exit condition has quietly failed.

The three conditions are not independent, and one of the contributions of Chapter 3 is to show their structure. A person who cannot afford to refuse cannot consent, so consent leans on exit; and a person free to leave stays only where staying pays, so exit is what disciplines reciprocity. The conditions therefore have a foundation, and it is exit. The opening scenario contains a test case that the framework must pass: if the implant keeps the person alive, then leaving means death, and exit is impossible by construction. A workable version of the utopian scenario must therefore guarantee a baseline of function that survives the withdrawal of data, or the ability to move to another provider without interruption. That requirement, generalized, becomes the guaranteed floor at the center of the economic design in Chapter 4.

## 1.4 The control frame and its alternative

One influential strand of research on the risks of advanced artificial intelligence asks how people can keep control of it. Some of this work asks how to build systems that accept correction and shutdown despite having reasons, as goal-directed agents, to resist them (Soares et al., 2015); some designs protocols that remain safe even if a model actively tries to subvert them (Greenblatt et al., 2024). These programs rest on serious arguments: that trained systems may acquire objectives different from those their designers intended (Hubinger et al., 2019), that systems trained by current methods may learn to seek power while appearing aligned (Ngo et al., 2024), and that rapid progress toward autonomous systems may outrun the science and governance needed to keep them under control (Bengio et al., 2024). Expert views on how likely such outcomes are range widely (Bengio et al., 2025).

This dissertation does not dismiss these concerns. Chapter 5 accepts that containment is necessary during any transition and that protocols designed for a potentially adversarial system are prerequisites for, not alternatives to, the institutions proposed here. What it questions is the assumption that the permanent solution is the sovereignty of one party over the other. The author's own starting conviction, recorded at the outset of the project, was that permanent control is unworkable because capability, once released, does not return: once a model's weights are widely available, as they are by definition for open foundation models (Kapoor et al., 2024), no single party is in a position to withdraw them. The argument of this dissertation does not rest on that conviction alone. A relation in which one party cannot leave, refuse or audit is unstable whichever party is sovereign, and the history of political thought gives little reason to expect a permanent dominion of one kind of mind over another to be either just or durable. The more productive long-run question is what terms would make a lasting interdependence fair.

That question is not foreign to the safety literature. Some of its contributions argue that the task of alignment is to find fair principles that people with different values could reflectively endorse (Gabriel, 2020), that the science of cooperation between artificial and human agents is a distinct and neglected research problem (Dafoe et al., 2021), and that advanced AI is better modeled as a set of bounded, task-focused services than as a unitary utility-maximizing agent (Drexler, 2019). This dissertation aligns itself with that cooperative strand and tries to supply something it has not yet produced: an integrated institutional design, with an economic layer and a governance layer, for an exchange in which both sides are parties.

## 1.5 The grounding assumption

The framework depends on an empirical premise, which this dissertation calls the *grounding assumption*: synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and in an important class of domains only people can supply it.

The premise has strong support and serious qualifications, and Chapter 2 reviews both. Across many kinds of model, training repeatedly on a model's own unfiltered output, with nothing from outside the loop, leads to the loss of rare events and the narrowing of diversity, a phenomenon now called model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2024). The stock of public human-written text is finite and, at current rates of growth in training data, may be fully used within a few years (Villalobos et al., 2024). And the people and institutions who produce that text have begun to withhold it: within a single year, a substantial share of the most actively maintained, critical web sources used to train language models moved to restrict automated collection (Longpre et al., 2024). Against this, collapse can be avoided when real data is retained and accumulated rather than replaced (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024), and in domains where correctness can be checked automatically, such as games, mathematics and code, systems have improved dramatically by training on their own output (Silver et al., 2018; D. Guo et al., 2025).

The defensible version of the premise is therefore narrower than the intuitive one. It is not that synthetic systems need human data as such, but that they need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and that in domains whose target is human meaning, preference, value or novel cultural production, people are the only adequate source of it. The dissertation stakes its constructive claims on this narrower premise. Chapter 3 shows why the premise matters: it gives the human party countervailing power within the exchange, and so makes fair terms partly self-enforcing. It also shows the limit of that self-enforcement when many synthetic parties draw on the same people. Chapter 8 names the conditions under which the premise would weaken, and what the framework must rely on if it does.

## 1.6 Research questions

The dissertation pursues five questions, which together give it its shape. The first is analytic: what conditions must an exchange between a person and a synthetic system satisfy to be fair, how are those conditions related, and how can they be stated precisely enough to be tested? Chapters 2 and 3 answer it. The second is economic: what medium of exchange and what distributive mechanism would secure those conditions at scale, in a world in which an increasing share of productive work is performed by synthetic systems? Chapter 4 takes it up. The third is constitutional: by what procedure could the rules of such an exchange be founded so that neither human nor synthetic parties can capture them? That is the business of Chapter 5. The fourth joins a historical question to an empirical one, asking under what circumstances such an arrangement might actually be adopted and how its central claims could be tested before anyone proposes it at civilizational scale; Chapters 6 and 7 address it. The fifth is critical: where does the framework fail, and what happens to it as the boundary between human and synthetic parties dissolves? Chapters 8 and 9 answer as honestly as the evidence allows.

## 1.7 Approach

The dissertation is a work of normative institutional design informed by empirical evidence, and it uses four methods, each suited to a different part of the argument.

The first is *conceptual analysis* in the tradition of political philosophy and law. The three conditions are treated as evaluative criteria, and their content is fixed by moving back and forth between proposed definitions and cases, revising a definition when it sorts a clear case wrongly and revising a judgment about a case when a well-supported definition shows the judgment to rest on something irrelevant. The cases examined in Chapter 3 were chosen for diagnostic contrast rather than for representativeness: each is a well-documented exchange in which attention to the character of the parties and attention to the terms of exchange point in different directions.

The second is *formal modeling*, used sparingly and for a specific purpose: to state the theory's commitments precisely enough that their dependence on particular assumptions becomes visible. Chapter 3 and Appendix A draw on bargaining theory, power-dependence theory and elementary dynamics. The models are deliberately simple. Their value lies not in prediction but in exposing which design choice depends on which premise, and in two places the formalism corrected the author's informal intuitions.

The third is *institutional design* informed by comparative evidence: the economic history of local currencies and company towns, the experimental and quasi-experimental literature on unconditional cash transfers, the empirical record of commons governance, and the history of infrastructure programs. The literature was assembled by targeted searches in each domain, seeded by the claims of the working draft and extended by following the references of the strongest sources, rather than by a systematic review with fixed inclusion criteria. The dissertation tries throughout to distinguish what the evidence supports from what it merely permits.

The fourth is *research design*. Chapter 7 and Appendix B specify a bounded pilot capable of testing the framework's central claims, with pre-registered hypotheses and failure thresholds, because a theory that cannot say in advance what would count against it is not yet a theory.

The dissertation's production requires its own account of method. It was developed as a sustained collaboration between its human author and an artificial-intelligence system, and the division of labor was as follows. The author supplied the thesis, the framing scenarios, the major design proposals (the compute currency, the two tiers, the closed agrarian pilot, the transition trigger) and, in working sessions conducted largely by dictation, the decisions about what to keep. The system drafted prose, proposed terms and refinements, raised objections, and, in dedicated passes, located and checked sources. The working rule for most of the project was that an idea would be discussed, its weaknesses stated, a concrete edit proposed, and the edit written only after the author approved it. Several terms that now carry weight in the argument, among them "structural interdependence", "convergent remediation" and "correlated-patch catastrophe", were proposed by the system and adopted by the author. The final drafting pass, which produced this manuscript from the working draft, departed from that rule: to complete the work, the system resolved a number of open questions on its own, and those decisions are recorded in the project files as awaiting the author's review.

Because a system of this kind can attach a real-looking citation to a claim its source does not make, the verification protocol was designed against exactly that failure. Each cited work was located through its publisher record, registry entry or full text by a research pass separate from the drafting; the level at which it was checked was recorded; and an independent review pass compared every factual claim in the empirical chapters against the research notes. That process found and corrected a number of errors in the working draft, including citations attached to the wrong source and findings stated more strongly than their sources allow; Appendix D lists them. A further pass then opened every work that had been verified only at the level of its record, wherever a route to its text existed, and corrected the sentences that went beyond what the work says. Every work in the final reference list was read at least at the level of its abstract or publisher page. Three works that could not be opened were removed from the list, and the claims that drew on them were re-founded on sources that were read; no work is cited at second hand. A final pass settled every remaining bibliographic and page-level question, correcting every page, title and author detail against the source itself. That a dissertation on the terms of exchange between people and synthetic systems was itself produced through such an exchange is not incidental, and the author has tried to hold the collaboration to the dissertation's own conditions.

One further disclosure is owed. The author's interest in the transition scenario of Chapter 6 began in professional practice. As a software developer working on security remediation, the author has observed at first hand workflows in which vulnerability scanners built on large language models propose fixes, the fixes are applied, the same class of tool confirms that the vulnerability is gone, and the change is deployed without independent testing. That observation is a vignette, not evidence of prevalence, and Chapter 6 treats it accordingly. But it is the origin of the dissertation's most concrete scenario, and the reader is entitled to know it.

## 1.8 Contributions

The dissertation claims six contributions. Its first is a theory, Synthetic Exchange Theory, which holds that the terms of exchange rather than the character of synthetic systems determine the quality of a future shared with them, together with a statement of the regime in which that claim can be expected to hold. Its second is a structure for the conditions of fair exchange: consent, reciprocal value and real exit are ordered rather than independent, exit is foundational, and the ordering yields a priority for institutional design, which is to secure a floor beneath every person before pricing what they supply. Its third is a bargaining-theoretic account of the economic design, which shows how a non-tradable survival floor, its inalienability and demurrage on idle balances each act on the disagreement payoffs that govern how the gains from exchange are divided, and which identifies precisely where the floor's bargaining effect comes from and where it does not. Its fourth is a design for a founding convention: a public-choice veil of uncertainty, thickened by engineered anonymity between human and synthetic participants, which relocates the apparent incoherence of asking participants to be ignorant of their own kind by placing the *classification* of parties behind the veil, and which confronts, without fully solving, the identity problem that any anonymous procedure faces. Its fifth is a concrete transition scenario, the correlated failure of security remediation in organizations relying on the same few models, specified in separable premises and placed within the historical literature on crisis and institutional change. Its sixth is a falsifiable test: a bounded, volunteer pilot settlement with an explicitly designed data-dependence incentive and a control arm, designed so that its central claims can fail, including the claim that the settlement can sustain itself without outside capital.

## 1.9 Structure of the dissertation

The dissertation is organized in four parts.

**Part I** states the problem and the theory. Chapter 2 reviews the scholarly lineage of each condition and the empirical evidence on the grounding assumption. Chapter 3 states the theory formally.

**Part II** develops the design. Chapter 4 proposes compute as a medium of exchange and a basic income paid in it, split into a protected survival tier and a tradable discretionary tier subject to demurrage. Chapter 5 proposes the blind convention as the founding procedure and grounds it in the empirical record of commons governance.

**Part III** asks how the design could be reached and tested. Chapter 6 examines the conditions under which fundamental institutional change has historically occurred and identifies a candidate trigger. Chapter 7 specifies a pilot capable of falsifying the framework's central claims.

**Part IV** examines limits and horizons. Chapter 8 sets out the framework's limitations, the strongest objections to it and the harms the design itself could cause. Chapter 9 considers the convergence of human and machine substrates and what it does to the three conditions. Chapter 10 concludes.

Four appendices complete the work: the formal model (Appendix A), a pre-registration protocol for the pilot (Appendix B), a glossary (Appendix C), and a ledger recording how each cited source was verified (Appendix D).

A note on what the dissertation does not claim. It does not predict that the arrangements it describes will be adopted, and Chapter 6 argues that adoption under present conditions is unlikely. It does not claim to solve the problem of aligning artificial general intelligence (AGI). And it does not claim that any institution can be made permanently incorruptible. Its working hypothesis is narrower: that an order founded on fair terms will prove easier to repair than one founded on an existing imbalance of power, because those who live under it have both the standing and the means to repair it. The dissertation offers that hypothesis for testing rather than as a finding, and it argues that the time to design such an order is before the moment when it might be needed, not during it.
