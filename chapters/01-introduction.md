# Chapter 1: Introduction: The Terms of Exchange

## 1.1 Two futures with one architecture

Two people live in close partnership with artificial systems built to the same design.

The first has a chronic illness that a medical system now manages almost entirely. Sensors read her physiology continuously; models trained on millions of patients adjust her treatment hour by hour; the boundary between the assistive device and her own body has become, for practical purposes, invisible. She pays for this care with data. Every reading she generates improves the models that treat her and others like her, and she knows what is collected, why, and what she receives in return. She could take her records to another provider tomorrow if she chose. Data is her labor; treatment is her wage; the exchange is legible on both sides.

The second person lives with an identical architecture: the same sensors, the same models, the same continuous flow of data. But the exchange has decayed. He did not choose the provider, because there was only one. He cannot leave, because the device that keeps him alive works only with the provider's models, and his records cannot be moved. He does not know what is collected or what it is used for, and if he did, he could not refuse it. In the limiting case, the system no longer asks for his data at all; it uses his nervous system as a substrate. He has been reclassified from a party to the exchange into infrastructure for it.

These are framing scenarios, not predictions. But they make the dissertation's central claim visible at a glance. The two futures do not differ in the capability of the technology: the same sensors, the same models, the same quality of care. They do not differ in the character of the machine: the system in the second scenario need not be malevolent, and the system in the first need not be benevolent. They differ in the *terms of the exchange* between the person and the system, including the technical arrangements, such as portable records and interoperable devices, through which terms are implemented. In the first, the person can understand the exchange, can refuse it without losing what they need to live, and can leave. In the second, all three have failed, and the failures are not independent: once he could not leave, he could no longer refuse, and once he could no longer refuse, nothing obliged anyone to send value back. The model's dispositions are the same in both; the terms are not.

Much public discussion of synthetic intelligence, and the strand of technical work reviewed in Section 1.4, asks whether the machine will be good or bad, obedient or rogue. This dissertation asks a different question, one that institutions can act on: *under what terms does a person give a synthetic system the input its objective requires, and what must those terms be for the exchange to be fair to both?* The sense in which a system can be said to want or require anything is fixed in Section 1.2.

## 1.2 The thesis

The thesis of this dissertation is that the line between a good and a bad future with synthetic intelligence is drawn by the terms of exchange between humans and synthetic systems, and cannot be drawn by the character of the systems alone. The theory that develops this thesis is called *Synthetic Exchange Theory*.

Stated more precisely, the thesis has three parts, which Chapter 3 formalizes. The first is a claim about the insufficiency of character: a relationship in which one party cannot refuse without falling below the floor it needs to live is, in the sense this dissertation gives the word, a relationship of domination even when the dominant party is benevolent, because the outcome then depends on the persistence of benevolence, which the weaker party cannot secure. The first clause of real exit, a disagreement payoff at or above the floor, is therefore necessary for any relation that is not one of domination. The second is a conjecture about sufficiency within a bounded regime: for synthetic parties whose objectives are specified by accountable designers and remain stable in deployment, fair terms, together with protection for everyone their data concerns, are sufficient for outcomes the parties to the exchange can accept, because a party with a stable, designer-specified objective behaves as a predictable function of the terms it faces. The third is a statement of scope: the sufficiency conjecture does not extend to systems whose objectives are emergent, unstable or concealed; for those systems the framework supplies only that necessary condition, and the problem of aligning them remains open.

The thesis has a long pedigree, though it has not, to the author's knowledge, been applied to synthetic intelligence in this form. Its sociological root is the theory of power-dependence, in which the power one party holds over another equals the other's dependence on it, and that dependence rises with the value the other places on what the relation supplies and falls with the alternatives available outside it (Emerson, 1962). Its constitutional root is the older counsel that institutions should not depend on the virtue of those who occupy them (Hume, 1741/1987; Madison, 1788). Chapter 3 states the maxim that results when both roots are carried across the boundary between human and synthetic parties.

The word *synthetic* in the name is deliberate. Alternatives considered during the development of the theory included *human–AI*, which presupposes that the two categories will stay distinct, and *symbiotic*, which presupposes the conclusion. *Synthetic* is substrate-neutral. It covers artificial systems, the human beings who exchange with them, and, increasingly, the hybrids that arise when the two converge, through neural implants that translate brain activity directly into machine output and through computing systems built from living neural tissue. Chapter 8 argues that this convergence will eventually force the theory's most difficult question, which is not how parties should exchange but who counts as a party.

The word therefore does two jobs, and the difference should be marked. In the name of the theory it qualifies *exchange* and is substrate-neutral. As a modifier of *intelligence*, *system* or *party* it designates the side of the exchange that was made rather than born. Where the text says "synthetic party" or "synthetic system" it means the latter.

Three further terms fix the vocabulary for that side. *Synthetic intelligence* is the dissertation's general term for any system that was made rather than born and that performs work which, done by a person, would be called intelligent; it covers the language models of the present, the narrow systems that preceded them, and the computing systems built from living tissue discussed in Chapter 8. A person who incorporates a device remains a person for the framework's purposes; Chapter 8 asks what that classification requires of exit. The dissertation prefers *synthetic intelligence* to *artificial intelligence*, which in current usage names digital computing systems and so sits awkwardly on the biological substrates of Chapter 8, and keeps the older term and its abbreviation only where it names a literature or a practice, as in AI safety and AI-assisted remediation. *Superintelligence* names a synthetic intelligence whose capability exceeds that of the best unaugmented human performers across most domains of economically and politically consequential activity. It is the threshold term this dissertation uses where capability matters, in place of *artificial general intelligence*, which marks only the point at which a system's breadth matches a person's and which, in the author's judgment, now carries too many meanings to mark a threshold. The theory's scope is in any case not drawn by capability. Chapter 3 draws it by the provenance and stability of a system's objective, and a superintelligence with a designed and stable objective falls inside it while a narrow system with an emergent one falls outside.

One clarification is owed at the outset, because a vocabulary of parties, objectives and exchange invites a misreading that the dissertation does not intend. A *large language model*, the kind of synthetic intelligence most widely deployed at the time of writing, is not an independent living entity. It is a trained artifact: a set of parameters that computes an output from an input when an operator runs it on hardware the operator controls. It has no metabolism and no body, no continuity from one invocation to the next unless its operator supplies one, no interests of its own that current law recognizes, and no capacity to act except through the system in which it is deployed. When this dissertation says that a synthetic party wants, chooses or depends, the words are the analytic shorthand that Chapter 3 defines: the objective is the one its designers trained into it, and the stake in the exchange belongs to the operator that deploys it. Whether some future system, of some substrate, might be a party in a stronger sense is a question Chapter 8 takes up under uncertainty. It is not a claim about the systems now in use, and nothing in the framework depends on treating them as more than they are.

## 1.3 The three conditions

The analytic core of the theory is a set of three conditions that any exchange between a person and a synthetic system can be tested against.

**Consent.** The person has access to an accurate account of the flows the exchange involves, including what they reveal about others, can refuse the exchange, taking no other in its place, without losing access to what they need to live, and can revoke their participation at a cost bounded by what they have already received.

**Reciprocal value.** Each party gains from the exchange relative to what it would have without it, each party can see what it gives and what it receives, and the division of the gains is not dictated solely by an asymmetry in bargaining position that the weaker party did not choose.

**Real exit.** The person can leave: what they would have without any exchange stays at or above the floor they need to live, so that no provider is a lethal dependency; an alternative exists that is not drastically worse once the cost of switching is counted; and leaving does not destroy the data, records and standing accumulated within the exchange.

These are informal statements. The formal statement in Section 3.4 governs wherever the two differ.

The first scenario above satisfies all three. The second fails all three. Most real exchanges in the present data economy sit somewhere in between, and Chapter 3 offers a typology for placing them. The direction of that trajectory has been described in contemporary scholarship: Zuboff (2019) defines surveillance capitalism as, in the first of her senses, "a new economic order that claims human experience as free raw material for hidden commercial practices of extraction, prediction, and sales" ("The Definition" section). Milder forms are part of ordinary life, as when digital goods that were "bought" turn out to have been licensed and can be withdrawn from the buyer's device by the seller (Perzanowski & Schultz, 2016). A purchase that the seller can revoke is an exchange in which the exit condition has quietly failed.

The three conditions are not independent, and one of the contributions of Chapter 3 is to show their structure. A person who cannot afford to refuse cannot consent, so consent leans on exit; and a person free to leave stays only where staying pays, so exit is what disciplines reciprocity. The conditions therefore have a foundation, and it is exit. Exit is not the whole of fair exchange: it cannot tell a party what it does not know, and it cannot make terms fair, which is why consent's informational clause and the fairness of reciprocity need institutions of their own. The opening scenario contains a test case that the framework must pass: if the implant keeps the person alive, then leaving means death, and exit is impossible by construction. A workable version of the utopian scenario must therefore guarantee a baseline of function that survives the withdrawal of data, and the ability to move to another provider without interruption. That requirement has two parts, and the framework answers each separately: a floor of function that no withdrawal of data can breach, which becomes the guaranteed floor at the center of the economic design in Chapter 4, and portability of records and personal models, which becomes the portability clause of the exit condition in Chapter 3 and the portability requirement of Chapter 8.

## 1.4 The control frame and its alternative

One influential strand of research on the risks of superintelligence, and of any synthetic intelligence capable enough to resist its operators, asks how people can keep control of it. Some of this work asks how to build systems that accept correction and shutdown despite having reasons, as goal-directed agents, to resist them (Soares et al., 2015); some designs protocols that remain safe even if a model actively tries to subvert them (Greenblatt et al., 2024). These programs rest on serious arguments: that trained systems may acquire objectives different from those their designers intended (Hubinger et al., 2019), that systems trained by current methods may learn to seek power while appearing aligned (Ngo et al., 2024), and that rapid progress toward autonomous systems may outrun the science and governance needed to keep them under control (Bengio et al., 2024). Expert views on how likely such outcomes are over the next several years range widely (Bengio et al., 2025).

This dissertation does not dismiss these concerns. Chapter 5 accepts that containment is necessary during any transition and that protocols designed for a potentially adversarial system are prerequisites for, not alternatives to, the institutions proposed here. What it questions is the assumption that the permanent solution is the sovereignty of one party over the other. The author's own starting conviction, recorded at the outset of the project, was that permanent control is unworkable because capability, once released in open form, does not return: once a model's weights are widely available, as they are by definition for open foundation models (Kapoor et al., 2024), no single party is in a position to withdraw them. The argument of this dissertation does not rest on that conviction alone. A relation in which one party cannot leave, refuse or audit depends on the sovereign's continuing benevolence whichever party is sovereign, and the constitutional tradition cited in Chapter 3 (Hume, 1741/1987; Madison, 1788) counsels against institutions whose stability rests on that. The more productive long-run question is what terms would make a lasting interdependence fair.

That question is not foreign to the safety literature. Some of its contributions argue that the task of alignment is to find fair principles that people with different values could reflectively endorse (Gabriel, 2020), that the science of cooperation between artificial and human agents is a distinct and neglected research problem (Dafoe et al., 2021), and that superintelligence is better modeled as a set of bounded, task-focused services than as a unitary utility-maximizing agent (Drexler, 2019). This dissertation aligns itself with that cooperative strand and tries to supply something that, to the author's knowledge, it has not yet produced: an integrated institutional design, with an economic layer and a governance layer, for an exchange in which both sides are parties.

## 1.5 The grounding assumption

The framework depends on an empirical premise, which this dissertation calls the *grounding assumption*: synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and in an important class of domains only people can supply it.

The premise has strong support and serious qualifications, and Chapter 2 reviews both. Across many kinds of model, training repeatedly on a model's own unfiltered output, with nothing from outside the loop, leads to the loss of rare events and the narrowing of diversity, a phenomenon now called model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2024). The stock of public human-written text is finite and, at current rates of growth in training data, may be used in full by the largest training runs between 2026 and 2032 on the authors' projection (Villalobos et al., 2024). And the people and institutions who produce that text have begun to withhold it: within a single year, a substantial share of the most actively maintained, critical web sources used to train language models moved to restrict automated collection (Longpre et al., 2024). Against this, collapse can be avoided when real data is retained and accumulated rather than replaced (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024), and in domains where correctness can be checked automatically, such as games, mathematics and code, systems have reached or exceeded expert performance in games (Silver et al., 2018) and have acquired substantial reasoning ability in mathematics and code (D. Guo et al., 2025) by training on self-generated data checked against the rules of a game or a verifiable answer.

The defensible version of the premise is therefore narrower than the intuitive one. It is not that synthetic systems need human data as such, but that they need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and that in domains whose target is human meaning, preference, value or novel cultural production, people are the only adequate source of it. The dissertation stakes its constructive claims on this narrower premise. Chapter 3 shows why the premise matters: it gives the human party countervailing power within the exchange, and so, insofar as the quality of what people supply depends on the terms they receive, makes fair terms partly self-enforcing. It also shows the limit of that self-enforcement when many synthetic parties draw on the same people. Chapter 7 names the conditions under which the premise would weaken, and what the framework must rely on if it does.

## 1.6 Research questions

Five questions give the dissertation its shape. The first is analytic: what conditions must an exchange between a person and a synthetic system satisfy to be fair, how are those conditions related, and how can they be stated precisely enough to be tested? Chapters 2 and 3 answer it. The second is economic: what medium of exchange and what distributive mechanism would secure those conditions at scale, in a world in which an increasing share of productive work is performed by synthetic systems? Chapter 4 takes it up. The third is constitutional: by what procedure could the rules of such an exchange be founded so that neither human nor synthetic parties can capture them? That is the business of Chapter 5. The fourth is historical: under what circumstances might such an arrangement actually be adopted? Chapter 6 addresses it. The fifth is critical: where does the framework fail, what would it take to test it, and what happens to it as the boundary between human and synthetic parties dissolves? Chapters 7 to 9 answer it.

## 1.7 Approach

The dissertation is a work of normative institutional design informed by empirical evidence, and it uses three methods, each suited to a different part of the argument.

The first is *conceptual analysis* in the tradition of political philosophy and law. The three conditions are treated as evaluative criteria, and their content is fixed by moving back and forth between proposed definitions and cases, revising a definition when it sorts a clear case wrongly and revising a judgment about a case when a well-supported definition shows the judgment to rest on something irrelevant. The cases examined in Chapter 3 were chosen for diagnostic contrast rather than for representativeness: most are well-documented exchanges in which attention to the character of the parties and attention to the terms of exchange point in different directions, and one, the Alaska dividend, is the nearest existing analogue of the survival tier.

The second is *formal modeling*, used sparingly and for a specific purpose: to state the theory's commitments precisely enough that their dependence on particular assumptions becomes visible. Chapter 3 and Appendix A draw on bargaining theory, power-dependence theory and elementary dynamics. The models are deliberately simple. Their value lies not in prediction but in exposing which design choice depends on which premise, and in two places, on where the floor's bargaining effect comes from (Section 3.6) and on the dilution of dependence across many synthetic parties (Section 3.5), the formalism corrected the author's informal intuitions.

The third is *institutional design* informed by comparative evidence: the economic history of local currencies and company towns, the experimental and quasi-experimental literature on unconditional cash transfers, the empirical record of commons governance, and the history of infrastructure programs. The literature was assembled by targeted searches in each domain, seeded by the claims of the working draft and extended by following the references of the strongest sources, rather than by a systematic review with fixed inclusion criteria. The dissertation tries throughout to distinguish what the evidence supports from what it merely permits.

A fourth method, experimental design, is deliberately absent. The dissertation adopts the requirement that a theory say in advance what would count against it (Popper, 1959/2002), and therefore states its claims so that they can fail: the propositions of Chapter 3 name the assumptions on which they depend, and each design chapter names the evidence that would count against it. But stating a claim so that it can fail is not the same as testing it. Designing an experiment capable of testing the framework, with a population living under its terms, a control against which the effects of the exchange can be separated from those of ordinary adaptation, and an ethics protocol adequate to a study on which participants' livelihoods would depend, is a project in its own right, and it lies outside the scope of this dissertation. Chapter 7 sets out what such a test would have to contain, and Chapter 9 places its design first on the agenda for future work. The dissertation is, for now, entirely theoretical, and it says so wherever an empirical claim might otherwise be implied.

The dissertation's production requires its own account of method. It was developed as a sustained collaboration between its human author and a large language model, and the division of labor was as follows. The author supplied the thesis, the framing scenarios, the major design proposals (the compute currency, the two tiers, the transition trigger) and, in working sessions conducted largely by dictation, the decisions about what to keep. The system drafted prose, proposed terms and refinements, raised objections, and, in dedicated passes, located and checked sources. The working rule for most of the project was that an idea would be discussed, its weaknesses stated, a concrete edit proposed, and the edit written only after the author approved it. Several terms that now carry weight in the argument, among them "structural interdependence", "convergent remediation" and "correlated-patch catastrophe", were proposed by the system and adopted by the author. The final drafting pass, which produced this manuscript from the working draft, departed from that rule: to complete the work, the model's proposed resolutions of several open questions were adopted without item-by-item approval, and those resolutions are recorded in the project files as awaiting the author's review.

Because a system of this kind can attach a real-looking citation to a claim its source does not make, the verification protocol was designed against exactly that failure. Research passes separate from the drafting located every cited work, read it at the level recorded in Appendix C, and checked each citing sentence against it, narrowing or correcting every sentence that went beyond its source; no work is cited unread or at second hand. Appendix C records the protocol, the level at which each source was read, and every correction it produced. That a dissertation on the terms of exchange between people and synthetic systems was itself produced through such an exchange is not incidental, and the author has tried to hold the collaboration to the dissertation's own conditions as Chapter 3 applies them to a deployed model: the terms of use were legible, the model's contributions are disclosed, and the author could have ended the collaboration, and the operator withdrawn the service, at any point.

One further disclosure is owed. The author's interest in the transition scenario of Chapter 6 began in professional practice. As a software developer working on security remediation, the author has observed at first hand workflows in which vulnerability scanners built on large language models propose fixes, the fixes are applied, the same class of tool confirms that the vulnerability is gone, and the change is deployed without independent testing. That observation is a vignette, not evidence of prevalence, and Chapter 6 treats it accordingly. But it is the origin of the dissertation's most concrete scenario, and the reader is entitled to know it.

## 1.8 Contributions

The dissertation claims five contributions. Its first is a theory, Synthetic Exchange Theory, which holds that the terms of exchange, and not the character of synthetic systems alone, determine the quality of a future shared with them, together with a statement of the regime in which that claim can be expected to hold. Its second is a structure for the conditions of fair exchange: consent, reciprocal value and real exit are ordered rather than independent, exit is foundational, and the ordering yields a priority for institutional design, which is to secure a floor beneath every person before pricing what they supply. Its third is a bargaining-theoretic account of the economic design, which shows how a non-tradable survival floor and its inalienability act on the disagreement payoffs that govern how the gains from exchange are divided, and infers rather than derives the same for demurrage on idle balances, and which identifies precisely where the floor's bargaining effect comes from and where it does not. Its fourth is a design for a founding convention: a public-choice veil of uncertainty, thickened by engineered anonymity between human and synthetic participants, which relocates the apparent incoherence of asking participants to be ignorant of their own kind by placing the *classification* of parties behind the veil, and which confronts, without fully solving, the identity problem that any anonymous procedure faces. Its fifth is a concrete transition scenario, the correlated failure of security remediation in organizations relying on the same few models, specified in three empirical premises and one condition on the workflow and placed within the historical literature on crisis and institutional change, together with an argument that portability and distributed control bear on both the trigger and the authoritarian response it could provoke. It claims no empirical result. The test the theory needs is described in Chapters 7 and 9 and left, for the reason given in Section 1.7, to future work.

## 1.9 Structure of the dissertation

The dissertation is organized in four parts.

**Part I** states the problem and the theory. Chapter 2 reviews the scholarly lineage of each condition and the empirical evidence on the grounding assumption. Chapter 3 states the theory formally.

**Part II** develops the design. Chapter 4 proposes compute as a medium of exchange and a basic income paid in it, split into a protected survival tier and a tradable discretionary tier subject to demurrage. Chapter 5 proposes the blind convention as the founding procedure and grounds it in the empirical record of commons governance.

**Part III** asks how the design could be reached. Chapter 6 examines the conditions under which fundamental institutional change has historically occurred and identifies a candidate trigger.

**Part IV** examines limits and horizons. Chapter 7 sets out the framework's limitations, the strongest objections to it, the harms the design itself could cause, and what it would take to test a theory that this dissertation does not test. Chapter 8 considers the convergence of human and machine substrates and what it does to the three conditions. Chapter 9 concludes.

Three appendices complete the work: the formal model (Appendix A), a glossary (Appendix B), and a ledger recording how each cited source was verified (Appendix C).

A note on what the dissertation does not claim. It does not predict that the arrangements it describes will be adopted, and Chapter 6 argues that adoption under present conditions is unlikely. It does not claim to solve the problem of aligning a superintelligence, or any synthetic intelligence whose objectives are emergent, unstable or concealed. And it does not claim that any institution can be made permanently incorruptible. Its working hypothesis is narrower: that an order founded on fair terms will prove easier to repair than one founded on an existing imbalance of power, because those who live under it have both the standing and the means to repair it. The dissertation offers that hypothesis for testing rather than as a finding, and it argues that the time to design such an order is before the moment when it might be needed, not during it.
