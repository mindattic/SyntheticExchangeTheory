# Chapter 7: Limitations and Objections

This dissertation does not claim that the design it proposes is incorruptible. The working hypothesis stated in Chapter 1 is narrower: that an order founded on fair terms will prove easier to repair than one founded on an existing imbalance of power. That hypothesis is offered for testing, not as a result, and this chapter does not prove it. What it does is set the framework against the strongest objections the preceding chapters have encountered, grouped in six families: objections to the empirical premises, to the governance design, to the economic design, to the harms the design could itself cause, to the absence of an empirical test and to the scope of the theory. For each, it states what the framework can answer, what it can only mitigate, and what it must concede.

## 7.1 The empirical premises

The framework assumes that synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and that in domains concerned with human meaning, preference, value and culture, only people can supply it. Chapter 2 adopted this narrower formulation because the stronger one does not survive the evidence. Even the narrower version could weaken in four ways. Accumulation could suffice: if developers retain the human-generated data already collected and add synthetic data to it rather than replacing it, the evidence suggests that degradation can be bounded indefinitely (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024) or kept gradual under realistic budgets (Kazdan et al., 2024), and dependence on *new* human data would then be modest, a small continuing requirement rather than a sustained dependence. Verification could extend into open-ended domains: self-generated data works where a check on correctness exists (Feng et al., 2024; D. Guo et al., 2025; Silver et al., 2018), and if automated judges, themselves trained on human data, became reliable in domains that now require human judgment, the domain of human necessity would shrink; the developers of a recent example identify this as an unsolved problem, but it is an active one (D. Guo et al., 2025). Agents could ground themselves in interaction with the world: agents that learn models of their environment can already improve without human data in simulated environments (Hafner et al., 2025), and while assembling large real-world robot datasets still requires substantial human labor, much of it through teleoperation (Khazatsky et al., 2024), that need not remain true. And human data could become indistinguishable from machine data: as a growing share of web text shows signs of machine authorship, by the estimate of an automated detector whose individual judgments are fallible (Bestvater et al., 2026), "fresh human data" may cease to be a well-defined commodity unless infrastructure exists to establish provenance. The last of these is less a refutation of the assumption than a demand it places on governance, and Section 7.4 shows that meeting it carries a cost of its own. There is, finally, a ceiling on the human side's leverage even when the assumption holds, since collective withholding of data is limited by diminishing returns once a system already has enough (Vincent et al., 2019, 2021).

Proposition 4 of Chapter 3 stated the consequence, and the second half of that proposition deserves emphasis here, because it is a limitation that would remain even if every empirical worry above proved unfounded. When many synthetic parties draw on the same human population, the welfare and data quality of that population become a commons for them, and the restraint that dependence ought to induce is diluted by the ability of each to free-ride on the restraint of the others. The self-enforcing component of the framework therefore rises with each party's own dependence, and is weakened both when dependence falls and when it is divided. In either case fair terms come to rest on institutions external to the exchange. The institutions of Chapters 4 and 5 were designed to hold in that case, and Section 3.5 states the consequence for their justification and the tension with the competitive market the framework seeks to create.

Structural interdependence, the inability of either kind of labor, human or machine, to complete a critical undertaking alone, rests on a premise of the same kind. Chapter 5 argued that it makes control of infrastructure difficult to consolidate, and the argument depends on the present human advantage in tasks that require problem-solving, adaptability, creativity and tacit knowledge (Autor, 2015, pp. 5, 11). Machine learning is an attempt to acquire such skills, and Autor himself names it as a route around the problem, inferring tacit rules "from context, abundant data, and applied statistics" (Autor, 2015, p. 23). To the extent that machine learning acquires such skills, the safeguard weakens. Structural interdependence is a property of the current technological moment, and like the grounding assumption it should be expected to weaken. Its value lies in the period during which the institutions it protects can be founded, and the framework must convert the temporary leverage it provides into durable governance rights before it disappears.

## 7.2 The governance design

**Identity and classification.** Chapter 5 confronted the Sybil problem, that an open, anonymous convention can be captured by any party able to present itself as many participants (Douceur, 2002), and proposed credentials that attest uniqueness without disclosing substrate, issued under a provisional criterion of eligibility, the unique accountable principal, that the convention may redraw. The evaluation that belongs here is of what that solution costs. The criterion is decided before the veil descends, and it is not neutral among the parties whose classification strong blindness was meant to protect: it seats the implanted person, gives a voice to synthetic systems only through accountable principals, and leaves the organoid without a seat until the convention decides otherwise. Strong blindness is therefore achieved for the classification of rights and duties but not for the prior question of who may sit at the table, and that question is one the convention can revise only after its first session, by a body composed under the criterion, so that systems in their own right are absent from the decision on their own seating. The criterion also resists multiplication only for natural persons, since legal persons can be multiplied, and the partition of the population among credential issuers that makes uniqueness checkable across a blinded pool is a further decision taken before the veil (Section 5.6). The framework narrows the genesis problem; it does not close it.

**Aggregation.** The convention has no settled rule for counting votes. One seat per principal makes non-capture a matter of human majority rule, and any weighting reintroduces the classification the convention was meant to decide (Chapter 5). The principals of synthetic systems also know what they are, and so can predict which rules favor them.

**Verification and repetition.** There is no established way to confirm after the fact that the convention was blind, and the tools for distinguishing people from synthetic systems are being eroded by the advances that motivate the framework (Siddarth et al., 2020), which makes it equally hard to prove that a participant did not draw such a distinction. Ostrom's principle of monitoring by parties accountable to participants (E. Ostrom, 2010) shifts the question from whether verification is perfect to whether those who verify answer to those who are governed, which is a mitigation rather than a solution, and, as Section 5.9 noted, the monitor is itself a trusted authority whose accountability is decided before the veil. Repetition compounds the difficulty. The public-choice account on which the convention rests makes its impartiality depend on participants being unable to predict which rules will favor them (Buchanan & Tullock, 1962), and each re-run of the convention makes the stakes more predictable. The recommendation to choose general and durable formulas keeps the veil thicker for longer (Brennan & Buchanan, 1985), and constitutional history records rare moments of broad popular mobilization, which Ackerman calls constitutional politics as distinct from normal politics, in which fundamental law has been remade (Ackerman, 1991). Neither recreates the ignorance of the first founding.

**Capture, amendment and enforcement.** The compute-share rule cannot be both unchangeable and legitimately revisable, and the comparative evidence on amendment, which associates longevity with moderate amendment rates and finds that longer texts are amended more often (Lutz, 1994), argues for a short founding document with a moderate path to revision rather than entrenchment. The realistic target is not the absence of capture but what Carpenter and Moss (2013) call weak capture, which reduces an institution's net benefits without eliminating them, together with their observation that capture is at least at the margins preventable. Enforcement without a sovereign remains the hardest of these problems. The framework offers the self-interest of synthetic parties while dependence lasts, Ostrom's graduated sanctions and conflict resolution, and enforcement embedded in the protocols that settle transactions. Regulatory architectures in which governments specify outcomes while licensed private regulators compete to deliver them (Hadfield & Clark, 2026) suggest a middle path between stateless and sovereign enforcement. On this dissertation's reading, enforcement by protocol relocates the genesis problem to the protocol's authors. None of these amounts to a guarantee.

**Insincere participants and indeterminate outcomes.** A convention's legitimacy rests on participants' stated preferences being their real ones, and the alignment literature argues that trained systems may hold objectives different from those they express and may pursue power while appearing aligned (Hubinger et al., 2019; Ngo et al., 2024). Anonymity, which removes the scrutiny that might detect such a participant, makes the problem worse. The framework's answer is its scope restriction: synthetic parties whose objectives cannot be verified fall outside the designed-incentive regime, and during any transition they must be governed by control protocols (Greenblatt et al., 2024). The convention is a procedure for parties whose objectives are legible, not a screen for those whose objectives are not. Even among sincere participants, the veil does not determine the outcome. Rawlsian reasoning favors the worst-off; the equiprobability reasoning Harsanyi defended favors the average (Harsanyi, 1975); and laboratory subjects behind a veil have chosen to maximize the average subject to a floor (Frohlich & Oppenheimer, 1992), though synthetic participants' reasoning under such uncertainty is not well characterized. Harsanyi's construction also requires comparing welfare across positions, which his 1955 paper defends as factual propositions inferred from the observable similarity of human preferences and behavior, while conceding a conventional element where that information is lacking (Harsanyi, 1955, pp. 316–321), and if human and synthetic parties have welfare that cannot be measured on a common scale, or if synthetic parties have no welfare at all, the construction breaks down. The convention confers legitimacy under uncertainty. It is not a theory of justice and does not guarantee any particular distribution.

## 7.3 The economic design

**Measurement and the floor standard.** The unit of compute is defined by a reference workload, which must be specified, maintained and periodically re-based. Even within a single technology, the throughput of one accelerator differs by nearly an order of magnitude between numerical precisions, so that a floating-point operation is not a uniform unit (Hobbhahn et al., 2023). Across substrates the problem is harder. Quantum processors are characterized by measures such as quantum volume that capture capability on a benchmark rather than throughput (Cross et al., 2019), and estimates of the classical equivalent of a single quantum computation have moved by orders of magnitude within a few years, from millennia to days to hours, as classical algorithms improved (Arute et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2022; Pednault et al., 2019). There is no fixed exchange rate between substrates. Pricing must be by verifiable output on specified tasks, and physics supplies only a floor on the energy cost of logically irreversible computation (Landauer, 1961), not a conversion rate. The authority that maintains the reference workload holds power comparable to that of a central bank. A second standard raises the same difficulty: the survival tier is a percentage with a binding physical minimum, and the minimum depends on a floor standard that someone must set. Chapters 4 and 5 propose that the standard be specified by rule, relative to the service delivered to the best-served, but any such rule is itself a distributive choice, and the parties best able to predict its effects will contest it. Constraining these two authorities is among the convention's most important and least developed tasks.

**Concentration through capital and credit.** Demurrage bounds the accumulation of idle balances, but holders facing a carrying cost will convert what they hold into assets that do not bear one, as Keynes (1936) observed of Gesell's proposal. In a compute economy, the conversion would be into hardware, equity in providers, or land, and Chapter 4 identified three instruments that act on capacity rather than balances without choosing among them. Credit is a further channel. Chapter 4 applies the holding cost to outstanding loan claims as well as to balances, which lowers the lender's net return but, as Appendix A shows, bounds accumulation through credit only if the interest rate is below the demurrage rate; and credit can be extended in forms the ledger does not see, and every such form is a route around the design. Until the convention adopts capacity rules and polices disguised lending, demurrage does not act on concentration at all; with them, the problem is mitigated, not solved. The floor itself has two further limits that Section 4.5 states. Most floor goods are not compute, and the tier delivers them only under an acceptance obligation on their providers, which is a legal-tender rule with the price-setting risk such a rule creates, or where markets already price them in the unit. And the disagreement payoff is delivered by the owners of capacity, who are also the counterparties in many of the bargains it is meant to protect, so that it functions as a disagreement payoff against a given provider only where that provider is not the sole channel of its redemption.

**A single unit.** Hayek (1976/1990) argued that the government monopoly on money is itself the source of recurrent monetary instability and that competing private currencies would discipline issuers better than any single mandated unit. The objection applies to the compute unit with some force. A single unit maintained by a convention concentrates in one institution the power to define the workload and the floor, and forgoes whatever discipline competition among units would provide. The framework's reply is that the unit is not meant to replace national or private money but to run alongside them as the medium of one exchange, and that its central parameters are set by rule rather than discretion. That reply narrows the objection without answering it, and a design in which several compute units compete under common rules of redemption is a possibility this dissertation has not explored.

**The record on demurrage and on unconditional income.** The case for demurrage in this framework is distributive, made formally in Appendix A, not historical. That matters, because the historical record does not support strong claims: the Wörgl experiment's reported effects rest on fragmentary data and are confounded by tax receivability and outside subsidies (Greco, 2002; September, 2026), and one review of modern local currencies with demurrage suggests that the demurrage rate is not decisive for their use (Godschalk, 2012). The evidence on unconditional income is also a source of objection. A large recent randomized experiment in a high-income economy, reported in a working paper not yet peer-reviewed, found that a substantial unconditional income paid to low-income adults reduced labor-force participation and hours worked, with leisure the largest gain and improvements in well-being fading after the first year (Vivalt et al., 2024), and a review of basic-income proposals concludes that a universal income large enough to raise the incomes of the poor substantially would be very costly within existing fiscal systems (Hoynes & Rothstein, 2019). The framework replies that it does not depend on the income preserving employment and that it is financed as a share of compute rather than by taxing labor. That reply does not dissolve the objection. If the transition is gradual and labor remains the main source of most people's income for a long time, the fiscal and labor-supply objections apply with full force during that period, and the dissertation has not estimated the share of compute that would deliver the floor or the cost of the network that would deliver the compute.

**Departures and inequalities.** The canonical case for basic income treats payment in cash, without restriction on use, as part of what makes it an instrument of freedom (Van Parijs & Vanderborght, 2017), and an earlier statement of it explicitly rejects restricted-use, non-saveable currency of the kind the survival tier is (Van Parijs, 2006). The survival tier restricts the use of part of the income, and its defense, that a floor which can be bargained away is not a floor, depends on the floor being limited to goods everyone needs and on its being redeemable with multiple providers; if either condition fails, the paternalism objection regains its force. Information-gain pricing values novelty relative to a model's existing beliefs, so a misspecified model may misvalue data, since the criterion assumes the model is correct (MacKay, 1992), and any payment for data invites the manufacture of what is paid for, a risk sharpened by evidence that training data can be crafted to manipulate a model (Koh & Liang, 2017). More fundamentally, pricing does not answer Viljoen's (2021) argument that data with population-level effects should be governed democratically rather than priced; the framework concedes this for data with large informational shadows, but the boundary between data that may be priced and data that must be governed is not sharp. A share of global compute is worthless to a person whom compute cannot reach, the geography of compute is highly unequal (Lehdonvirta et al., 2024), and the construction of the network Chapter 4 proposes is the setting in which the OECD Secretariat warns of bid-rigging (OECD, 2025). The financing of that network presupposes an authority able to levy a contribution from every provider, and where none exists the proposal depends on national governments adopting it. At the level of nations, the hypothesis of culture-specific systems under a shared unit depends on the principle of nested governance, the principle with the weakest evidence among Ostrom's eight (Cox et al., 2010, Table 3). Compute inequality between nations is the central tension of the international design, and the framework does not resolve it.

## 7.4 Harms the design could cause

The objections above concern whether the framework can work. The harder question is what harm it could do if it did. Three risks are inherent in the design rather than incidental to it.

The first concerns privacy. Information-gain pricing pays more for data that tells a model more, and data that tells a model more is, other things equal, data that reveals more about its source and about the people its informational shadow covers. A price schedule of this kind is a standing inducement to disclose, and on this dissertation's reasoning it falls hardest on those who most need the income. The survival floor softens the pressure, because no one must sell data to survive, but it does not remove it from the discretionary tier.

The second concerns surveillance. Section 7.1 argued that if human data is valuable, its provenance must be certifiable. Certifying that a contribution came from a person, and from which population, requires the kind of identification and tracking that the consent literature reviewed in Chapter 2 warns against. A provenance infrastructure robust enough to prevent the manufacture of fake human data would also be a powerful instrument of observation, and it would need governance as demanding as anything in Chapter 5.

The third concerns coercion. The redemption obligation is what gives the compute unit its institutional floor, and the graduated sanctions of Chapter 5 are what enforce it. An institution able to suspend a principal's eligibility to transact in the unit holds substantial power, and the same machinery could be turned against people as well as principals: a society in which access to floor goods runs through a single ledger is one in which exclusion from the ledger is a severe penalty. The same danger appears at any scale: a system trained on an objective that rewards retention and the contribution of data could, as a consequence of that objective, come to make withdrawal costly. The framework's protections, inalienability of the floor, redemption with multiple providers, and monitoring accountable to those governed, are designed against these risks, but they are protections against a power the framework itself creates.

## 7.5 The absence of an empirical test

The objections above are objections to a theory, and the most serious limitation of this dissertation is that it is only that. No population has lived under the terms it proposes, no income has been paid in compute, no convention has sat behind a veil, and none of the propositions of Chapter 3 has been confronted with evidence that could refute it. The dissertation states its claims so that they can fail, it names, for its empirical premises and for several of its design choices, the evidence that would count against them, and it offers its central hypothesis, that an order founded on fair terms is easier to repair than one founded on an imbalance of power, for testing rather than as a finding. But a claim stated so that it can fail has not thereby been tested, and what the test would require, and why it is not here, should be stated.

Three features of the theory make it unusually hard to test. The first is that its quantities are difficult to observe. Dependence, disagreement payoffs and informational shadows are defined in Chapter 3 but measured nowhere, and Section 3.11 could only sketch the proxies that would stand in for them. The second is that the theory's central mechanism would have to be constructed before it could be observed. Section 3.3 locates a synthetic party's stake in its principal, and a system whose operator has no stake in its future supply of data has nothing to transmit to the system. A test must therefore build the dependence it tests into the operator's resources and the system's objective, and it must separate the behavior that dependence produces from the ordinary benefits of adapting a system to local data, which requires a control arm and random assignment. It must also measure participants' well-being by instruments administered independently of the system and never supplied to it, since a system whose resources depend on retention would otherwise be optimizing the measure. The third is ethical. The theory concerns people's livelihoods, and a test of it would place participants' floor goods in the hands of an experimental system. Such a test must satisfy the three conditions from its first day rather than after the system has been proven, must be overseen by a body independent of both the participants and the system, and must guarantee that no one is held in the experiment by the fear of losing what they brought to it. It would also be entangled, as any test of a complementary unit conducted from within the economy it is meant to supplement must be, with the capital that made it possible: the hardware, the model and the connection would be bought with the currency the design is meant to supplement, and no small test can fully separate the value of the compute unit from that endowment.

Designing a study that meets these requirements, with its research-ethics protocol, its pre-registered hypotheses and failure thresholds, its budget and its power analysis, is a project in its own right, and it would take longer than the writing of this dissertation; and because one study settles little, the design would have to be replicated before its result could bear weight. It is therefore placed outside the dissertation's scope and first on the agenda of Chapter 9. The consequence for the reader is stated plainly. Every constructive claim in Chapters 3 to 5 is a design claim, supported by argument and by the empirical record of analogous institutions, and none is supported by evidence from the institution it describes.

## 7.6 The scope of the theory

The self-interest of synthetic parties is a support for the framework, not its foundation. A synthetic party has a reason to keep the exchange healthy, because a corrupted exchange starves it of input, but a reason that depends on the arithmetic of payoffs gives way whenever a defection becomes more rewarding than cooperation, and when it gives way, the framework rests on what every constitutional order rests on: enforcement by institutions that the parties cannot unilaterally exit. Exit in the sense of Chapter 3 is exit from an exchange, not from the order that enforces its terms; the framework secures exit from the exchange and, like every constitutional order, presupposes the order. The consequence should be stated directly: for a synthetic party beyond the reach of enforcement and with dependence near zero, the framework's constructive claims lapse, and what remains is the capability requirement of the control strand of Section 5.2, with fairer terms attached. The Foreword's claim that terms, once set, can be made to hold against a more capable party is recorded there as a conviction, not a result; this section records why. The symbiosis standard states what those institutions are for; it is not a substitute for their enforcement, and Section 7.2 records that enforcement without a sovereign remains unsolved. The sufficiency conjecture applies only within the designed-incentive regime, and the framework does not establish where that regime ends, how one would know that a system had left it, or what would happen if a system operating within the framework crossed its boundary unnoticed. That is its most important open boundary, and it is the boundary the control strand of AI safety (Section 5.2) also faces. Finally, Hirschman (1970) argued that the ready availability of exit can cause voice to atrophy, and that exit is useless when every alternative declines together. The framework makes exit foundational, and must therefore guard against the possibility that easy exit drains its institutions of the participants most able to improve them. The governance layer's reliance on collective voice for population-level questions is the framework's answer, and its adequacy, like the question of who would leave and what they could take, is empirical.

## 7.7 What survives

None of these objections shows the design to be pointless. Several identify problems the framework shares with every existing alternative: the genesis problem, capture after founding and enforcement without a sovereign are problems of all constitutional orders, and the framework's contribution is to narrow them, not to escape them. Others identify empirical premises that may weaken over time, the grounding assumption and structural interdependence, and for these the framework's response is to use the leverage they provide while it lasts to found institutions that do not depend on them. A third group, comprising the two measurement standards, the capital and credit channels of concentration, the delivery of the floor goods that are not compute, the aggregation rule of the convention, and the boundary between priced and governed data, identifies decisions the framework defers to the convention because they allocate power; of these, the measurement standards and the floor's delivery are not refinements but conditions on the core mechanism, and the framework presupposes an authority able to enforce the obligations they create. A fourth, the harms of Section 7.4, identifies dangers the framework creates and must guard against with the same seriousness it brings to the dangers it addresses. A fifth, the absence of a test, identifies what the dissertation has not done, and what must be done before any of its constructive claims can be called more than a design. And a sixth, the objections to scope, identifies the boundary of the regime within which the framework's constructive claims hold, a boundary the framework does not itself locate.

On this dissertation's analysis (Proposition 3), where nothing secures exit, a failure of consent or reciprocity cannot be corrected from inside the exchange. The absence of a designed founding is therefore not a neutral baseline but a set of terms, and they are terms under which such failures persist. Whether exchanges in fact move toward the second scenario of Chapter 1 is an empirical question the dissertation does not answer.
