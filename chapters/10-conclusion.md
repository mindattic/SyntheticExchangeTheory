# Chapter 10: Conclusion

## 10.1 The argument in brief

This dissertation began with two people living with the same technology on different terms, and with a claim: that the difference between a good and a bad future with artificial intelligence lies in the terms of exchange between people and synthetic systems, not in the character of the systems. The chapters that followed tried to make that claim precise, to build institutions on it, to say how they might come into being and be tested, and to find where they fail.

Chapter 2 traced the lineage of the three conditions, consent, reciprocal value and real exit, and found in each lineage a characteristic failure: consent that cannot survive the information demands placed on it, data whose effects fall on people who never agreed to share it, mutually beneficial exchanges that are nonetheless unfair, and exit that is formally available but practically impossible. It reviewed the evidence on the framework's empirical premise and adopted a narrower formulation of it than the intuitive one: synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and where the target is human meaning, preference, value or culture, only people can supply it.

Chapter 3 stated the theory formally. It showed that the three conditions are ordered by priority, with exit as their foundation, because voluntary consent presupposes the ability to refuse without falling below a floor and exit is what disciplines reciprocity; it also showed what exit cannot do, which is to inform a party or to make terms fair. It recast the grounding assumption in the language of power-dependence and showed that fair terms are partly self-enforcing, to a degree that rises with each synthetic party's dependence on human input, and that this self-enforcement is diluted when many synthetic parties draw on the same people. And it used bargaining theory to show how the three main elements of the economic design act on the disagreement payoffs that govern how the gains from exchange are divided: a guaranteed floor removes the impatience that forces concession, its inalienability prevents that protection from being sold, and a holding cost on idle balances dampens the advantage of being able to wait. The same analysis corrected an intuition the dissertation had begun with, that an unconditional floor shifts the division of every bargain; it does so only where it replaces something the counterparty would otherwise have supplied.

Chapter 4 proposed compute as a medium of exchange, defined by the completion of a reference workload rather than by any substrate, and a basic income paid as an equal share of global compute, split at the source into a protected survival tier with a binding physical minimum and a tradable discretionary tier subject to demurrage. It set the design against the historical record, which is more modest than enthusiasts of demurrage have claimed, and against the evidence on unconditional income, which is more mixed than either its advocates or its critics usually admit. It identified the limit of every monetary remedy, the conversion of money into productive capital, and argued that a rule acting on capacity, not only on balances, is a necessary complement.

Chapter 5 proposed that the rules of the exchange be founded by a convention conducted under a public-choice veil of uncertainty, thickened by engineered anonymity so that participants do not know whether their counterparties are human or synthetic. It relocated the apparent incoherence of asking participants to be ignorant of their own kind by placing the classification of parties, rather than their substrate, behind the veil, and it conceded that a provisional criterion of eligibility, needed to resist capture by multiplication, must be set before the veil descends. It grounded the proposal in the empirical record of commons governance, mapping all eight of Ostrom's design principles onto the framework and identifying where the mapping is weak.

Chapter 6 asked how such an arrangement might come into being and answered conditionally: comprehensive redesigns have usually followed failure, a specific and already visible candidate for such a failure exists in the correlated errors of AI-assisted security remediation, and a crisis of that kind could open a window onto worse institutions as easily as better ones. Chapter 7 specified a pilot designed so that its central claims can fail, with an incentive built into the system's operator rather than assumed of the system, and a randomized control that separates that incentive from ordinary adaptation. Chapter 8 set out the framework's limitations and the harms it could itself cause, and Chapter 9 followed the convergence of biological and artificial substrates to the point at which the theory's question changes from how parties should exchange to who counts as a party.

## 10.2 What the dissertation contributes

It offers a theory, Synthetic Exchange Theory, and a statement of its scope: benevolent character is insufficient for a good outcome and real exit is necessary for any relation that is not one of domination; fair terms, satisfied at every level the data concerns, are conjectured to be sufficient for parties whose objectives are designed and stable; and the framework claims no more than necessary conditions for parties whose objectives are not. It offers a structure for the conditions of fair exchange, in which exit is foundational, and draws from that structure a priority for institutional design: secure the floor beneath every person before pricing what they supply, and build governance for what neither the floor nor the price can reach.

It offers a bargaining-theoretic account of the economic design, in which the survival tier is an inalienable disagreement payoff whose principal effect is to remove the impatience of those below the floor, and in which demurrage converts the compounding of accumulated wealth into a bound proportional to current net inflow. It offers a design for founding a convention whose participants may be of different kinds, with an account of how the question of self-ignorance can be relocated into the classification of parties and an account of the identity problem that remains.

It offers a concrete transition scenario, convergent remediation, specified in four separable premises with their evidence and counterarguments, together with an argument that the same structural remedy, independent verification and distributed control, answers both the trigger and the authoritarian response it might provoke. And it offers a falsifiable test, with an explicitly designed incentive, a randomized control, pre-registered hypotheses, defenses against the confounds most likely to produce false success, and an ethical protocol that requires the pilot to satisfy the conditions it tests.

## 10.3 An agenda for research

Each part of the argument points to empirical and design work that would strengthen or refute it.

**Measuring dependence.** The framework's self-enforcing component rises with each synthetic party's dependence on human input and weakens when that dependence is shared among many. Both quantities can be measured: by the marginal contribution of fresh, provenance-certified human data to model quality in domains without automatic verification, by the rate at which that contribution declines as synthetic data accumulates, by the effect of collective withholding, and by how many synthetic parties draw on the same populations. Research of this kind would convert the grounding assumption from a premise into a quantity.

**Correlated remediation.** The correlated-patch scenario rests on premises that can be tested directly. Studies could measure the correlation of security fixes proposed by different models for the same vulnerabilities across many codebases, the rate at which same-class validators fail to detect shared defects, and the prevalence of the fully unverified remediation workflow in practice. If the correlation is lower than the scenario assumes, or independent verification remains the norm, the scenario should be revised.

**Experiments on the blind convention.** The strongest existing evidence for the anonymity condition comes from studies in which people chose principles to govern an AI system from behind a veil, and in which knowing that their counterparts were programs made their endorsement of the chosen principles less firm (Weidinger et al., 2023). Experiments could extend this design to conventions in which synthetic systems are participants rather than the things governed, and test whether concealing participants' kind changes the rules they adopt, whether placing classification behind the veil produces different boundaries from those drawn in the open, and whether formulas resist tailoring better than figures.

**The reference workload and the floor standard.** The definition of the unit requires a governance procedure for specifying and re-basing a reference workload across substrates, and the survival tier requires one for setting the floor standard, both comparable in difficulty to the conduct of monetary policy. Work in benchmark design, measurement science and institutional economics could specify such procedures.

**Magnitudes.** The dissertation offers no estimate of the share of compute that would deliver the floor, of the cost of the delivery network, or of the cost and statistical power of the pilot. Each is a prerequisite for judging the proposal affordable and its test informative.

**Portability of personal models.** The convergence chapter argued that exit from an implanted system must include ownership and portability of the personal models the exchange produces. Legal and technical research on what portability of a voice model or a neural decoder would require is urgent, because the people who depend on such systems exist now.

**The pilot.** Above all, the framework needs the test that Chapter 7 describes, and then its replication.

## 10.4 A closing reflection

The dominant question in public discussion of artificial intelligence is whether the machines will be good. It is a natural question, and it is the wrong one to rest on. Arrangements that depend on the goodness of the powerful last exactly as long as that goodness does, and those who depend on them can do nothing to make it last longer. That does not change when the powerful party is made of silicon, or when it is the human party that holds power over the synthetic one.

Recall the second person of Chapter 1. Nothing in his story required a malevolent machine. He could not leave, and so he could not refuse, and so nothing obliged anyone to send value back. The alternative this dissertation has defended is older than the technology it addresses: build the door first. Ensure that every party can leave, and so can refuse what it cannot afford, insist on what it is owed, and see what it gives. Those conditions do not guarantee a good future. They offer something more modest: a future that can be repaired when it goes wrong, by the people, and perhaps someday the other parties, who live in it. That such a future is also more durable is the hypothesis this dissertation offers for testing, not a finding.

The time to set those terms is before they are needed. When the window opens, the ideas lying around will be the ones adopted. This dissertation is an attempt to make sure that a fair design is among them.
