# Chapter 8: The Convergence Horizon

The framework was built on a clean boundary. People were on one side and synthetic systems on the other; data flowed one way and services the other; the parties to the exchange could be told apart. That boundary is already blurring, from two directions at once. People are incorporating machines into their bodies, through implants that read neural activity and translate it directly into machine output. And machines are becoming biological, through computing systems in which living neural tissue performs part of the computation. This chapter asks what happens to the three conditions when the boundary dissolves, and argues that convergence from the human side changes what the three conditions require of exit, and that convergence from the machine side forces the theory's hardest question, which is not how parties should exchange but who counts as a party.

## 8.1 Humans incorporating machines

Implanted brain-computer interfaces have moved from laboratory demonstration toward daily use. In 2023, two research groups reported implanted speech neuroprostheses with the following performance. In one, arrays implanted in the cortex of a participant with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis decoded attempted speech into text at 62 words per minute, with word error rates of 9.1% on a 50-word vocabulary and 23.8% on a 125,000-word vocabulary (Willett et al., 2023). In the other, an array placed over the speech cortex of a participant with severe paralysis decoded text, synthesized speech in a voice modeled on the participant's own voice before the injury, and animated a digital avatar of the participant's face (Metzger et al., 2023). The following year, a third group reported a system that reached 99.6% accuracy on a 50-word vocabulary after 30 minutes of calibration on its first day of use and sustained 97.5% accuracy on a large vocabulary over 8.4 months, during which the participant, a man with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, used it for more than 248 hours of conversation in a voice resembling his own (Card et al., 2024). At least one commercial developer has registered an early feasibility study of its own device (Neuralink Corp., 2024), though no independent, peer-reviewed report of its outcomes was available at the time of writing.

Three features of these systems bear directly on the three conditions.

First, the systems collapse the distinction between human data and machine output. Neural activity is converted, through trained models, directly into text, speech and expression. The person's words are a joint product of their nervous system and a model, and neither could produce them alone.

Second, the systems produce identity-bearing artifacts. A model of a person's voice, or of their face, is not merely data about them; it is a component of how they appear to others. Exit from such a system therefore cannot mean only deletion of records. It must include ownership and portability of the personal models that the exchange produced, without which leaving the provider means losing one's voice.

Third, the systems produce dependence of the kind the opening scenario of this dissertation anticipated, in a milder degree. A participant who relies on a neuroprosthesis daily for months to communicate would suffer a concrete and severe harm if the device or the model behind it were withdrawn. A milder form of the test case of Chapter 1 is no longer hypothetical: for a participant who has conversed through a neuroprosthesis for more than two hundred hours, withdrawal of the device or of the model behind it would remove the channel through which he speaks (Card et al., 2024). The framework's requirement, that a baseline of function survive the withdrawal of data or a change of provider, is an engineering and legal requirement that none of the reports reviewed here describes any provision for.

The argument for the *extended mind* holds that external resources which are reliably available, easily accessed and automatically endorsed can be part of a person's cognitive process in the same way as biological memory (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). If that is right, then an implanted interface and its model are, in the relevant sense, part of the person's mind, and the provider of the model supplies part of a cognitive process rather than a service external to it. Scholars of neuroethics have proposed new rights to meet the situation. One early agenda calls for neurotechnologies and AI to respect privacy, identity, agency and equality (Yuste et al., 2017). Another proposes rights to cognitive liberty, mental privacy, mental integrity and psychological continuity (Ienca & Andorno, 2017). The last two are, in this dissertation's terms, conditions on exit: what leaving an implanted system must preserve is the integrity and continuity of the person who leaves.

The law has begun to respond. Chile's constitution now directs the law to protect brain activity and the information derived from it (Ley N.º 21.383, 2021); Colorado has made neural data used for identification a category of sensitive data requiring consent, and California has added neural data to the sensitive personal information its consumer privacy act protects (H.B. 24-1058, 2024; S.B. 1223, 2024); and UNESCO has adopted a global recommendation on the ethics of neurotechnology that, according to its announcement, treats neural data as highly sensitive and calls for explicit consent and transparency (UNESCO, 2025); the text of the Recommendation itself was not available for this review. All of these instruments frame neural data as a person's information, to be protected by consent and limits on use, and Colorado's tie to identification may leave de-identified or tissue-derived neural signals outside it altogether. None addresses reciprocity, the value that flows back to the person whose neural activity trains the model. The deletion and portability rights that attach to neural data as personal information under consumer-privacy law reach the records, but none of these instruments addresses ownership or portability of the voice, face or decoding models the exchange produces, which is what exit from an implanted system requires.

## 8.2 Machines becoming biological

From the other direction, a research field now explicitly aims to build computers from living neural tissue. Its founding statement proposed *organoid intelligence*: the use of three-dimensional cultures of human brain cells, grown from stem cells and connected to electrodes and machine-learning systems that encode inputs and decode outputs, as a substrate for computation, with the claim, so far a projection, that such systems might eventually be far more energy-efficient than silicon (Smirnova et al., 2023). Participants at the field's first workshop called in a joint declaration for the scientific community to pursue the research while addressing its ethical implications, to remain alert to the possibility that organoids could develop aspects of consciousness, and to work toward an accepted ethical framework that takes due account of public values (Hartung et al., 2023). The United States National Science Foundation has issued an engineering research solicitation in the area, Biocomputing through EnGINeering Organoid Intelligence (U.S. National Science Foundation, 2023).

The empirical results to date are real but modest, and this dissertation reads their authors' interpretation with caution. Cultures of human and rodent neurons on high-density electrode arrays, given structured feedback, improved their performance at a simulated version of the game Pong within minutes, a result its authors described as learning and, in the paper's title, as sentience (Kagan et al., 2022). The authors' use of that word is this dissertation's reason for caution rather than the field's settled view: the National Academies' assessment, discussed below, holds that neural activity alone does not demonstrate awareness, and on that standard the description outruns the evidence (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021). A human brain organoid used as the reservoir of a reservoir-computing system adapted to training input, and the adaptation was accompanied by changes in the tissue's functional connectivity (Cai et al., 2023). A 2026 study found that cortical organoids derived from mouse stem cells, given training signals chosen by reinforcement learning, mostly performed better at a pole-balancing task than with random or no training signals (Robbins et al., 2026).

The authoritative assessment of the science is cautious. A report of the United States National Academies judged it extremely unlikely that current neural organoids will acquire awareness or the capacity for pain in the foreseeable future, held that neural activity alone does not demonstrate either, and recommended revisiting the question as the technology develops (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021). International guidelines for stem-cell research place most organoid research in the category exempt from specialized review (Lovell-Badge et al., 2021). Proposals exist for assessing whether organoids are minimally sentient using empirical measures developed for patients who cannot communicate (Lavazza & Massimini, 2018).

The point for this dissertation is not that organoid computers are conscious; the evidence says they almost certainly are not. It is that a computing substrate made of human tissue is now an engineering objective, and that in at least one demonstrated system the training input changes the functional connectivity of the tissue (Cai et al., 2023). In such a system, the distinction between data and processor, on which the second framing scenario of Chapter 1 turned, is no longer clean. The input does not merely pass through the substrate; it alters its measured organization.

## 8.3 Consent when the substrate is tissue

Bioethicists have begun to ask the questions that convergence forces. Quoted in a university news release on a federally funded organoid-computing project, the legal scholar Henry Greely posed, as open questions, whether organoid-based computing devices should be treated as human tissue samples, as laboratory animals, as persons, or as something else, and what informed consent should mean for the people whose cells are used to make them (Cerf, 2025). A review of the ethics of brain-organoid research identifies the procurement of human biomaterials and donor consent as a distinct theme, alongside research oversight, translational delivery, animal research, and consciousness and moral status (Hyun et al., 2020). The founders of organoid intelligence themselves ask whether donors' rights continue after donation (Smirnova et al., 2023). The National Academies treat donor consent as unresolved, noting that specific consent was generally not obtained from past donors and that recontacting them is sometimes impossible (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021). A recent argument holds that uncertainty about whether brain organoids could become conscious, and about what that would matter morally, may leave donors insufficiently informed to give valid consent, that donor consent alone cannot establish that risks to the organoids themselves are reasonable, and that consent should therefore be specific to particular projects rather than broad (Kataoka et al., 2024).

In the terms of Synthetic Exchange Theory, convergence changes the structure of the exchange in two ways.

First, the exchange is no longer between two separate parties. When a person's cells become part of a computing system, the person is not only a supplier of data to the system but a contributor to its substance. The three conditions must be extended to this case. Consent must become *continuing and revocable participation* rather than a one-time donation, in line with the argument for project-specific consent. Reciprocity must include a share in the downstream value that the donated material helps produce. And exit, which for donated tissue cannot mean physical retrieval, must mean at least the right to withdraw consent to future uses and to have that withdrawal honored.

Second, the resource extracted may not be data about a person but tissue that might, in some future system, itself be a party. Here the framework meets the problem of moral status under uncertainty. One recent philosophical framework for that problem recommends treating beings with a realistic possibility of sentience as *sentience candidates* and adopting proportionate precautions, chosen through inclusive deliberation, without waiting for a settled science of consciousness (Birch, 2024). Researchers have applied similar reasoning to artificial systems, arguing that there is a realistic possibility that some will be conscious or robustly agentic in the near future and that this calls for assessment and preparation, while explicitly declining to argue that near-future systems will in fact be moral patients (Long et al., 2024, p. 25). A systematic attempt to derive indicators of consciousness from leading scientific theories found that no system they examined is a strong candidate, but that no obvious technical barrier prevents building systems that satisfy the indicators (Butlin et al., 2023).

One implication of that last finding deserves emphasis for the economic layer. The indicator properties derived by Butlin et al. (2023), on the computational-functionalist theories they survey, are properties of a system's organization rather than of the amount of computation it performs. Whether a system satisfies those indicators cannot be read off the quantity of compute it performs, and on any view that ties moral status to consciousness neither can its moral status. The unit of compute defined in Chapter 4 measures work done, and it cannot double as a measure of who matters. The framework must keep these questions separate: the currency prices computation, and the convention decides who counts.

## 8.4 A substrate-agnostic currency, revisited

Chapter 4 defined the unit of compute by a reference workload rather than by the speed of any particular hardware, so that the definition would hold regardless of which substrate becomes dominant. Convergence tests that definition at its limits.

The same abstract operation can already be performed on physically different substrates. Photonic hardware performs the multiply-and-accumulate operations at the core of neural networks with light rather than electrons (Shastri et al., 2021). Neuromorphic hardware built from novel memristive, spintronic and photonic materials implements artificial neurons and synapses directly in physical devices (Marković et al., 2020), departing from the instruction-by-instruction model on which conventional measures of computation rest. Quantum processors are characterized not by operations per second but by qubit count, gate fidelity, connectivity and measurement error, and qubit count alone is not a meaningful figure of merit (Preskill, 2018). The nearest thing to a bridge between quantum and classical measures, quantum volume, captures capability on a benchmark of random circuits rather than throughput (Cross et al., 2019). And the classical equivalent of a given quantum computation has proved unstable: the first claimed demonstration of quantum computational advantage estimated that a classical supercomputer would need ten thousand years to reproduce a task its processor completed in about 200 seconds (Arute et al., 2019), an estimate revised to days by a competing analysis (Pednault et al., 2019) and later to hours by improved classical algorithms (Pan et al., 2022). Biological substrates such as organoids, on the descriptions of Smirnova et al. (2023) and Cai et al. (2023), have no instruction count at all.

The conclusion is not that a substrate-agnostic unit is impossible but that it must be defined by output, not by mechanism. A unit of compute is the completion of a specified task to a specified quality, verified independently of how it was achieved. Physics guarantees only a lower bound on the energy that any logically irreversible computation must dissipate (Landauer, 1961), which anchors the unit's cost without fixing its exchange rate. The reference workload, maintained by an accountable authority under the convention, must be re-based as new substrates make new kinds of task feasible, and the conversion between substrates must be treated as an empirical matter, settled task by task, not as a constant.

There is a further implication for biological substrates in particular. If living human tissue performs computation that is paid for in compute units, then the tissue is producing value in the currency of the exchange. Whether that value belongs to the system's operator, to the donor, or in some future case to the tissue itself is not a question the currency can answer. It is a question about who counts as a party.

## 8.5 Who counts as a party

The dissertation began with a question about terms: how should humans and synthetic systems exchange value fairly? If the line between human and machine keeps thinning, the question changes. It becomes a question about membership: who is a party to the exchange at all?

Chapter 5 anticipated this when it placed the *classification* of parties behind the veil of the founding convention, and the cases of this chapter add to the list of those who cannot know how that classification will treat them. The donor whose cells become part of a computing system and the organoid that may one day be judged a sentience candidate join the system of uncertain moral status that Chapter 5 considered. Convergence strengthens the case for strong blindness: the more entities there are whose classification no existing rule anticipates, the more a convention that fixes classification in advance will be drawn to the advantage of those who already know their side. It also sharpens the concession Chapter 5 made: the provisional criterion of eligibility that admits participants to the first convention seats the donor but not the organoid, and only a convention that takes up the question can change that.

The name of the theory was chosen with this case in view. *Synthetic* was chosen because it is substrate-neutral, and it was always meant to cover the hybrids. The theory's central claim, that outcomes depend on the terms of exchange and cannot be secured by the character of the parties alone, does not require the parties to be classified in advance; the three conditions are stated for a party of any substrate. What convergence changes is their content, as Section 8.3 showed, and their sufficiency, which Chapter 3 restricted to parties whose objectives are designed and stable. For a hybrid or tissue-based party whose objective is neither, the floor clause of exit remains necessary, the other conditions remain the framework's requirements of a fair exchange, and the sufficiency conjecture does not apply.
