# Chapter 9: Conclusion

## 9.1 The argument in brief

This dissertation began with two people living with the same technology on different terms, and with a claim: that the difference between a good and a bad future with synthetic intelligence lies in the terms of exchange between people and synthetic systems, and cannot be secured by the character of the systems alone. The chapters that followed tried to make that claim precise, to build institutions on it, to say how they might come into being and to find where they fail.

Chapter 2 traced the lineage of the three conditions, consent, reciprocal value and real exit, and found in each lineage characteristic failures: consent that cannot survive the information demands placed on it, data whose effects fall on people who never agreed to share it, mutually beneficial exchanges that are nonetheless unfair, and exit that is formally available but practically impossible. It reviewed the evidence on the framework's empirical premise and adopted a narrower formulation of it than the intuitive one: synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and where the target is human meaning, preference, value or culture, only people can supply it.

Chapter 3 stated the theory formally. It showed that the three conditions are partially ordered, with exit prior to the other two and as their foundation, because voluntary consent presupposes the ability to refuse without falling below a floor and exit bounds reciprocity from below; it also showed what exit cannot do, which is to inform a party or to make terms fair. It recast the grounding assumption in the language of power-dependence and argued that fair terms are partly self-enforcing, to a degree that rises with each synthetic party's dependence on human input and insofar as the quality of that input depends on the terms its source receives, and that this self-enforcement is diluted when many synthetic parties draw on the same people. And it used bargaining theory to show how the first two elements of the economic design act on the disagreement payoffs that govern how the gains from exchange are divided: a guaranteed floor removes the impatience that forces concession, and its inalienability prevents that protection from being sold. It stated, as an inference from the model rather than a result in it, that a holding cost on idle balances dampens the advantage of being able to wait. The same analysis corrected an intuition the dissertation had begun with, that an unconditional floor shifts the division of every bargain; it lowers the counterparty's payoff only where it replaces something the counterparty would otherwise have supplied, and in neither the additive nor the substitution case changes how the remaining surplus is divided.

Chapter 4 proposed compute as a medium of exchange, defined by the completion of a reference workload rather than by any substrate, and a basic income paid as an equal share of global compute, split at the source into a protected survival tier with a binding physical minimum and a tradable discretionary tier subject to demurrage. It set the design against the historical record, which supports less than is often claimed for it (September, 2026), and against the evidence on unconditional income, which is mixed in ways that limit strong claims on either side. It identified the limit of every monetary remedy, the conversion of money into productive capital, and argued that a rule acting on capacity, not only on balances, is a necessary complement.

Chapter 5 proposed that the rules of the exchange be founded by a convention conducted under a public-choice veil of uncertainty, thickened by engineered anonymity so that participants do not know whether their counterparties are human or synthetic. It relocated the apparent incoherence of asking participants to be ignorant of their own kind by placing the classification of parties, rather than their substrate, behind the veil, a relocation with force for borderline cases and none for present principals, and it conceded that four things must be set before the veil descends: a provisional criterion of eligibility, needed to resist capture by multiplication; an aggregation rule; a partition of credential issuers; and the accountability of the body that monitors the convention's blindness. It grounded the proposal in the empirical record of commons governance, mapping all eight of Ostrom's design principles onto the framework and identifying where the mapping is weak.

Chapter 6 asked how such an arrangement might come into being and answered conditionally: the comprehensive redesigns it reviews followed failure, a specific and already visible candidate for such a failure exists in the correlated errors of AI-assisted security remediation, and a crisis of that kind could open a window onto worse institutions as easily as better ones. Chapter 7 set out the framework's limitations and the harms it could itself cause, and recorded that the theory has not been tested and what a test would require. Chapter 8 followed the convergence of biological and artificial substrates to the point at which the theory's question changes from how parties should exchange to who counts as a party.

## 9.2 What the dissertation contributes

It offers a theory, Synthetic Exchange Theory, and a statement of its scope: benevolent character is insufficient for an outcome whose persistence the weaker party can secure, and a disagreement payoff at the floor, the first clause of real exit, is necessary, for a party that cannot leave, to any relation that is not one of domination; fair terms, satisfied at every level the data concerns, are conjectured to be sufficient, once they are enforced, for parties whose objectives are designed and stable and whose stake is held by an accountable principal, with whether the terms can be made to bind as the conjecture's open premise; and the framework claims no more than that necessary condition for parties whose objectives are not. It offers a structure for the conditions of fair exchange, in which exit is foundational, and draws from that structure a priority for institutional design: secure the floor beneath every person before pricing what they supply, and build governance for what neither the floor nor the price can reach.

It offers a bargaining-theoretic account of the economic design, in which the survival tier is an inalienable disagreement payoff, delivered under an acceptance obligation the framework presupposes an authority to enforce, whose principal effect is to remove the impatience of those below the floor, and in which demurrage bounds idle balances in proportion to current net inflow, a bound that has distributive effect only jointly with a rule on compute-generating capacity, since the design leaves the credit channel open. It offers a design for founding a convention whose participants may be of different kinds, with an account of how the question of self-ignorance can be relocated into the classification of parties, a relocation with force for borderline cases and none for present principals and whose aggregation rule is open, and an account of the identity problem that remains.

It offers a concrete transition scenario, the correlated-patch catastrophe produced by convergent remediation, specified in three empirical premises and one condition on the workflow, each with its evidence and counterarguments, together with an argument that the authoritarian response it might provoke, the concentration of control over compute, is what the framework's portability and distributed control are designed against, and that against the trigger itself the framework supplies only the preconditions of independent verification, not the verification. It does not offer an empirical result. The theory is stated so that it can be tested, in the propositions of Chapter 3 and in the failure conditions each design chapter names, but the experiment capable of testing it has not been designed, and Section 9.3 says what it would require.

## 9.3 An agenda for research

Each part of the argument points to empirical and design work that would strengthen or refute it.

**An empirical test.** Above all, the framework needs a test it can fail, and this dissertation has not designed one. Chapter 7 stated the requirements such a test must meet. In concrete terms, they are: a bounded population living under the framework's terms, including a floor paid in the unit and real rights of exit from the first day; a synthetic party whose operator's resources, and whose objective, depend on the volume and quality of the data participants contribute under revocable consent and on the number who choose to remain, so that the dependence of Proposition 4 is built rather than assumed; a control arm served by a system trained on the same data without that dependence, with random assignment, so that the effect of the exchange can be separated from the effect of local adaptation; measures of well-being collected independently and never shown to the system; pre-registered hypotheses and failure thresholds for exit, for well-being and for the value of the unit; records of who leaves and of what they take with them, since Section 3.10 argued that a low rate of departure is not evidence of satisfaction; oversight by a body independent of the participants, the operator and any model provider; an exit package guaranteeing that no participant is held by the fear of losing what they brought; and an accounting of the capital that funds the test, which cannot be fully separated from the value of the unit (Section 7.5). Each of those elements raises questions of research ethics, cost and statistical power that must be answered before anyone is recruited, and answering them would take longer than this dissertation took to write. The design of that study is the first task of the work that follows this one. Until it is designed, run and replicated, the theory remains what this dissertation has presented: a design, argued for, and untested.

**Measuring dependence.** The framework's self-enforcing component rises with each synthetic party's dependence on human input and weakens when that dependence is shared among many. Both quantities can be measured: by the marginal contribution of fresh, provenance-certified human data to model quality in domains without automatic verification, by the rate at which that contribution declines as synthetic data accumulates, by the effect of collective withholding, and by how many synthetic parties draw on the same populations. Research of this kind would convert the grounding assumption from a premise into a quantity.

**Correlated remediation.** The correlated-patch scenario rests on premises that can be tested directly. Studies could measure the correlation of security fixes proposed by different models for the same vulnerabilities across many codebases, the rate at which same-class validators fail to detect shared defects, and the prevalence of the fully unverified remediation workflow in practice. If the correlation is lower than the scenario assumes, or independent verification remains the norm, the scenario should be revised. Premise 1 would be weakened if independently graded studies on vulnerabilities of ordinary difficulty found that model-written security fixes rarely leave the flaw open, and Premise 3 if fixes sharing a defect were found to be deployed over periods long enough for the defect to be detected and corrected before it was widespread.

**Experiments on the blind convention.** The most direct existing evidence bearing on the anonymity condition comes from studies in which people chose principles to govern an AI assistant from behind a veil; in the one study in which participants knew that their groupmates were programs, the veil's effect on reflective endorsement of the chosen principles was no longer detected, with the qualifications Section 5.3 states (Weidinger et al., 2023, Study 4). Experiments could extend this design to conventions in which synthetic systems are participants rather than the things governed, and test whether concealing participants' kind changes the rules they adopt, whether placing classification behind the veil produces different boundaries from those drawn in the open, and whether formulas resist tailoring better than figures.

**The reference workload and the floor standard.** The definition of the unit requires a governance procedure for specifying and re-basing a reference workload across substrates, and the survival tier requires one for setting the floor standard, both comparable in importance, and in the power they confer, to the conduct of monetary policy (Sections 4.3, 7.3). Work in benchmark design, measurement science and institutional economics could specify such procedures.

**Magnitudes.** The dissertation offers no estimate of the share of compute that would deliver the floor or of the cost of the delivery network. Each is a prerequisite for judging the proposal affordable.

**Portability of personal models.** The convergence chapter argued that exit from an implanted system must include ownership and portability of the personal models the exchange produces. Legal and technical research on what portability of a voice model or a neural decoder would require is urgent, because the people who depend on such systems exist now.

## 9.4 A closing reflection

Much public discussion of synthetic intelligence asks whether the systems will be good. Chapter 3 argued, following Hume (1741/1987, pp. 42–43) and Madison (1788), that arrangements which depend on the goodness of the powerful last only as long as that goodness does, and that those who depend on them can do nothing to make it last longer. That does not change when the more powerful party is synthetic, or when it is the human party that holds power over the synthetic one.

In the second scenario of Chapter 1, no malevolence was required. The person could not leave, and so he could not refuse, and so nothing obliged anyone to send value back. The alternative this dissertation has defended draws on the constitutional tradition that refuses to rely on the virtue of the powerful (Hume, 1741/1987; Madison, 1788) and on Hirschman's (1970) analysis of exit and voice, and it adds a priority of its own (Proposition 3): secure exit first. The priority is to secure that every party can leave, and so can refuse what it cannot afford and depart from an exchange that returns it nothing, and then that institutions secure what exit cannot: an account of what each party gives and a fair division of what the exchange creates. Those conditions do not guarantee a good future. They offer something more modest: a future that can be repaired when it goes wrong, by the people, and perhaps in time the other parties, subject to it. That such a future is also easier to repair than one founded on an existing imbalance of power is the hypothesis this dissertation offers for testing, not a finding.

The time to set those terms is before they are needed. Chapter 6 argued, following Kingdon (1984/2003) and Friedman (1962/2002, p. xiv), that in a policy window the solutions adopted are those already developed. This dissertation is an attempt to make it more likely that a fair design is among the alternatives available.
