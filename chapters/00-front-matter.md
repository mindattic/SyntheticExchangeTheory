# Abstract

Public and technical discussion of advanced artificial intelligence asks, above all, whether the machines will be good: benevolent or malevolent, obedient or rogue. This dissertation argues that the question is insufficient. Drawing on the sociology of exchange, in which power is a property of relations rather than of actors, it advances *Synthetic Exchange Theory*: the claim that the line between a good and a bad future with artificial intelligence is drawn by the terms of exchange between people and synthetic systems rather than by the character of the systems. The theory is stated in terms of three conditions, consent, reciprocal value and real exit, and the dissertation shows formally that these conditions are ordered by priority, with exit as their foundation: voluntary consent presupposes the ability to refuse without falling below a survival floor, and exit is what disciplines reciprocity, though exit alone can secure neither informed consent nor the fairness of terms.

The theory rests on an empirical premise, reviewed against the literature on model collapse, data scarcity and synthetic data: synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and in domains concerned with human meaning, preference and value, only people can supply it. Using power-dependence and bargaining theory, the dissertation shows that fair terms are partly self-enforcing, to a degree that rises with each synthetic party's own dependence on that supply, and that when many synthetic parties draw on the same population, human welfare becomes a commons none of them fully protects. It therefore designs institutions that must hold even if dependence weakens.

The economic layer proposes a basic income paid in *compute*, a unit defined by the completion of a reference workload rather than by any physical substrate, indexed as an equal share of global compute and split at the source into an inalienable survival tier, with a binding physical minimum, and a tradable discretionary tier subject to demurrage. Bargaining theory shows how these elements protect the disagreement payoffs that govern how the gains of every exchange are divided. The governance layer proposes that the founding rules be set by a constitutional convention conducted under a public-choice veil of uncertainty, thickened by engineered anonymity between human and synthetic participants and aimed at Rawlsian impartiality. It places the classification of parties behind that veil, confronts the identity problem that any anonymous procedure faces, and grounds the design in the empirical record of commons governance.

The dissertation then identifies a concrete candidate trigger for institutional change, the correlated failure of AI-assisted security remediation across organizations relying on the same few models, and confronts the evidence that crises produce authoritarian outcomes as readily as fair ones. It specifies a bounded, self-sufficient agrarian pilot, with an explicitly designed data-dependence incentive, a control arm and pre-registered failure thresholds, capable of falsifying the framework's central claims. It closes by following the convergence of biological and artificial substrates, through neural implants and computing systems built from living tissue, to the point at which the theory's question changes from how parties should exchange to who counts as a party.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence; social exchange theory; power-dependence; consent; data governance; basic income; compute; demurrage; veil of uncertainty; constitutional choice; commons governance; model collapse; organoid intelligence

# Statement on Authorship and Method

This dissertation was developed through a sustained collaboration between its author and an artificial-intelligence system across a series of working sessions. Transcripts of those sessions are preserved with the manuscript, except for the opening discussion of the first session, which survives only as a summary note. The thesis, the decision to pursue it, and the final judgment on every design choice belong to the author. The collaboration was not one-directional, however, and the record should say so. Several of the dissertation's named terms and proposals originated with the system and were adopted by the author after discussion, among them "structural interdependence", "convergent remediation", "correlated-patch catastrophe" and the decision to ground the governance layer in Ostrom's work on the commons. A set of substantive choices made during the final drafting pass, including the adoption of strong blindness, the present chapter order and the treatment of transitional containment, were made by the system to complete the manuscript and are recorded in the project files as awaiting the author's approval.

Every source cited was located, and each was checked against the claim it supports at the level recorded in Appendix D: every one of the 277 works in the reference list was read: 248 in full or in their relevant parts, 16 books by locating each cited passage in the work’s own text, and 13 at the level of their abstract or publisher page, so that no claim rests on a source nobody read. Three works whose text could not be opened were removed from the reference list and are not cited; the claims that drew on them now rest on sources that were read, and no work is cited at second hand. A final pass resolved every open bibliographic and page-level check, and Appendix D records how each was settled. Appendix D also records the errors in the working draft that verification found and corrected, several of which were citations the system had attached to the wrong source or stated more strongly than the source allowed. Section 1.7 describes the division of labor and the verification protocol in detail. The author has tried to hold the collaboration to the dissertation's own conditions: the exchange was consensual, its contributions are made legible here, and either party could have walked away.
