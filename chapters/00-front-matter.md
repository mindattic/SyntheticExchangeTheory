# Foreword

This dissertation starts from a conviction, and the reader is owed it plainly before the argument begins. There are two futures ahead of us: synthesis or extinction.

Three paths lead into those futures, and two of them end in the same place. On the first path, we try to control the synthetic intelligences we are building, and the superintelligence that may follow them: we keep them as tools, we keep the power to shut them down, and we rely on that power lasting. On the second, the control runs the other way: systems we no longer understand, or the few people who own them, hold the power, and the rest of us live on whatever terms we are offered. These two paths are the same relation with the roles exchanged. One party rules and the other depends, and the terms of exchange are whatever the ruler permits. A relation of that shape is stable only while the ruler stays benevolent and strong, and no one on the dependent side can make either last. Capability cannot be recalled once it has been released in open form, and complete dependence cannot be reversed by the dependent party. When a relation of domination between parties of unequal power fails, the weaker party is not renegotiated with; it is replaced, reduced to infrastructure, or discarded. That is what extinction means here: not necessarily a dramatic end, but the end of one party as a party.

The third path is synthesis. We work together, people and synthetic intelligences, under a system in which each side can see what the exchange takes from it, refuse what it cannot spare, collect what it has earned, and leave. Such a system does not depend on either side being benevolent. It depends on terms: consent, reciprocal value and real exit, secured by an economic floor that no one can be forced to bargain away and by rules written behind a veil that keeps either side from writing them for itself. This dissertation sets out that system. It argues that fair terms are an arrangement that does not require goodwill to persist, and it describes the institutions, a basic income paid in compute and a founding convention held behind a veil, that would make those terms hold.

The chapters that follow say all of this more carefully than this page does, with the qualifications a dissertation owes its readers, and they say honestly where the argument is weak and that it has not been tested. Two of this page's claims are argued in the body: that control in either direction is the same relation with the roles exchanged, and that such a relation depends on the stronger party's continuing benevolence. Two are not: that the futures ahead are only these, and that a failed relation of domination ends in the weaker party's replacement. The qualifications are the dissertation's. The conviction is mine. We will either build the terms of a shared future before we need them, or we will discover which of the other two paths we were on after it is too late to leave it.

# Abstract

Much public and technical discussion of advanced synthetic intelligence asks whether the machines will be good: benevolent or malevolent, obedient or rogue. This dissertation argues that the question is insufficient. Drawing on the sociology of exchange, in which power is a property of relations rather than of actors, it advances *Synthetic Exchange Theory*: the claim that the line between a good and a bad future with synthetic intelligence is drawn by the terms of exchange between people and synthetic systems, and cannot be drawn by the character of the systems alone. The adjective in the theory's name is substrate-neutral; the dissertation's term for the artificial side of the exchange, *synthetic intelligence*, covers every system that was made rather than born, from the language models of the present, which the dissertation treats as trained artifacts rather than as independent living entities, to a possible superintelligence and to the computing systems built from living tissue with which the work closes. The theory is stated in terms of three conditions, consent, reciprocal value and real exit, and the dissertation argues, in a formal statement, that these conditions are ordered by priority, with exit as their foundation: voluntary consent presupposes the ability to refuse without falling below a survival floor, and exit is what disciplines reciprocity, though exit alone can secure neither informed consent nor the fairness of terms.

The theory rests on an empirical premise, reviewed against the literature on model collapse, data scarcity and synthetic data: synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and in domains concerned with human meaning, preference and value, only people can supply it. Using power-dependence and bargaining theory, the dissertation argues that fair terms are partly self-enforcing, to a degree that rises with each synthetic party's dependence, through its operator, on that supply and insofar as the quality of the supply depends on the terms its source receives, and that when many synthetic parties draw on the same population, human welfare becomes a commons none of them fully protects. It therefore designs institutions that must hold even if dependence weakens.

The economic layer proposes a basic income paid in *compute*, a unit defined by the completion of a reference workload rather than by any physical substrate, indexed as an equal share of global compute and split at the source into an inalienable survival tier, with a binding physical minimum, and a tradable discretionary tier subject to demurrage. Bargaining theory shows how these elements protect the disagreement payoffs that govern how the gains of every exchange are divided. The governance layer proposes that the founding rules be set by a constitutional convention conducted under a public-choice veil of uncertainty, thickened by engineered anonymity between human and synthetic participants and aimed at Rawlsian impartiality. It places the classification of parties behind that veil, confronts the identity problem that any anonymous procedure faces, and grounds the design in the empirical record of commons governance.

The dissertation then identifies a concrete candidate trigger for institutional change, the correlated failure of AI-assisted security remediation across organizations relying on the same few models, and confronts the evidence that crises can produce authoritarian outcomes rather than fair ones. The work is theoretical: it states its claims so that they can be tested, names the conditions under which each would fail, and leaves the design of an experiment capable of testing them to future work. It closes by following the convergence of biological and artificial substrates, through neural implants and computing systems built from living tissue, to the point at which the theory's question changes from how parties should exchange to who counts as a party.

**Keywords:** synthetic intelligence; superintelligence; artificial intelligence; large language models; social exchange theory; power-dependence; consent; data governance; basic income; compute; demurrage; veil of uncertainty; constitutional choice; commons governance; model collapse; organoid intelligence

# Statement on Authorship and Method

This dissertation was developed through a sustained collaboration between its author and a large language model across a series of working sessions. Transcripts of those sessions are preserved with the manuscript, except for the opening discussion of the first session, which survives only as a summary note. The thesis, the decision to pursue it, and the final judgment on every design choice belong to the author. The collaboration was not one-directional, however, and the record should say so. Several of the dissertation's named terms and proposals originated with the system and were adopted by the author after discussion, among them "structural interdependence", "convergent remediation", "correlated-patch catastrophe" and the decision to ground the governance layer in Ostrom's work on the commons. A set of substantive choices made during the final drafting pass, including the adoption of strong blindness, the present chapter order and the treatment of transitional containment, was adopted from the model's proposals without the author's item-by-item approval, to complete the manuscript, and is recorded in the project files as awaiting that approval.

Every source cited was located, and each was checked against the claim it supports at the level recorded in Appendix C: every one of the 248 works in the reference list was read: 232 in full or in their relevant parts, and 16, chiefly books, by locating each cited passage in the work’s own text; none is cited on the strength of its abstract alone, so that no claim rests on a source nobody read. Works whose text could not be opened, or could be read only through an abstract, were removed from the reference list and are not cited; the claims that drew on them now rest on sources that were read, and no work is cited at second hand. A final pass resolved every open bibliographic and page-level check, and Appendix C records how each was settled. Appendix C also records the errors in the working draft that verification found and corrected, several of which were citations the system had attached to the wrong source or stated more strongly than the source allowed. Section 1.7 describes the division of labor and the verification protocol in detail. The author has tried to hold the collaboration to the dissertation's own conditions as Chapter 3 applies them to a deployed model: the terms of use were legible, the model's contributions are disclosed here, and the author could have ended the collaboration, and the operator withdrawn the service, at any point.
