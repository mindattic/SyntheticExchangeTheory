# Appendix C: Glossary

Terms are defined as they are used in this dissertation. Where a term has a wider meaning in another literature, the entry notes the difference.

**Blind convention.** The founding procedure proposed in Chapter 5: a constitutional convention conducted under a public-choice veil of uncertainty, thickened by engineered anonymity so that participants do not know whether their counterparties are human or synthetic, and aimed at Rawlsian impartiality. Under the strong form, participants also do not know how the rules will classify them.

**Company town (regime).** An exchange regime in which wages or entitlements are paid in a medium redeemable only with the payer, so that reciprocity cannot be audited and exit is costly. One of the five regimes of the typology in Chapter 3.

**Compute (unit).** The currency unit proposed in Chapter 4: the completion of one standard quantity of a reference workload to a specified quality, the workload being fixed by the convention. The name deliberately avoids reference to any substrate. Distinguished from *compute* in its ordinary sense, meaning processing capacity in general.

**Convergent remediation.** The process, described in Chapter 6, by which many organizations remediate the same class of flaw in code they maintain themselves using fixes proposed and checked by the same small set of foundation models, so that their fixes become correlated rather than independent.

**Correlated-patch catastrophe.** The candidate acute trigger of Chapter 6: a failure in which a defect introduced by convergent remediation is deployed across many organizations at once and is exploited simultaneously, converting what would have been many independent failures into one correlated failure.

**Critical juncture.** A relatively short period in which the structural constraints that normally keep institutions on their path are loosened, so that choices made within it have large and lasting effects (Chapter 6).

**Demurrage.** A holding cost on idle balances of a currency, expressed as a percentage per period. Applied in this framework to positive discretionary balances and to outstanding loan claims, never to debts owed. Historically associated with Silvio Gesell and with the stamp-scrip experiments of the early 1930s.

**Designed-incentive regime.** The class of synthetic parties whose objectives are specified by accountable designers and remain stable under deployment, so that their behavior is a predictable function of the terms they face. The regime in which the sufficiency conjecture of Chapter 3 is offered. Chapter 7 uses "sub-AGI" as shorthand for it; the defining feature is the provenance and stability of the objective, not a threshold of capability.

**Disagreement payoff.** What a party receives while an exchange is not taking place, including during refusal, negotiation or impasse. Distinguished from an exit option, which affects a bargain only when its exercise is credible. A disagreement payoff below the floor makes a party impatient and so weakens it in every bargain.

**Discretionary tier.** The tradable, saveable and lendable portion of each basic-income allocation, subject to demurrage.

**Enclosure (regime).** An exchange regime in which exit, consent and reciprocity have all failed, so that the person has become infrastructure for the system rather than a party to an exchange with it. The dystopian scenario of Chapter 1.

**Exchange.** A pair of opposing flows between parties, each given because the other is received. Distinguished from a gift (no conditionality) and a taking (no consent).

**Exit guarantee.** In the pilot of Chapter 7, a fund held by an outside trustee sufficient to pay any departing resident the registered value of what they contributed. A backstop that performs, for the pilot, the protective function of the survival tier; not a measure of the pilot's success.

**Exit option.** A party's best alternative exchange if the current one ends.

**Floor.** The physical bundle of goods and services without which a party cannot continue to function or participate in other exchanges. For a human being: power, water, food, shelter, basic communication and emergency care.

**Floor standard.** The rule that fixes what the floor contains and at what level of service, against which the survival tier's binding minimum is set. Proposed in Chapters 4 and 5 to be specified relative to the service delivered to the best-served, and identified in Chapter 8 as a contested parameter.

**Flow.** A transfer of something of value from one party to another: data, labor, attention, compute, goods, services, or tissue.

**Genesis rules.** The first rules of a governance system, which shape every later rule because later rules are made under them. The term borrows from the "genesis block" of a blockchain, the first block from which every subsequent block descends, a metaphor used in the project's early working sessions.

**Grounding assumption.** The empirical premise that synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, which in domains of human meaning, preference, value and culture only people can supply. Stated in Chapter 1, evaluated in Chapters 2 and 8, and formalized in Proposition 4 of Chapter 3 through the dependence $\kappa_j$ of each synthetic party on human input.

**Informational shadow.** The set of parties about whom a data flow licenses inferences. A flow whose shadow extends beyond its source is relational, and individual consent is insufficient to legitimate it.

**Information-gain pricing.** The proposal of Chapter 4 to price human data by the expected reduction in a model's uncertainty about its parameters that the data produces, rather than by the social status of its source.

**Lead system.** In the pilot architecture of Chapter 7, the orchestrating model that delegates to narrower sub-agents, aggregates their outputs, and makes resident-facing decisions.

**Level matching.** Proposition 5 of Chapter 3: an exchange whose informational shadow extends beyond its source satisfies the three conditions only if they hold at the level of the population the shadow covers.

**Marketplace of the desperate (regime).** An exchange regime in which value flows both ways but the weaker party cannot afford to refuse, so that consent is formal only.

**Model collapse.** The degradation of generative models trained recursively on data produced by earlier models, characterized by loss of the tails of the original distribution and reduced variance.

**Partnership (regime).** An exchange regime in which exit, consent and reciprocity all hold. The target of the framework.

**Party.** Any entity that can be a source or recipient of value in an exchange and whose welfare, or whose principal's welfare, the framework counts. Used in an analytic sense that leaves open whether synthetic parties have moral status. A synthetic party is treated as a composite: its objective belongs to the deployed system, its stake in the exchange to the principal that operates it.

**Patronage (regime).** An exchange regime in which exit and reciprocity hold but consent fails: benefits are delivered on terms the recipient did not choose but can leave.

**Policy window.** A brief opening in which problems, solutions and politics converge, often after a focusing event, and in which solutions already developed are likely to be adopted (Chapter 6).

**Real exit.** The condition that a party's disagreement payoff is at or above its floor, that it has a credible alternative not drastically worse than the current exchange, and that leaving does not destroy assets accumulated within the exchange.

**Reciprocal value.** The condition that each party gains from the exchange relative to its disagreement payoff, that the gains are legible to both, and that the division of the surplus is not determined solely by an unchosen asymmetry in disagreement payoffs.

**Redemption obligation.** The obligation of principals operating synthetic systems under the framework to honor redemptions in compute. It gives the unit an institutional backing beneath its commodity value, in the role tax receivability played for municipal scrip (Chapter 4).

**Reference workload.** The basket of representative tasks, each with a quality target and rules of configuration, whose completion defines a unit of compute. Fixed and periodically re-based by the convention.

**Stop rules.** Conditions, fixed in advance by the pilot's independent ethics board, under which the pilot's closed economy is suspended and the exit guarantee activated (Appendix B).

**Strong blindness / weak blindness.** Two scopes of concealment in the blind convention. Under weak blindness, participants do not know whether their counterparties are human or synthetic. Under strong blindness, they also do not know how the rules they adopt will classify them, though each knows its own substrate.

**Structural interdependence.** The condition, described in Chapter 5, in which neither human nor synthetic parties can complete a critical undertaking without the other, so that cooperation rests on what is physically possible rather than on trust. Chosen over "trust" and "dual accountability" because trust implies a betrayal that could succeed, and accountability implies a sovereign to whom account is given.

**Survival tier.** The portion of each basic-income allocation earmarked at the source for floor goods: a fixed percentage of the allocation, raised when necessary to a binding physical minimum set by the floor standard. Non-tradable and non-borrowable: an inalienable disagreement payoff.

**Synthetic.** As used in the name of the theory, substrate-neutral: covering artificial systems, human beings, and the hybrids that arise when the two converge. The framework's term for the class of parties, chosen over *human–AI* and *symbiotic*.

**Unique accountable principal.** The provisional criterion of eligibility for a seat in the blind convention proposed in Chapter 5: an entity identifiable as one bearer of the obligations the convention will create. Set before the veil, revisable by the convention.

**Veil of uncertainty.** The condition, in public-choice accounts of constitutional choice, in which real, self-interested participants choose rules while genuinely uncertain about which position they will occupy under them, so that self-interest recommends rules acceptable from any position. Distinguished from Rawls's idealized veil of ignorance.
