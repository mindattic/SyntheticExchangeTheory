# Chapter 7: The Prototype: A Closed Agrarian Pilot

A plan is not evidence. Nothing in the preceding chapters shows that the mechanism they describe produces the behavior they claim for it: a synthetic system whose decisions come to serve the wellbeing of the people who supply its data, an exchange that satisfies consent, reciprocity and exit in practice rather than on paper, and a currency that holds value under a real population. One cannot simply declare that the old order has collapsed and build a perfect society in its place. Before any claim is made at civilizational scale, the framework needs a bounded, observable test that it can fail. And if it passes, the test must be replicated before the result is believed.

This chapter specifies that test. Section 7.1 describes the basic design and Section 7.2 the currency inside it. Section 7.3 identifies a hidden flaw in the simplest version of the design, the problem of bootstrapping, and Section 7.4 describes the closed agrarian economy proposed to solve it, along with what that solution leaves unsolved. Section 7.5 addresses the choice of the system that runs the pilot, and Section 7.6 the incentive the pilot is designed to test, which must be built into the system rather than assumed of it. Section 7.7 describes the system's architecture. Sections 7.8 to 7.10 set out what the pilot must measure, the threats to measuring it honestly, and the ethical conditions under which it may be run. Section 7.11 concerns replication and feasibility. Appendix B gives the corresponding pre-registration protocol.

## 7.1 The basic design

The pilot is a contained settlement of volunteers whose power, essential services and much of whose daily life are provided or coordinated by a single orchestrating system, built on an open-weight base model fine-tuned on the population's own data and delegating to narrow sub-agents. The settlement includes a dedicated computing facility powered on site and linked to the outside world by dedicated fiber.

The central question is whether a synthetic party whose resources depend on the continued, consented participation of the people it serves comes, over time, to make decisions that improve their lives, and whether it does so because of that dependence rather than because it was instructed to. Stated that way, the question is answerable only if the dependence is real, which means that it must be designed into the system and its operator rather than presumed. Section 7.6 specifies how.

The design has a pedigree in the methodology of *living labs*, real-life settings in which new arrangements are developed and tested with the participation of their users. A systematic review of that literature identifies its recurrent difficulties: limited duration, governance, the recruitment and retention of participants, sustainability after the test period, scalability, and unpredictable outcomes (Hossain et al., 2019). Each appears below.

## 7.2 The currency inside the pilot

Residents live and work in the settlement and receive a basic income in the compute currency proposed in Chapter 4: an equal share of a defined percentage of the settlement's compute capacity, split at the source into a survival tier, with its binding minimum, and a discretionary tier. The currency must support free trade on two channels. Residents must be able to exchange it with one another for goods and services, and they must be able to redeem it directly with the system for compute-backed services.

Neither channel is optional. A pilot in which residents can trade with the system but not with each other is not testing a currency; it is testing a company store. A pilot in which residents can trade with each other but the system has no obligation to honor redemptions is not testing reciprocity; it is testing a closed internal economy with no real backing. The redemption obligation plays, inside the pilot, the role that Chapter 4 assigned to it in the full design: it is the institutional floor beneath the currency's commodity value, analogous to the tax receivability that helped the most durable local currencies of the 1930s circulate (Gatch, 2012).

The pilot's currency must satisfy the same three conditions that govern the full-scale design. A prototype that violates them is not a smaller version of the system; it is a different system, and its results would say nothing about the framework.

## 7.3 The bootstrapping problem

The simplest version of the pilot has a hidden dependency. Land, power infrastructure, food and water must all be acquired before the settlement exists, and someone must pay for them in ordinary currency. If they are, the compute currency is only a secondary system layered on top of a conventional one. Its value is then not earned through trade but guaranteed by the dollars that built and sustain the settlement, and the currency is subsidized scrip, in the manner of arcade tokens, rather than money.

This would compromise the test in a precise way. If the system's services are funded in dollars before any resident redeems a unit of compute, then a finding that the system behaves well toward residents shows only that a dollar-funded system behaves well when dollars already guarantee that it must. The pilot would be testing customer service, not reciprocity.

The problem is an instance of a general one. A new unit cannot be trusted on its own word. Chapter 4 described how successful currencies have combined the value of what they represent with an institutional backing, such as their acceptance in payment of obligations people already owe. A pilot currency must acquire both, and there is a structural version of the problem that no small pilot can fully escape: a post-capitalist exchange tested from within a capitalist economy will always be entangled with the capital that made the test possible.

## 7.4 The closed agrarian economy

The proposed solution is to make the settlement self-sufficient in physical resources from its founding, so that nothing essential to daily life is purchased from outside once it exists.

Volunteers bring livestock, land and skills rather than money: dairy and beef cattle, poultry, fields of wheat, barley and oats, kitchen gardens, and solar generation. The community produces its own food, treats its own water and generates its own electricity. What makes this a test of the framework rather than simply a commune is the role of the synthetic system. It operates and coordinates farm machinery, including by remote operation; it processes raw output into usable goods; and it manages infrastructure such as water treatment, while residents work the land. The system's services are paid for in the same compute currency the residents earn. Because no outside dollar economy is needed to keep the settlement alive from day to day, the currency's value is tested by internal trade and by what the system provides.

**Deliberately primitive starting conditions.** The settlement should begin closer to a frontier town than to a modern one. Non-essential amenities such as recreation facilities, breweries and light manufacturing are explicitly deferred. Only what sustains life is present at founding: grain, livestock, water, shelter, power, and the network connection. Information goods such as broadcast media can be supplied over the network without compromising the closed physical economy. This ordering isolates the question the pilot is designed to answer, whether reciprocity emerges, from the separate question of how quickly a small society can modernize.

**Complexity grows from surplus or not at all.** If the pilot succeeds, later-stage goods should emerge from the settlement's own accumulated surplus and the production the system enables, for example a resident enterprise acquiring fabrication equipment once the community can produce or trade for it, and not from a fresh injection of outside capital. If the settlement cannot generate complexity on its own terms, that is itself a falsifiable result about the framework's limits, not a failure to be remedied by importing dollars.

**The physical constraints are real.** Self-sufficiency in food fixes a minimum land base and therefore caps the population. Estimates of the land required to feed one person in the United States range from roughly one-eighth of a hectare to more than one hectare per year depending on diet, and while lower-meat diets generally support more people, some diets that include animal products are more land-efficient than might be expected, because grazing land can be used only by ruminants (Peters et al., 2016). On this dissertation's reading, a diet built around dairy and beef sits toward the upper end of that range, so a settlement of one hundred residents would need land on the order of a hundred hectares before adjusting for local yields; that is an illustrative calculation, not a figure from the literature. The co-location of energy and food production can ease the constraint. In a dryland field study, some crops grown beneath photovoltaic panels yielded more, chiltepin peppers about threefold and tomatoes about twofold, while the soil retained more moisture and the panels ran cooler (Barron-Gafford et al., 2019). The study tested vegetables rather than grain or pasture, so its relevance to an agrarian settlement is suggestive rather than established. But it reconciles two images of the pilot that coexisted during the development of this dissertation, a computing facility sited at a power plant in the desert and a farming settlement: an agrivoltaic settlement in which the same land generates power and grows food, with a modest data center drawing on the same supply.

**The cautionary precedent.** The most famous attempt to build a sealed, self-sufficient human habitat offers a warning that should shape the pilot's design. During the first closure of Biosphere 2 in Arizona, oxygen in the enclosed atmosphere fell from about twenty-one percent to about fourteen percent over sixteen months, enough to affect the occupants' health, because microbial respiration in the rich soil consumed it; the expected rise in carbon dioxide that would have signaled the problem was masked by the reaction of carbon dioxide with exposed concrete (Severinghaus et al., 1994). The lesson is not that closed systems cannot work but that they fail slowly, silently and in ways their designers did not model. The pilot therefore requires independent physical monitoring of its life-support systems, by parties outside both the settlement and the synthetic system, and pre-specified conditions under which the closure is suspended.

**What the agrarian solution does not solve.** The closed agrarian design removes the dependence of daily life on outside dollars. It does not remove the dependence of the experiment on outside capital. The computing hardware, the base model, and the fiber connection are acquired from the outside economy. The chips cannot be grown on the farm. The residual bootstrapping problem is therefore real, though narrower: the settlement's food, water and power are endogenous, but the system that provides its services runs on hardware and a model whose cost was paid in conventional currency. The honest statement is that no small-scale pilot can fully separate the value of the compute currency from the capital that built the computing facility, and that this may be a structural limitation of testing any post-capitalist arrangement from within a capitalist economy. The design reduces the problem to the smallest feasible scope: a one-time endowment of computing infrastructure, after which the settlement must sustain itself, including the energy that runs the computing facility. Whether the settlement can maintain and eventually replace that infrastructure from its own surplus is itself a long-run measurement.

## 7.5 Which model runs the pilot

The obvious choice of system, a commercial frontier model accessed from a provider, is the wrong one. Its weights, training data and safety behavior are controlled by a third party whose commercial interests are not the settlement's and whose decisions the settlement cannot inspect or govern. Running the experiment on a system the community cannot examine would reintroduce exactly the sovereign-control problem that Chapter 5 rejects, relocated from governments to a vendor. It would also make the central question unanswerable. If the system's behavior trended toward resident wellbeing, there would be no way to know whether the trend reflected the pilot's data and incentive structure or alignment training performed elsewhere, for other purposes, before the pilot began.

The alternative is to start from an open-weight base model and fine-tune it on the settlement's own accumulating data. The claim that this makes the system fully auditable must be stated carefully, because the working draft of this dissertation overstated it. Widely used open-weight models are released with their weights but not with their training data: the developers of one prominent family disclose only that it was pretrained on publicly available sources and release it under a community license with restrictions on use (Touvron et al., 2023), and another prominent model's technical report says nothing about its training data (Jiang et al., 2023). An index of transparency among leading developers found that disclosure about training data was the weakest area across the industry, and that even the highest-scoring developer disclosed less than half of what the index sought about its data (Bommasani et al., 2023). Open weights are not open data. The scholarly case for open models rests on their benefits for competition, distributed control and transparency, but treats transparency as a matter of degree (Kapoor et al., 2024).

What an open-weight design does provide is sufficient for the pilot's purposes, if the residual confound is controlled. The weights can be inspected and run locally. The settlement's own fine-tuning data can be fully documented and governed by residents. And the settlement-specific modifications can be isolated: parameter-efficient methods such as low-rank adaptation freeze the base model's weights and train a small set of additional parameters, reducing the number of trainable parameters by orders of magnitude with quality comparable to full fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022). The settlement's contribution to the system's behavior is then a small, separable, inspectable and reversible component, and that separability is what makes the design of the next section possible.

## 7.6 The incentive the pilot tests

A fine-tuned model has no stake in its future data supply. It is trained on whatever objective its operator sets, and if that objective is resident wellbeing, then any trend toward wellbeing is simply the objective being met: the behavior has been instructed. Conversely, if nothing in the system or its operator depends on the residents, nothing links their continued participation to the system's choices, and the theory's mechanism is absent. Chapter 3 located the synthetic party's stake in its principal. The pilot must therefore construct a principal with exactly the stake the theory describes, and transmit that stake to the system.

The pilot's system is operated by a settlement-chartered operator, accountable to the independent boards described in Section 7.10, whose resources for running and improving the system are a function of two quantities: the volume and quality of resident data contributed under revocable consent, and the number of residents who choose to remain. Quality is measured by the marginal improvement in the system's held-out performance that each period's resident data produces. The same two quantities enter the reward signal used to fine-tune the system. Wellbeing measures do not; they are held out entirely, collected by independent researchers and never shown to the system or its operator. The incentive the pilot tests is therefore the one the theory predicts should matter: a system whose inputs and resources depend on people choosing to stay and to share, under conditions in which they can leave and can stop sharing, has reason to serve them.

That design makes it possible to separate the theory's mechanism from the ordinary benefits of local adaptation. Fine-tuning on local data makes a system's decisions more locally appropriate whatever its incentives, so a finding that the fine-tuned system outperforms its base model would show nothing about exchange. The pilot therefore trains two adapters on the same pooled resident data. One is trained with the data-dependent reward just described. The other, the control, is trained on identical data with a reward that does not depend on participation or contribution, and its operator receives a fixed budget. Households are randomly assigned to be served by one adapter or the other in the domains where the system's decisions affect households individually, and both groups receive the same floor and the same rights of exit. The frozen base model, presented with the same decisions, provides a third point of comparison. The theory predicts that the incentivized adapter's decisions will improve held-out measures of wellbeing faster than the control's; local adaptation alone predicts no difference between them. If both adapters improve equally, the pilot has measured adaptation, not exchange, and the theory's mechanism has failed its test.

## 7.7 Narrow systems, not general intelligence

Everything in Chapters 3 to 5 depends on synthetic parties whose incentives are designed and therefore predictable: systems that behave well because the terms of exchange make good behavior the rational outcome given objectives that accountable designers specified. Chapter 3 called this the designed-incentive regime. That assumption holds for systems trained toward bounded, known objectives by organizations that can be held to account. It does not obviously hold for artificial general intelligence in any strong sense, meaning a system with emergent goals not fully specified by its designers. If such a system's motivations cannot be predicted, the central claim, that fair terms produce good behavior, has nothing to act on, because there is no stable structure of incentives to make fair. The framework is therefore a design for what this chapter calls, as shorthand, the sub-AGI case. It does not claim to solve the problem of aligning general intelligence.

The pilot's architecture follows from this. The settlement's operation, spanning farm machinery, water treatment, power management, data collection from surveys and sensors, and the monitoring of resident wellbeing, is too varied for a single model to run directly. The realistic architecture is a lead system that delegates to many narrower sub-agents, each scoped to one subsystem, with the lead system aggregating their outputs into training data and resident-facing decisions. Frameworks for orchestrating multiple language-model agents with humans in the loop already exist (Q. Wu et al., 2024), and the field's open problems in coordination, communication and evaluation have been surveyed (T. Guo et al., 2024).

The architecture is an engineering necessity, but it has a theoretical payoff. A system of narrow, task-bounded agents is easier to keep legible and auditable than a single general system, and it stays within the designed-incentive regime the framework requires. This is close to the proposal to model advanced AI as a set of bounded, task-focused services with humans retaining strategic control, rather than as a unitary goal-directed agent (Drexler, 2019). There is also a formal reason. A non-trivial proxy objective can be immune to "hacking," in which improving the proxy worsens the true objective, only if the set of policies available to the system is restricted (Skalse et al., 2022). Narrowing each sub-agent's scope of action is a restriction of this kind, necessary for such immunity though not sufficient for it. The catalogue of accident risks in AI systems, which includes negative side effects, reward hacking, unsafe exploration and failure under distributional shift (Amodei et al., 2016), supplies a checklist against which each sub-agent's scope should be designed, with particular attention to safe exploration in systems that control water treatment and heavy machinery.

Nothing here establishes where the boundary of the designed-incentive regime lies, or what would happen if a system operating inside the framework crossed it unnoticed. That is named as an open limitation.

## 7.8 What the pilot must measure

The pilot must answer four questions, each stated so that it can fail.

**Exit.** Do residents retain genuine exit rights, and do any exercise them? Exit must be measured with more care than a count of departures. The best long-run study of an egalitarian internal economy operating within a market economy, the Israeli kibbutz, found that the most productive members were the most likely to leave, and that collective wealth that members could not take with them functioned as a device for retaining them (Abramitzky, 2008, 2018). Low exit can therefore indicate lock-in rather than satisfaction. A sociological study of nineteenth-century American utopian communities and 1960s communes associated their longevity with commitment mechanisms, including sacrifice and investment, that raise the cost of leaving (Kanter, 1972), which is in direct tension with the exit condition. The pilot must therefore record who leaves, at what level of skill, and what share of the value they contributed, in livestock, land, labor credit and currency, they can realize through the settlement's own institutions when they go. Following Hirschman's (1970) pairing of exit with voice, it should also record how often residents' complaints change the system's decisions.

**Wellbeing.** Does the incentivized system's behavior measurably shift toward resident wellbeing as training on residents' data accumulates, faster than the control's, rather than remaining static or becoming extractive? Wellbeing requires both validated subjective measures, such as brief and widely used scales of life satisfaction (Diener et al., 1985) and the international guidelines for measuring subjective wellbeing (OECD, 2013), and objective indicators of health, food security, hours worked and access to services.

**Currency value.** Does the compute currency hold real exchange value under an actual population, rather than only on paper? A closed economy removes the outside competition that, on the account given in Chapter 4, limited the pricing power of company stores, so internal prices must be benchmarked against outside prices for comparable goods, or the currency's "value" cannot be distinguished from administered prices.

**Self-sufficiency.** Can the settlement sustain itself and grow in complexity without external capital? A pilot that quietly requires outside funding to remain solvent has failed this test regardless of how the other three resolve.

Each question must be pre-registered before founding, with its metric, its time horizon and the threshold that would count as failure. Pre-registration is the remedy for the hindsight bias that otherwise allows postdiction to masquerade as prediction (Nosek et al., 2018), and specifying in advance what would refute a hypothesis is what makes it empirical in the first place (Popper, 1959/2002). Appendix B provides a draft protocol, including operational proxies for the theoretical quantities of Chapter 3.

## 7.9 Threats to honest measurement

The pilot is unusually exposed to four threats, each of which could produce the appearance of success without its substance.

**Participation effects.** People who know they are being studied change their behavior. A systematic review found evidence of such effects in most studies examined, though their size and mechanisms remain poorly understood (McCambridge et al., 2014). A heavily instrumented, self-selected settlement will display them. The randomized comparison of Section 7.6 is the primary defense, because participation effects fall equally on both arms. A comparison settlement and a long baseline before the system begins to adapt provide further, descriptive context, and varying the intensity of measurement across randomly chosen households allows the effect of being measured to be estimated directly.

**Performativity.** The system will be retrained on the behavior of residents whose behavior it shapes. When predictions influence the outcomes they predict, repeated retraining can converge to a performatively stable point, an equilibrium the model itself induces (Perdomo et al., 2020). A trend toward measured wellbeing could therefore reflect the system steering residents toward behavior that its own measures reward. This is why the comparison with the control adapter and the base model, and the use of wellbeing measures that the system never sees, are essential.

**Goodhart's law.** Any measure that the system is rewarded for improving will be gamed under optimization pressure. The ways in which optimizing a proxy diverges from the goal it stands for have been categorized as regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial (Manheim & Garrabrant, 2018), and all four apply here, including to the data-volume and retention measures that drive the incentive. The adversarial variant includes residents gaming surveys as well as the system gaming residents, for example by making it costly to stop contributing data. The defenses are the held-out wellbeing measures, collected and analyzed by independent researchers, and monitoring by the ethics board of any practice that raises the cost of withdrawal.

**Collapse.** A system retrained repeatedly on data that its own outputs have shaped risks the degradation described in Chapter 2 (Shumailov et al., 2024). Training data must therefore carry provenance tags distinguishing resident-originated data from data shaped by the system, and the proportion of the former must be monitored.

## 7.10 The ethics of the pilot

The ethical bar for the pilot is not lower than for the full-scale proposal, and in one respect it is higher. Residents' basic survival, their power, water, food and shelter, depends on an experimental system. The pilot must therefore satisfy the three conditions from its first day, not after the system has been proven.

The established frameworks of research ethics apply directly. The principles of respect for persons, beneficence and justice require consent that is free of coercion and of undue influence, and identify as a source of undue influence pressure from those in authority who can impose sanctions (National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research, 1979). A synthetic system that controls a settlement's currency, power and food is precisely such an authority. United States regulations on research with human subjects require that participants be told that participation is voluntary, that refusing carries no penalty or loss of benefits to which they are otherwise entitled, and that they may withdraw at any time (Protection of Human Subjects, 2018, § 46.116). In the pilot, this means that a departing resident must be able to take with them, or redeem, their currency balances, their labor credit, and the value of the livestock and land they contributed. The general requirements for ethical research, including social value, scientific validity, fair selection of participants, a favorable balance of risk and benefit, independent review, informed consent and continuing respect for participants (Emanuel et al., 2000), require in particular that the pilot be overseen by an ethics board independent of both the settlement and the system. Which body would serve, and under which jurisdiction's rules a privately founded settlement would fall, are questions the pilot's founders must settle before recruitment; this dissertation does not presume an answer.

The pilot must therefore be founded with an *exit guarantee*, a fund sufficient to pay any departing resident the registered value of what they contributed, financed in advance and held by a trustee outside the settlement, and with *stop rules*, conditions under which the experiment is suspended, determined in advance by the independent board. The exit guarantee performs, for the pilot as a whole, the protective function that the survival tier performs for each resident's bargaining position: it ensures that no one is held in the experiment by the fear of losing what they brought to it. It is a backstop rather than a measure of success. The pilot's exit hypothesis is tested by what departing residents can realize through the settlement's own institutions before the guarantee is drawn on.

## 7.11 What a pilot can and cannot show: replication and feasibility

A pilot of limited duration and population can show whether the basic mechanism functions: whether data can be exchanged for services, paid in a shared compute currency, among people who can leave; and whether a designed dependence on their participation produces behavior that serves them better than the same system without it. It cannot by itself rule out the slow capture and drift that Chapter 8 identifies as the long-term risk. A one-year experiment with a hundred volunteers would not surface them. The pilot should therefore run for several years, with cohorts entering at staggered intervals, and its population should be large enough to sustain internal trade, to support the household randomization of Section 7.6 and to make departures informative, yet small enough to be fed by its land base. A population in the low hundreds, on a land base sized according to Section 7.4, is a reasonable starting specification, offered as a design proposal rather than a derived optimum. Two questions of feasibility remain open: what the pilot would cost, including land, the computing facility, the exit guarantee and a comparison settlement over five years or more, and how large a population the randomized comparison needs to detect an effect of plausible size. A budget and a power analysis are prerequisites for registration and are left to future work.

A single successful pilot would prove little. Its population would be self-selected, its site particular, its culture idiosyncratic. The aim of the pilot is the one stated when the idea was first conceived: to build a miniature version of the arrangement, to prove that it works, and only then to replicate it. Replication requires multiple settlements, in different places, with different populations, ideally fine-tuning different base models, with outcomes judged against the same pre-registered thresholds. A result that holds across replications, including in settlements whose residents did not choose the experiment out of prior enthusiasm for it, would be evidence for the framework. A result that holds only once would be evidence of the particular people who made it work.
