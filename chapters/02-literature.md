# Chapter 2: Literature and Grounding

The three conditions of Synthetic Exchange Theory are not new ideas. Each has a substantial scholarly lineage, and each lineage has discovered, in its own vocabulary, a characteristic way in which the condition fails. This chapter maps that terrain. It begins with the sociological theory of exchange from which the dissertation takes its name and its central analytic tool. It then examines, in turn, the literature on consent and its limits, the objection from relational data, the proposal to treat data as labor and its critics, the philosophy of exploitation, the economics and sociology of exit, the emerging institutions of collective data governance, and the current state of the law. The final sections turn to the empirical premise on which the framework rests, the continuing dependence of synthetic systems on human input, and set out what the evidence does and does not support.

## 2.1 Exchange as the unit of analysis

Social exchange theory began with the proposal that social behavior can be understood as an exchange of goods, material and non-material alike, in which approval, prestige and help are traded as surely as money and labor (Homans, 1958). Its early statements drew the lesson that people in exchange calculate something like profit, reward minus cost, and that their judgments of fairness track whether rewards are proportional to investments. Blau (1964) distinguished social exchange, which carries unspecified obligations and depends on trust built up incrementally, from economic exchange, whose terms are specified in advance and enforced by law, and showed how imbalances in exchange produce power. A person who needs a service that another controls, and who has nothing to offer in return, can coerce the other, obtain the service elsewhere, do without it, or subordinate themselves; when the first three are unavailable, subordination is what remains.

The decisive contribution for this dissertation is Emerson's (1962) theory of power-dependence, which locates power in the relation between actors rather than in either of them. In his formulation, the dependence of one actor on another is directly proportional to the first actor's motivational investment in goals the second mediates and inversely proportional to the availability of those goals outside the relation; the power of the second actor over the first is equal to that dependence. An unbalanced relation, Emerson argued, can be rebalanced in four ways. The weaker party can withdraw its motivational investment in what the stronger supplies; it can extend its network to cultivate alternatives; a status order can emerge in which the weaker party confers on the stronger a standing the stronger comes to value; or the weaker parties can form a coalition that the stronger must deal with collectively. These operations recur throughout this dissertation, which refers to them consistently as withdrawal, network extension, status emergence and coalition formation. Laboratory studies of exchange networks were later reported to show that power is a function of position in the network, so that less dependent actors with better alternative sources obtain better terms even when they do not know their position, and that emergent commitments to particular partners impede the use of power (Cook & Emerson, 1978; Cook et al., 1983). The further inference, that such commitment deepens dependence, is this dissertation's own. The tradition of interdependence theory begun by Thibaut and Kelley (1959) adds a distinction between satisfaction with a relationship and dependence on it: dependence is governed by the comparison level for alternatives, the standard a member uses in deciding whether to remain in a relationship or leave it (Thibaut & Kelley, 1959, p. 21), so that a person can be dissatisfied with a relationship and still be unable to leave it. A modern review places the structure of interdependence at the center of an account of how relationships shape interaction and commitment (Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003).

These ideas supply the dissertation's analytic frame. If power is relational in this sense, then the danger of a world with powerful synthetic systems lies not only in the systems but in the structure of the relations in which people are placed with respect to them. Whether an AI system is good or bad is a question about an attribute of an actor. Whether a person can leave, refuse or audit their exchange with it is a question about a relation, and it is the second question that institutions can act on.

One further strand of this literature anticipates a design question taken up later. Comparing the forms that exchange can take, Molm (2003) argues that whether terms are negotiated explicitly or arise from reciprocal giving without a guaranteed return changes the mechanisms through which power is used and alters how prominent the cooperative and the competitive faces of exchange become. The point is a caution against assuming that every exchange between people and synthetic systems should be made as explicit and contractual as possible. Some, such as the licensing of data for training, may need to be negotiated; others, such as continuing care, may be better governed as reciprocal relationships with protections in the background.

## 2.2 Consent and its limits

The first condition, consent, has the longest lineage and the best-documented failures.

An influential philosophical account of informed consent treats it, in its primary sense, as an autonomous authorization: an authorization given intentionally, with substantial understanding, and substantially free of controlling influences (Faden & Beauchamp, 1986, pp. 241–273, 277–280). Each element has been shown to fail in the context of data. On understanding, a review of behavioral research on privacy organizes the evidence around three themes: people are uncertain about the consequences of their privacy choices and about their own preferences, their concern is highly dependent on context, and it is malleable by the commercial and governmental actors who benefit from shaping it (Acquisti et al., 2015). On the practical machinery of consent, Solove (2013) argued that the dominant model of "privacy self-management," in which individuals are given notice and choice and left to manage their own privacy, fails for both cognitive and structural reasons. People cannot make informed tradeoffs, there are too many entities to manage, harms emerge from the aggregation of data over time, and downstream uses are unknowable at the moment consent is given. He identified a dilemma: consent is often not meaningful, but the obvious alternative, paternalistic restriction, denies people the freedom to choose. More recently, Solove (2024) has gone further, arguing that most consent in privacy law is a fiction and that it should be treated as "murky consent," authorizing only a weak and restricted license, paired with substantive duties on organizations, including a duty of loyalty and a duty to avoid unreasonable risk.

On controlling influences, the literature on manipulation defines it as intentionally and covertly influencing a person's decisions by exploiting their vulnerabilities, distinguished from persuasion, which offers reasons, and from coercion, which openly constrains options (Susser et al., 2019). Manipulation damages autonomy through the process itself, even when the outcome is good for the person. Its practice has been documented at scale: a crawl of some eleven thousand shopping websites found 1,818 instances of interface designs that steer users toward choices they would not otherwise make, and identified twenty-two third-party vendors offering such patterns as turnkey products (Mathur et al., 2019). A consent obtained through an interface engineered to obtain it is not the autonomous authorization the theory requires.

Two alternatives to the informational model of consent are important for this dissertation. Nissenbaum's (2009) theory of contextual integrity holds that a flow of information is appropriate when it conforms to the norms of the context in which the information was shared, norms specified by the actors involved, the type of information, and the principles governing its transmission. Contextual integrity supplies a standard for appropriate flows that does not depend on individual agreement. And Manson and O'Neill (2007) argue that informed consent should be understood as a communicative transaction that waives specific obligations in a controlled way; it can never be fully specific or fully explicit, and its ethical quality lies in the success of the communication rather than in the quantity of information disclosed.

The lesson for Synthetic Exchange Theory is that the consent condition cannot rest on information alone. The formal statement of Chapter 3 accordingly treats consent as having three clauses: an informational clause, which the literature shows is routinely unmet but can be improved by better standards of communication and contextual norms; a voluntariness clause, which depends on the person's ability to refuse without losing what they need to live; and a revocability clause. The second clause is where consent depends on exit, and Chapter 3 argues that it, rather than the informational clause, is usually the binding constraint.

## 2.3 The relational objection

The most serious objection to any framework built on individual exchange is that data about one person is also data about others.

Barocas and Nissenbaum (2014) showed that the two standard instruments of data protection, anonymization and informed consent, both fail under the conditions of large-scale data analysis. Anonymity fails because individuals can be re-identified or, more importantly, because they need not be identified at all to be affected by inferences drawn from data about people like them. Consent fails partly because of a transparency paradox, in which notices simple enough to understand cannot describe complex flows with fidelity, and partly because of what they call the tyranny of the minority: the information volunteered by a few can reveal the same facts about the many who share their observable characteristics. This problem holds, they stress, even when every individual chooses well for themselves.

Viljoen (2021) generalized the point into a relational theory of data governance. The primary purpose of data production in the digital economy, she argues, is to relate people to one another on the basis of shared features, which creates interests at the level of populations. Existing law governs "vertical" relations between a data subject and a data collector; it has no way to represent the "horizontal" relations among people who share features and are therefore affected by one another's data. Viljoen classifies the main reform proposals into two families, both of which she finds inadequate because both are individualist: "propertarian" proposals, which treat data as an alienable asset, including proposals to treat data as labor, and "dignitarian" proposals, which treat data as an extension of the self. Her alternative is to govern data as a democratic medium, through institutions that can represent population-level interests.

This objection must be answered, and Chapter 3 attempts an answer. It introduces the concept of the *informational shadow* of a flow, the set of people about whom the flow licenses inferences, and argues that the three conditions must hold at the level of the population the shadow covers; Proposition 5 states the rule. The answer concedes the core of Viljoen's argument: where data is relational, the remedy must be collective and governed democratically, not merely priced. What the dissertation retains from the exchange framework is the insistence that the collective, too, must be able to consent, to receive value, and to leave.

## 2.4 Data as labor and its critics

The idea that data is a form of labor and that the services people receive are a kind of wage has a real lineage. Arrieta-Ibarra et al. (2018) argued that the digital economy treats user data as capital, ignoring users' role in producing it, and that this framing weakens incentives to produce valuable data, concentrates gains, and feeds fears about automation. Treating data at least partly as labor could restore a functioning market for user contributions, they argued, but the dominant buyers of data would resist, so that the shift would require countervailing power combining competition, a "data labor movement," and regulation. Posner and Weyl (2018) developed the idea as part of a broader program of market reforms. Lanier (2013) proposed an information economy in which people would receive small payments for the data they contribute to network services, and a later essay with Weyl adopted the term "data dignity" and proposed "mediators of individual data," volunteer organizations representing their members that would negotiate data royalties or wages and so bring collective bargaining to the people whose data is valuable (Lanier & Weyl, 2018).

The critics of this program press two objections. The first is the relational objection already discussed: Viljoen (2021) explicitly includes data-as-labor among the individualist reforms she rejects, on the ground that granting a person a right to sell their data does not secure fair conditions of sale and cannot represent the interests of the many others whom the data concerns. The second is that pricing data commodifies something that should not be commodified, a concern with deep roots in the philosophy of property (Radin, 1987).

This dissertation takes from the data-as-labor tradition its central insight, which is that the flow of data from people to synthetic systems is an exchange and should be governed as one, with value flowing back. It rejects a pure per-person market in data for the reasons the critics give, and its economic design reflects that rejection in three ways. The basic income of Chapter 4 is paid to everyone regardless of the market value of their data, so that the floor beneath each person does not depend on what their life is worth to a model. The pricing of data is by informational novelty rather than by social status, so that the undersampled are not undervalued. And the governance of data with large informational shadows is collective. Notably, Arrieta-Ibarra et al. themselves concede that the shift they propose depends on countervailing power, which in the vocabulary of exchange theory means coalition formation. The data-as-labor tradition and its relational critics converge, on this reading, on the need for collective institutions.

## 2.5 Exploitation

A further philosophical complication bears directly on the reciprocity condition. An exchange can be consensual and beneficial to both parties and still be exploitative. The philosophical literature calls this mutually advantageous exploitation, in which one party takes unfair advantage of another even though the other gains and agrees, and one prominent account, set out in the survey by Zwolinski, Ferguson and Wertheimer, judges the unfairness against a benchmark such as the price that would obtain in a competitive market free of informational asymmetry and undue pressure (Zwolinski et al., 2022). Contemporary philosophical treatments distinguish the moral weight of exploitation, how wrong it is, from its moral force, whether others may intervene, and observe that prohibiting mutually beneficial but unfair transactions can leave the vulnerable worse off; redistribution aimed at the underlying vulnerability is offered as an alternative to banning the transactions (Zwolinski et al., 2022). Zwolinski (2012) has argued, against structural accounts, that background injustice should play relatively little role in determining whether a particular transaction is exploitative.

Two consequences follow for Synthetic Exchange Theory. First, "value flows both ways" is not enough. The reciprocity condition must include a fairness clause, and Chapter 3 states one: the division of the gains must not be determined solely by an asymmetry in disagreement payoffs that the weaker party did not choose. Second, the exploitation literature supports the dissertation's strategy of addressing unfair exchanges by improving the weaker party's position rather than by prohibiting the exchanges. The guaranteed floor of Chapter 4 is precisely an intervention on the underlying vulnerability. It leaves people free to make exchanges with synthetic systems and ensures that they are not forced to make them on any terms offered.

## 2.6 Exit, voice and lock-in

Hirschman's (1970) analysis of exit and voice is the classic account of how members of an organization respond to its decline. They can leave, which signals decline indirectly through lost revenue or membership, or they can attempt to change the organization from within, which Hirschman called voice and described as political action par excellence. Voice is the only recourse where exit is unavailable, as in families, states and pure monopolies. Two of Hirschman's further observations bear on this dissertation. The ready availability of exit can cause the art of voice to atrophy. And when quality declines across an entire industry at once, exit becomes ineffective movement between equally deteriorating firms. A market in which a few providers of synthetic services share similar practices is a case of the second kind: switching among them is not real exit.

The economics of switching costs explains why exit is so often difficult in technology markets. Switching costs and network effects lock customers into early choices and give sellers market power after the fact; firms therefore compete fiercely to acquire customers with low introductory prices and then exploit them, a pattern described as "bargain-then-ripoff," and firms may choose incompatibility strategically to weaken rivals, so that policy should look particularly carefully at markets where incompatibility is chosen rather than inevitable (Farrell & Klemperer, 2007). The provision of free or subsidized services by AI providers, followed by deepening dependence, fits this pattern.

The real-exit condition of Synthetic Exchange Theory is accordingly stated in terms that address both literatures: it requires that a person's disagreement payoff be at or above the floor, that a credible alternative exist whose value net of switching costs is not drastically lower, and that leaving not destroy assets accumulated within the exchange. Hirschman's analysis adds a requirement that the formal statement takes up explicitly: where exit is costly, as it is at the level of populations whose data has already been absorbed into trained models, and where every alternative shares the same defect, the conditions must be secured through voice, which is the function of the governance layer.

## 2.7 Collective institutions and data leverage

If individual exchange cannot by itself satisfy the three conditions for relational data, collective institutions must. A growing literature describes what they might look like.

Data trusts would allow individuals to pool their data rights in legal trusts whose trustees owe them a duty of undivided loyalty, stricter than a duty of care; people could choose trusts that reflect their values and move between them, and the pooling would increase their negotiating power and allow consent to be negotiated collectively (Delacroix & Lawrence, 2019). Data cooperatives, modeled on credit unions, would hold and protect members' data, run analyses for members' benefit, and negotiate with service providers on their behalf (Hardjono & Pentland, 2019). A comparative review of legal mechanisms for data stewardship concludes that no single mechanism suits every case and that a shared purpose must come first (Ada Lovelace Institute, 2021).

The literature on data leverage turns the logic of power-dependence into concrete actions. Because data-dependent technologies depend on the public's contributions, the public holds potential leverage: people can withhold or delete their data in a *data strike*, contribute deliberately harmful data, or redirect their contributions to a competitor they prefer, a strategy called *conscious data contribution* (Vincent et al., 2021). In Emerson's terms a data strike is not itself a rebalancing operation. It is an exercise of the countervailing power that a system's dependence on data already confers, and the collective organization needed to make it effective is coalition formation; conscious data contribution, by strengthening an alternative provider, is a form of network extension. A simulation study suggests that data strikes can degrade the performance of recommender systems (Vincent et al., 2019), though their effectiveness is limited by diminishing returns once a firm already has enough data, a limit that echoes the qualifications on the grounding assumption discussed below (Vincent et al., 2021).

There is direct evidence that such leverage is already being exercised at scale. A longitudinal audit of the consent signals attached to some fourteen thousand web domains underlying widely used training corpora found that within a single year, a substantial fraction of the most actively maintained critical sources had moved to restrict automated collection fully, and that terms of service restricted crawling for close to half of one major corpus (Longpre et al., 2024). The human side's leverage, in other words, is not hypothetical. People and publishers are already exercising it, though through blunt instruments that were never designed for the purpose.

## 2.8 The state of the law

Existing law contains partial analogues of each condition, and their limits are instructive.

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation requires that withdrawing consent be as easy as giving it, and provides that whether a contract is made conditional on consent to unnecessary processing bears on whether consent was freely given (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, art. 7). These provisions are the closest legal analogue to the voluntariness clause of the consent condition. The regulation's right to erasure gives individuals grounds to require deletion, subject to exceptions including scientific research (art. 17). Its right to data portability is the closest legal analogue to real exit, but it is narrow: it covers only data the individual has provided, processed on the basis of consent or contract by automated means, with direct transfer between controllers required only where technically feasible, and it must not adversely affect the rights of others (art. 20). Inferred data and trained models fall outside it. The more recent Data Act extends access and portability to data generated by connected devices and requires that customers be able to switch between cloud providers, with charges for switching to be eliminated from January 2027 (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854). It is the clearest current example of bounded switching costs written into law, and evidence that real exit is a legislatively achievable condition.

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act entered into force in August 2024, with its prohibitions applying from February 2025 and its obligations on providers of general-purpose models from August 2025 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). Those obligations include a policy for complying with copyright law, including honoring opt-outs from text and data mining, and the publication of a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training (art. 53). This is the closest existing law to a consent signal for training data, though it is an opt-out regime, not opt-in consent. A 2026 amending regulation, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the "Digital Omnibus on AI," published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force from 27 July, has deferred the obligations for high-risk systems to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone systems and to 2 August 2028 for systems embedded in regulated products, so that they do not yet apply (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; Agustinoy, 2026; ERTICO, 2026).

Neural data, which Chapter 9 discusses, has begun to receive specific protection. Chile amended its constitution in 2021 to require that scientific and technological development serve people and to direct the law to protect brain activity and the information derived from it (Ley N.º 21.383, 2021). Colorado in 2024 added neural data to the categories of sensitive data requiring consent under its consumer privacy law, where the data is used or intended for identification (H.B. 24-1058, 2024), and California added neural data to the "sensitive personal information" governed by its consumer privacy act (S.B. 1223, 2024). UNESCO adopted a global recommendation on the ethics of neurotechnology in November 2025 (UNESCO, 2025). All of these instruments treat neural data as a person's sensitive information. None yet addresses the case, considered in Chapter 9, in which living tissue is itself part of the computing substrate.

## 2.9 The empirical anchor: what synthetic systems need from people

The framework's grounding assumption is that synthetic systems need a continuing supply of input from outside their own generative loop, and that in an important class of domains only people can supply it. This section reviews the evidence for and against that assumption, because the dissertation's claim that fair terms are partly self-enforcing depends on it.

**The case for dependence.** A consistent body of work shows that generative models trained repeatedly on their own unfiltered output, with nothing from outside the loop, degrade. In the most widely cited study, models of several kinds, including a small language model fine-tuned over successive generations, lost the tails of the original distribution first and then converged toward distributions with greatly reduced variance; the authors called the process model collapse and concluded that sustained learning requires preserving access to data not generated by models (Shumailov et al., 2024). The same pattern appears in image models, where fully self-consuming training loops lose quality or diversity and only loops that receive enough fresh real data in each generation remain stable (Alemohammad et al., 2024); in statistical models of the simplest kind, where purely self-referential maximum-likelihood retraining converges on degenerate states that lock in whatever biases the initial sample contained (Jangjoo et al., 2026); and in language, where the lexical and especially the syntactic diversity of model outputs declines across generations of recursive training, while semantic diversity remains comparatively stable, with the steepest decline in the most creative task, story generation (Y. Guo et al., 2024). A theoretical analysis of mixed training in regression and random-feature models shows that if synthetic data makes up any non-vanishing fraction of a growing training set, additional data eventually stops improving the model, and that mixing strategies mitigate this only if real data grows at least as fast as synthetic data (Dohmatob et al., 2025).

These results acquire economic significance alongside evidence on the supply of human data. Compute-optimal training is data-hungry: the most influential study of the question found that model size and training data should be scaled in equal proportion (Hoffmann et al., 2022). For a fixed compute budget, training on up to about four epochs of repeated data changes loss negligibly compared with unique data, while further repetition adds progressively less until additional compute is worth almost nothing (Muennighoff et al., 2023). The effective stock of high-quality public human text is finite, and if current trends continue, the largest training runs will exhaust it within the next several years (Villalobos et al., 2024). Meanwhile, the stock is being diluted: a 2026 analysis using an automated detector estimated that about a tenth of a July 2026 sample of 10,000 English web pages, and more than a third of the pages in its larger sample published since the release of widely used chatbots, show significant signs of machine authorship, while cautioning that such detectors misclassify individual documents (Bestvater et al., 2026). And, as Section 2.7 showed, the people and institutions who produce the stock are withholding it (Longpre et al., 2024).

The value of human input is also visible in the methods by which current systems are made useful. Training with human preference judgments allowed a model with roughly one-hundredth as many parameters as its predecessor to produce outputs that human evaluators preferred (Ouyang et al., 2022), building on work showing that a relatively small quantity of human comparisons can substitute for a hand-specified objective (Christiano et al., 2017).

**The case against strong dependence.** Three bodies of evidence limit the assumption.

First, collapse depends on replacement. When synthetic data is added to an accumulating pool that retains the original human data, rather than replacing it, a linear-model analysis shows error remaining bounded however many generations pass, with consistent results in experiments across several kinds of model (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024), and under realistic fixed training budgets the degradation is gradual rather than catastrophic (Kazdan et al., 2024). A critical review identifies eight distinct and partly conflicting definitions of model collapse in the literature and argues that several prominent scenarios are readily avoidable (Schaeffer et al., 2025). Even the canonical study found that retaining a tenth of the original data in each generation limited the damage to minor degradation (Shumailov et al., 2024).

Second, where an external check on correctness exists, self-generated data can work extremely well. A system given no domain knowledge beyond the rules of chess, shogi and Go learned through self-play to defeat a world-champion program in each game (Silver et al., 2018). Reinforcement learning with automatically verifiable rewards produced substantial reasoning ability in a language model without human-written reasoning examples, though the final system still relied on curated human data and its authors identified domains without automatic verification as an unsolved problem (D. Guo et al., 2025). Synthetic data filtered by a sufficiently accurate verifier, even an imperfect one, avoided the collapse that unfiltered data produced in the tasks studied (Feng et al., 2024), and preference labels generated by a model can substitute for human labels in some settings, though the labels came from an off-the-shelf pretrained language model (Lee et al., 2024), whose own training text was human-written.

Third, agents can learn from interaction with an environment rather than from people. A world-model-based agent learned to perform well across more than a hundred and fifty tasks in simulated environments, including collecting diamonds in a complex game world from scratch, without human data (Hafner et al., 2025), building on earlier work showing that agents can be trained inside learned models of their environment (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018). Robotics remains limited by the scarcity and fragmentation of real-world training data (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024; Firoozi et al., 2025), and assembling large real-world robot datasets still requires substantial human labour, with demonstrations gathered by human data collectors (Khazatsky et al., 2024), so that even embodied data, on present methods, depends heavily on human work. Policies trained in simulation can be transferred to physical robots, though with a persistent gap between the two (Zhao et al., 2020). The inference that the physical world could one day supply an external signal that neither runs out nor comes from people is this dissertation's, and it concerns the future rather than the present.

**What the evidence supports.** Two cautions apply to all of this work. Almost all of the evidence for collapse comes from small models or stylized theory; no published study demonstrates the collapse of a frontier model under realistic data practices. And the evidence for substitution is concentrated in domains where correctness can be checked mechanically. Taken together, the literature supports the following formulation, which this dissertation adopts:

> Learning systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop. Where correctness can be verified mechanically, or where a faithful simulator exists, that information can come from verifiers, rules, simulators or the physical world, and dependence on people is weak. Where the target is human meaning, preference, value or new cultural production, people are the only adequate source of that information, and their contribution must keep pace with, and not merely seed, the volume of synthetic data in training.

This is narrower than the intuitive version of the assumption, but it survives the counterevidence, and it fits the exchange logic of the dissertation better. What people supply to synthetic systems is ground truth about people, which nothing else can verify. Chapter 3 formalizes the consequence: the human party's countervailing power within the exchange rises with each synthetic party's dependence on this input, and the framework must be designed to hold even if that dependence turns out to be small.

## 2.10 Where the literature leaves the thesis

The literature reviewed here leaves Synthetic Exchange Theory with four obligations.

It must treat consent as resting on more than information, because the evidence that informational consent fails is overwhelming, and it must locate the binding constraint in the voluntariness of refusal, which depends on exit. It must answer the relational objection with collective institutions rather than individual prices, while insisting that those institutions also satisfy the conditions of consent, reciprocity and exit at their own level. It must give the reciprocity condition a fairness standard that goes beyond mutual benefit, and it must address unfair exchanges by improving the weaker party's position rather than by prohibition. And it must state its empirical premise in the form the evidence supports, which is narrower and more defensible than the intuitive form, and design its institutions to survive the premise's weakening.

The next chapter takes up these obligations formally.
