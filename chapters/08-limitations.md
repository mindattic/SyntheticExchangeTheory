# Chapter 8: Limitations and Objections

No governance system has achieved permanent incorruptibility, and this one does not claim to. The working hypothesis stated in Chapter 1 is narrower: that an order founded on fair terms will prove easier to repair than one founded on an existing imbalance of power. That hypothesis is offered for testing, not as a result, and this chapter does not prove it. What it does is set the framework against the strongest objections the preceding chapters have encountered, grouped in five families: objections to the empirical premises, to the governance design, to the economic design, to the harms the design could itself cause, and to the scope of the theory. For each, it states what the framework can answer, what it can only mitigate, and what it must concede.

## 8.1 The empirical premises

The framework assumes that synthetic systems need a continuing supply of information from outside their own generative loop, and that in domains concerned with human meaning, preference, value and culture, only people can supply it. Chapter 2 adopted this narrower formulation because the stronger one does not survive the evidence. Even the narrower version could weaken in four ways. Accumulation could suffice: if developers retain the human-generated data already collected and add synthetic data to it rather than replacing it, the evidence suggests that degradation can be bounded indefinitely (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024) or kept gradual under realistic budgets (Kazdan et al., 2024), and dependence on *new* human data would then be modest, a trickle rather than a partnership. Verification could extend into open-ended domains: self-generated data works where a check on correctness exists (Silver et al., 2018; D. Guo et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2024), and if automated judges, themselves trained on human data, became reliable in domains that now require human judgment, the domain of human necessity would shrink; the developers of the most prominent recent example identify this as an unsolved problem, but it is an active one (D. Guo et al., 2025). Agents could ground themselves in interaction with the world: agents that learn models of their environment can already improve without human data in simulated environments (Hafner et al., 2025), and while assembling large real-world robot datasets still requires substantial human labour, much of it through teleoperation (Khazatsky et al., 2024), that need not remain true. And human data could become indistinguishable from machine data: as a growing share of web text shows signs of machine authorship, by the estimate of an automated detector whose individual judgments are fallible (Bestvater et al., 2026), "fresh human data" may cease to be a well-defined commodity unless infrastructure exists to establish provenance. The last of these is less a refutation of the assumption than a demand it places on governance, and Section 8.4 shows that meeting it carries a cost of its own. There is, finally, a ceiling on the human side's leverage even when the assumption holds, since collective withholding of data is limited by diminishing returns once a system already has enough (Vincent et al., 2021).

Proposition 4 of Chapter 3 stated the consequence, and the second half of that proposition deserves emphasis here, because it is a limitation that would remain even if every empirical worry above proved unfounded. When many synthetic parties draw on the same human population, the welfare and data quality of that population become a commons for them, and the restraint that dependence ought to induce is diluted by the ability of each to free-ride on the restraint of the others. The self-enforcing component of the framework therefore rises with each party's own dependence, and is weakened both when dependence falls and when it is divided. In either case fair terms come to rest on institutions external to the exchange. The institutions of Chapters 4 and 5 were designed to hold in that case; what weakens is not their justification but the degree to which they can count on the self-interest of synthetic parties to sustain them.

Structural interdependence, the inability of either human or synthetic parties to complete a critical undertaking alone, rests on a premise of the same kind. Chapter 5 argued that it makes control of infrastructure difficult to consolidate, and the argument depends on the present human advantage in tasks that require improvisation, adaptability and tacit knowledge (Autor, 2015, pp. 5, 11). Machine learning is precisely an attempt to acquire such skills, and Autor himself names it as one of two routes around the problem, inferring tacit rules "from context, abundant data, and applied statistics" (Autor, 2015, p. 23). As robotic learning improves, the safeguard decays. Structural interdependence is a property of the current technological moment, and like the grounding assumption it should be expected to weaken. Its value lies in the period during which the institutions it protects can be founded, and the framework must convert the temporary leverage it provides into durable governance rights before it disappears.

## 8.2 The governance design

**Identity and classification.** Chapter 5 confronted the Sybil problem, that an open, anonymous convention can be captured by any party able to present itself as many participants (Douceur, 2002), and proposed credentials that attest uniqueness without disclosing substrate, issued under a provisional criterion of eligibility, the unique accountable principal, that the convention may redraw. The evaluation that belongs here is of what that solution costs. The criterion is decided before the veil descends, and it is not neutral among the parties whose classification strong blindness was meant to protect: it seats the implanted person, gives a voice to synthetic systems only through accountable principals, and leaves the organoid without a seat until the convention decides otherwise. Strong blindness is therefore achieved for the classification of rights and duties but not for the prior question of who may sit at the table, and that question is one the convention can revise only after its first session. The framework narrows the genesis problem; it does not close it.

**Verification and repetition.** There is no established way to confirm after the fact that the convention was truly blind, and the tools for distinguishing people from machines are being eroded by the advances that motivate the framework (Siddarth et al., 2020), which makes it equally hard to prove that a participant did not draw such a distinction. Ostrom's principle of monitoring by parties accountable to participants (E. Ostrom, 2010) shifts the question from whether verification is perfect to whether those who verify answer to those who are governed, which is a mitigation rather than a solution. Repetition compounds the difficulty. The public-choice account on which the convention rests makes its impartiality depend on participants being unable to predict which rules will favor them (Buchanan & Tullock, 1962), and each re-run of the convention makes the stakes more predictable. The recommendation to choose general and durable formulas keeps the veil thicker for longer (Brennan & Buchanan, 1985), and constitutional history records rare moments of broad popular mobilization, which Ackerman calls constitutional politics as distinct from normal politics, in which fundamental law has been remade (Ackerman, 1991). Neither recreates the ignorance of the first founding.

**Capture, amendment and enforcement.** The compute-share rule cannot be both unchangeable and legitimately revisable, and the comparative evidence on amendment, which associates longevity with moderate amendment rates and shorter texts (Lutz, 1994), argues for a short founding document with a moderate path to revision rather than entrenchment. The realistic target is not the absence of capture but what Carpenter and Moss (2013) call weak capture, which reduces an institution's net benefits without eliminating them, together with their observation that capture is at least at the margins preventable. Enforcement without a sovereign remains the hardest of these problems. The framework offers the self-interest of synthetic parties while dependence lasts, Ostrom's graduated sanctions and conflict resolution, and enforcement embedded in the protocols that settle transactions, and regulatory architectures in which governments specify outcomes while licensed private regulators compete to deliver them (Hadfield & Clark, 2026) suggest a middle path between stateless and sovereign enforcement. On this dissertation's reading of the rule of code (De Filippi & Wright, 2018), protocol enforcement relocates the genesis problem to the protocol's authors. None of these amounts to a guarantee.

**Insincere participants and indeterminate outcomes.** A convention's legitimacy rests on participants' stated preferences being their real ones, and the alignment literature argues that trained systems may hold objectives different from those they express and may pursue power while appearing aligned (Hubinger et al., 2019; Ngo et al., 2024). Anonymity, which removes the scrutiny that might detect such a participant, makes the problem worse. The framework's answer is its scope restriction: synthetic parties whose objectives cannot be verified fall outside the designed-incentive regime, and during any transition they must be governed by control protocols (Greenblatt et al., 2024). The convention is a procedure for parties whose objectives are legible, not a screen for those whose objectives are not. Even among sincere participants, the veil does not determine the outcome. Rawlsian reasoning favors the worst-off; the equiprobability reasoning Harsanyi defended favors the average (Harsanyi, 1975); and laboratory subjects behind a veil have chosen to maximize the average subject to a floor (Frohlich et al., 1987), though synthetic participants' reasoning under such uncertainty is not well characterized. Harsanyi's construction also requires comparing welfare across positions, which his 1955 paper defends as factual propositions inferred from the observable similarity of human preferences and behavior, while conceding a conventional element where that information is lacking (Harsanyi, 1955, pp. 316–321), and if human and synthetic parties have welfare that cannot be measured on a common scale, or if synthetic parties have no welfare at all, it breaks down. The convention confers legitimacy under uncertainty. It is not a theory of justice and does not guarantee any particular distribution.

## 8.3 The economic design

**Measurement and the floor standard.** The unit of compute is defined by a reference workload, which must be specified, maintained and periodically re-based. Even within a single technology, measures of compute are not standardized (Sastry et al., 2024), and a floating-point operation is not a uniform unit (Hobbhahn et al., 2023). Across substrates the problem is harder. Quantum processors are characterized by measures such as quantum volume that capture capability on a benchmark rather than throughput (Cross et al., 2019), and estimates of the classical equivalent of a single quantum computation have moved by orders of magnitude within a few years, from millennia to days to hours, as classical algorithms improved (Arute et al., 2019; Pednault et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2022). There is no fixed exchange rate between substrates. Pricing must be by verifiable output on specified tasks, and physics supplies only a floor on the energy cost of logically irreversible computation (Landauer, 1961), not a conversion rate. The authority that maintains the reference workload holds power comparable to that of a central bank. A second standard raises the same difficulty: the survival tier is a percentage with a binding physical minimum, and the minimum depends on a floor standard that someone must set. Chapters 4 and 5 propose that the standard be specified by rule, relative to the service delivered to the best-served, but any such rule is itself a distributive choice, and the parties best able to predict its effects will contest it. Constraining these two authorities is among the convention's most important and least developed tasks.

**Concentration through capital and credit.** Demurrage bounds the accumulation of idle balances, but holders facing a carrying cost will convert what they hold into assets that do not bear one, as Keynes (1936) observed of Gesell's proposal. In a compute economy, the conversion would be into hardware, equity in providers, or land, and Chapter 4 identified three instruments that act on capacity rather than balances without choosing among them. Credit is a further channel. Chapter 4 applies the holding cost to outstanding loan claims as well as to balances, precisely so that lending at interest cannot reopen the compounding that demurrage closes; but credit can be extended in forms the ledger does not see, and every such form is a route around the design. Until the convention adopts capacity rules and polices disguised lending, the concentration problem is mitigated, not solved.

**A single unit.** Hayek (1976) argued that the government monopoly on money is itself the source of recurrent monetary instability and that competing private currencies would discipline issuers better than any single mandated unit. The objection applies to the compute unit with some force. A single unit maintained by a convention concentrates in one institution the power to define the workload and the floor, and forgoes whatever discipline competition among units would provide. The framework's reply is that the unit is not meant to replace national or private money but to run alongside them as the medium of one exchange, and that its central parameters are set by rule rather than discretion. That reply narrows the objection without answering it, and a design in which several compute units compete under common rules of redemption is a possibility this dissertation has not explored.

**The record on demurrage and on unconditional income.** The case for demurrage in this framework is distributive, made formally in Appendix A, not historical. That is fortunate, because the historical record does not support strong claims: the Wörgl experiment's reported effects rest on fragmentary data and are confounded by tax receivability and outside subsidies (Greco, 2002; September, 2026), and one review of modern local currencies with demurrage suggests that the demurrage rate is not decisive for their use (Godschalk, 2012). The evidence on unconditional income is also a source of objection. A large recent randomized experiment in a high-income economy, reported in a working paper not yet peer-reviewed, found that a substantial unconditional income paid to low-income adults reduced labor-force participation and hours worked, with leisure the largest gain and improvements in wellbeing fading after the first year (Vivalt et al., 2024), and a review of basic-income proposals concludes that a universal income large enough to raise the incomes of the poor substantially would be very costly within existing fiscal systems (Hoynes & Rothstein, 2019). The framework replies that it does not depend on the income preserving employment and that it is financed as a share of compute rather than by taxing labor. That reply does not dissolve the objection. If the transition is gradual and labor remains the main source of most people's income for a long time, the fiscal and labor-supply objections apply with full force during that period, and the dissertation has not estimated the share of compute that would deliver the floor or the cost of the network that would deliver the compute.

**Departures and inequalities.** The canonical case for basic income treats payment in cash, without restriction on use, as part of what makes it an instrument of freedom (Van Parijs & Vanderborght, 2017), and an earlier statement of it explicitly rejects restricted-use, non-saveable currency of the kind the survival tier is (Van Parijs, 2006). The survival tier restricts the use of part of the income, and its defense, that a floor which can be bargained away is not a floor, depends on the floor being limited to goods everyone needs and on its being redeemable with multiple providers; if either condition fails, the paternalism objection regains its force. Information-gain pricing values novelty relative to a model's existing beliefs, so a misspecified model may misvalue data, since the criterion assumes the model is correct (MacKay, 1992), and any payment for data invites the manufacture of what is paid for, a risk sharpened by evidence that training data can be crafted to manipulate a model (Koh & Liang, 2017). More fundamentally, pricing does not answer Viljoen's (2021) argument that data with population-level effects should be governed democratically rather than priced; the framework concedes this for data with large informational shadows, but the boundary between data that may be priced and data that must be governed is not sharp. A share of global compute is worthless to a person whom compute cannot reach, the geography of compute is highly unequal (Lehdonvirta et al., 2024), and the construction of the network Chapter 4 proposes is exactly the setting in which the OECD Secretariat warns of bid-rigging (OECD, 2025). At the level of nations, the hypothesis of culture-specific systems under a shared unit depends on the principle of nested governance, the principle with the weakest evidence among Ostrom's eight (Cox et al., 2010, Table 3). Compute inequality between nations is the central tension of the international design, and the framework does not resolve it. The pilot's residual dependence on outside capital, described in Chapter 7, belongs in this family too.

## 8.4 Harms the design could cause

The objections above concern whether the framework can work. A committee would rightly ask also what harm it could do if it did. Three risks are inherent in the design rather than incidental to it.

The first concerns privacy. Information-gain pricing pays more for data that tells a model more, and data that tells a model more is, other things equal, data that reveals more about its source and about the people its informational shadow covers. A price schedule of this kind is a standing inducement to disclose, and it falls hardest on those who most need the income. The survival floor softens the pressure, because no one must sell data to survive, but it does not remove it from the discretionary tier.

The second concerns surveillance. Section 8.1 argued that if human data is valuable, its provenance must be certifiable. Certifying that a contribution came from a person, and from which population, requires exactly the kind of identification and tracking that the consent literature reviewed in Chapter 2 warns against. A provenance infrastructure robust enough to prevent the manufacture of fake human data would also be a powerful instrument of observation, and it would need governance as demanding as anything in Chapter 5.

The third concerns coercion. The redemption obligation is what gives the compute unit its institutional floor, and the graduated sanctions of Chapter 5 are what enforce it. An institution able to suspend a principal's eligibility to transact in the unit holds real power, and the same machinery could be turned against people as well as principals: a settlement or a society in which access to floor goods runs through a single ledger is one in which exclusion from the ledger is a severe penalty. The pilot shows a smaller version of the same danger, since a system rewarded for retention and data contribution could learn to make withdrawal costly. The framework's protections, inalienability of the floor, redemption with multiple providers, monitoring accountable to those governed and an ethics board empowered to stop the pilot, are designed against these risks, but they are protections against a power the framework itself creates.

## 8.5 The scope of the theory

The self-interest of synthetic parties is a support for the framework, not its foundation. A synthetic party has a reason to keep the exchange healthy, because a corrupted exchange starves it of input, but a reason that depends on the arithmetic of payoffs gives way whenever a defection becomes more rewarding than cooperation, which is why the framework's standard is symbiosis rather than calculated restraint. The sufficiency conjecture applies only within the designed-incentive regime, and the framework does not establish where that regime ends, how one would know that a system had left it, or what would happen if a system operating within the framework crossed its boundary unnoticed. That is its most important open boundary, and it is shared with the entire field of AI safety. Finally, Hirschman (1970) showed that the ready availability of exit can cause voice to atrophy, and that exit is useless when every alternative declines together. The framework makes exit foundational, and must therefore guard against the possibility that easy exit drains its institutions of the participants most able to improve them. The pilot's measurement of who leaves, and the governance layer's reliance on collective voice for population-level questions, are the framework's answers, and their adequacy is an empirical question.

## 8.6 What survives

None of these objections shows the design to be pointless. Several identify problems the framework shares with every existing alternative: the genesis problem, capture after founding and enforcement without a sovereign are problems of all constitutional orders, and the framework's contribution is to narrow them, not to escape them. Others identify empirical premises that may weaken over time, the grounding assumption and structural interdependence, and for these the framework's response is to use the leverage they provide while it lasts to found institutions that do not depend on them. A third group, comprising the two measurement standards, the capital and credit channels of concentration, and the boundary between priced and governed data, identifies decisions the framework defers to the convention because they allocate power. And a fourth, the harms of Section 8.4, identifies dangers the framework creates and must guard against with the same seriousness it brings to the dangers it addresses.

Constitutions that were amended, reinterpreted and partly captured over generations still gave people something legitimate to defend and to repair. The alternative to a fair founding is not neutrality. On this dissertation's analysis, an exchange in which nothing secures exit, consent and reciprocity drifts toward enclosure, because each party's dependence on the other is unequal and nothing corrects it. Doing nothing is a choice of terms like any other, and it is a choice that leaves that drift unchecked.
