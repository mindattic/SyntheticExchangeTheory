# Chapter 6: The Transition Problem

A fair design does not adopt itself. The institutions proposed in Chapters 4 and 5 would redistribute bargaining power away from those who currently hold it, and those who currently hold it have no reason to accept them voluntarily. This chapter therefore asks a question that designers of ideal institutions often leave to others: under what circumstances might such an arrangement actually come into being?

The chapter's answer is conditional, and it is meant to discipline the rest of the dissertation rather than to advertise it. Large-scale institutional redesigns have usually followed the failure of the order they replaced, though not always, and not always in the direction of greater fairness. The chapter describes three slow-building pressures that could remove the slack from the present order, identifies a specific and already visible candidate for the kind of acute shock that has historically opened the way to redesign, and then confronts the possibility that such a shock would produce something worse than the present order rather than something better. It closes by stating what the chapter does and does not establish.

## 6.1 Crisis and institutional change

The proposition that fundamental change requires crisis has distinguished advocates across the political spectrum. Milton Friedman wrote that only a crisis, actual or perceived, produces real change, and that when it occurs, the actions taken depend on the ideas that are lying around (Friedman, 1962/2002, p. xiv). Karl Polanyi argued that the attempt to treat labor, land and money as commodities in a self-regulating market produced dislocations that ended in the collapse of the nineteenth-century liberal order, and that the 1930s and 1940s brought a "great transformation" in which fascism, socialism and the New Deal emerged as rival replacements for it (Polanyi, 1944/2001, pp. 3, 24, 31, 71–80). Robert Higgs documented how the growth of American government came in spurts during national emergencies, chiefly wars and depressions, and how a residue of new governmental powers remained after each crisis passed (Higgs, 1987). Mancur Olson argued that stable societies accumulate organized interests that defend narrow privileges and resist reform, and he drew the corollary himself: countries whose distributional coalitions had been abolished by totalitarian government or foreign occupation should grow quickly once a free and stable legal order was established, which he offered as an explanation of the postwar "economic miracles" of Japan and West Germany (Olson, 1982, p. 75). And Walter Scheidel's survey of the history of inequality concludes, soberly, that large reductions in inequality have come mainly from violent shocks rather than from peaceful reform (Scheidel, 2017).

The canonical examples are familiar. The Great Depression preceded the New Deal. The Second World War preceded the Bretton Woods institutions, whose design was negotiated under wartime conditions and reflected the power of the United States as much as any shared lesson from the preceding catastrophe (Steil, 2013).

Political science supplies a more careful vocabulary. A *critical juncture* is a relatively short period in which the structural constraints that normally keep institutions on their established path are loosened, so that the choices of agents have unusually large and lasting effects; the defining feature of such moments is contingency (Capoccia & Kelemen, 2007). Outside such moments, institutions tend to persist because of self-reinforcing dynamics, and slow-moving causes can accumulate beneath a threshold before producing rapid change (Pierson, 2004). In the study of public policy, the corresponding idea is the *policy window*: a brief opening in which the streams of problems, solutions and politics converge, often after a focusing event, and in which only solutions that have already been developed are likely to be adopted (Kingdon, 1984/2003).

Two qualifications must accompany this literature. First, crisis is not the only path to fundamental change. Significant institutional transformation also happens gradually, through incremental but cumulatively transformative processes such as layering, in which new elements added to existing institutions gradually alter their status and structure; drift, in which institutions that are not maintained as their environment changes slip in practice; and conversion, the redeployment of old institutions to new purposes (Streeck & Thelen, 2005, pp. 19–31). The claim of this chapter is therefore not that the framework could only be adopted after a collapse, but that a comprehensive redesign of the kind it proposes has most often followed one. Elements of the framework could be layered onto existing institutions gradually, and some already have been: the European requirement that switching between cloud providers become free of charge (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) is a step toward the exit condition taken without any crisis at all. Second, critical junctures are contingent. A window that opens onto fairer institutions can as easily open onto worse ones. Section 6.5 takes up this second qualification at length.

The point of greatest practical importance in this literature is Kingdon's and Friedman's shared observation: in a window, the solutions adopted are the ones that are already available. A fair design that has not been worked out before a crisis will not be invented during it. That is the strongest justification for doing the work of Chapters 3 to 5 now.

## 6.2 Slow strain: the removal of slack

Complex systems rarely fail from a single cause. Studies of failure in complex systems emphasize that catastrophe requires multiple small failures to combine, that such systems always contain latent failures and run continuously in a degraded mode, and that they keep functioning because of their many redundancies and the continual adaptations of the people who operate them (Cook, 1998). The inference that strain works by gradually consuming those redundancies and that reserve capacity is this dissertation's. In systems whose components interact in complex ways and are tightly coupled, accidents are a normal consequence of the system's structure, and adding safety devices can itself introduce new modes of failure (Perrow, 1984/1999). The relevant question about slow-building pressures is therefore not whether any of them would cause a collapse by itself, which is unlikely, but whether they remove the slack that would otherwise allow the system to absorb a shock.

Three such pressures are visible in the present.

**Automation and employment.** The evidence on automation and employment is mixed. Industrial robots have reduced employment and wages in the American local labor markets most exposed to them (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020). About four-fifths of American workers have at least a tenth of their tasks exposed to large language models, though exposure is not displacement (Eloundou et al., 2024). In at least one large field setting, an AI assistant raised the productivity of customer-support workers substantially, with the greatest gains for the least experienced, complementing rather than replacing them (Brynjolfsson, Li, et al., 2025). And the long historical record suggests that commentators routinely overstate the substitution of machines for labor and neglect the ways in which automation complements it (Autor, 2015). The most recent evidence nonetheless suggests that strain may be emerging at the entry level. A working paper first circulated in 2025 and revised in 2026, drawing on payroll data through mid-2026, finds no economy-wide displacement, but finds that employment of workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five in the occupations most exposed to AI has fallen to about nineteen percent below the trend for comparable workers in less exposed occupations, with the adjustment occurring through reduced hiring rather than layoffs (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, et al., 2025). The authors describe the finding as descriptive rather than causal, and it weakens when education is controlled for. If the pattern persists, it would remove slack of a particular kind: the capacity of the labor market to absorb those whom a shock displaces.

**Competition for physical resources.** The strain on physical resources is better documented. Data centers consumed roughly one and a half percent of global electricity in 2024, their consumption has grown by about twelve percent a year since 2017, and it is projected to more than double by 2030, with data centers accounting for nearly half of the growth in electricity demand in the United States over the same period (International Energy Agency, 2025). In the United States, data-center electricity use rose from about sixty terawatt-hours in the middle of the last decade to about 176 terawatt-hours in 2023, some 4.4 percent of national consumption, and is projected to reach between roughly seven and twelve percent by 2028 (Shehabi et al., 2024). Modeling of the water used to cool data centers and to generate the electricity they consume projects that global AI demand could account for several billion cubic meters of water withdrawal annually within a few years, though the estimate depends on projections of AI electricity demand and on assumed water-intensity factors (Li et al., 2025). Chapter 4 described the consequences for consumer hardware: manufacturers of the memory on which both data centers and personal devices depend have redirected capacity toward data-center customers (Micron Technology, 2025; TrendForce, 2026a). Competition between artificial intelligence and human needs for power, water and hardware does not itself constitute a crisis. It reduces the margin by which a society can absorb one.

**Strategic competition.** The third pressure is geopolitical. A small number of firms manufacture the advanced chips on which frontier systems depend, and their output flows to the buyers who pay most, while governments in a growing number of countries fund sovereign AI capabilities of their own (Chavez et al., 2026). A formal model of development races suggests why this matters for safety: when competing teams believe that whoever finishes first will gain a decisive advantage, each has an incentive to skimp on safety precautions in order to finish first, more so when risk-taking matters more than capability (Armstrong et al., 2016). Strategic competition thus erodes slack in a distinctive way. It reduces the willingness of every actor to accept the costs of caution, including the cost of the independent verification whose absence is the subject of the next section.

## 6.3 A candidate trigger: the correlated-patch catastrophe

Slow strain explains why a system might become fragile. It does not explain what would break it. Historically, the transformative moments described in Section 6.1 combined accumulated strain with a discrete, dateable shock. This section describes a specific candidate for such a shock in an economy increasingly mediated by artificial intelligence. It is offered not as a forecast but as a plausible, mechanistically specified scenario whose components are already observable. Other candidates exist, among them a scandal over data extraction severe enough to break public trust in AI developers all at once. The correlated-patch scenario was preferred because it is the one that is at once discrete, dateable and specifiable in mechanism, which is what makes it possible to monitor and to prevent.

The scenario concerns the maintenance of software security. Security teams increasingly use systems built on large language models both to find vulnerabilities and to write the code that fixes them. In the workflow that concerns this chapter, the same class of system then re-scans the patched code, declares the vulnerability resolved, and the change is deployed without independent testing. The verification step that the software development lifecycle was built around has been replaced by a check performed by a system of the same kind as the one that wrote the fix. The author has observed this workflow in professional practice, and AI-driven remediation is being promoted at scale: a 2026 practitioner research note reports that frontier developers have launched programs of AI-driven vulnerability discovery, that remediation of their findings is being routed through a small number of commercial clearinghouses, and warns of the concentration risk this creates (Cloud Security Alliance AI Safety Initiative, 2026). How widespread the fully unverified version of the workflow has become is not documented in any systematic survey, and this chapter does not claim that it is standard practice. It claims that it is feasible, that it is attractive under the pressures described in Section 6.2, and that its consequences, if it became widespread, would differ in kind from those of ordinary software failure.

The scenario rests on four premises, each of which can be examined separately.

**Premise 1: AI-generated fixes are often wrong in ways that look right.** The evidence for this premise is substantial. In a large-scale preprint study of AI-generated changes to resolve ordinary software issues, a standalone language model introduced roughly nine to eleven times as many new vulnerabilities as human developers did when resolving the same issues, and of three agent frameworks studied, the one given the most autonomy introduced by far the most (Sajadi et al., 2025). In an industry study of patches written by two frontier models for six serious real-world vulnerabilities, only about a quarter of more than six thousand patches fixed the vulnerability cleanly, and about half left at least one exploit path open; the authors named the pattern of patches that have the shape of a fix while leaving the flaw in place "fix-like artifacts with embedded defects," and concluded that the expected value of an unreviewed AI-generated security patch was negative (Mierczuk et al., 2026). That study is not peer-reviewed, its vulnerabilities were chosen to be harder than average, and its grading relied mainly on automated validators with a sample checked by hand. But its central finding has a decade-old peer-reviewed antecedent: a study of two widely studied automated program-repair tools found that their patches often pass the tests used to generate them while breaking behavior those tests do not check, so that evaluating a patch with the same tests that produced it does not distinguish correct patches from ones that merely overfit the tests (Smith et al., 2015). Peer-reviewed studies of neural vulnerability repair have found low rates of successful repair, with some categories of vulnerability missed by every model tested (Y. Wu et al., 2023), and even functionally correct code generated by leading models is frequently exploitable (Vero et al., 2025). Earlier work found that about forty percent of programs generated by a widely used coding assistant in security-relevant scenarios were vulnerable (Pearce et al., 2022), and that developers using an AI assistant wrote less secure code while being more confident that it was secure (Perry et al., 2023).

**Premise 2: The errors of different models are correlated.** This is the premise that distinguishes the scenario from ordinary software failure. An analysis of more than three hundred and fifty language models found that, on one leaderboard dataset, when two models both answer a question incorrectly they give the same wrong answer about sixty percent of the time, and that error correlation is higher among larger and more accurate models, even across different architectures and providers (Kim et al., 2025). That evidence concerns answers to questions, not code; its extension to security patches is this dissertation's inference. The broader point was anticipated in an early technical report on foundation models, which warned that because many systems are adapted from a few base models, the defects of a foundation model are inherited by everything built on it (Bommasani et al., 2021), and in a formal model showing that when many decision-makers adopt the same algorithm, overall decision quality can fall even if the algorithm is individually more accurate than the alternatives it replaces (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021). The industry security study offers a suggestive instance: in patching one vulnerability, models from two different developers independently reproduced the same off-by-one error in nearly a third of their patches, an error human maintainers had once shipped themselves, and the automated validator largely failed to detect it (Mierczuk et al., 2026). Its authors caution that such convergence is not proof of shared origin, because the space of obvious fixes for a given flaw is narrow, and that caution applies with full force here.

**Premise 3: Verification by the same class of system does not catch correlated errors.** If the errors of different models are correlated, a model used to check another model's work will tend to share its blind spots. The analysis of correlated errors makes this point explicitly about the use of language models as judges of other models' outputs (Kim et al., 2025). The security study's validator missing the shared regression is a concrete case (Mierczuk et al., 2026), and the finding that the tests used to produce a patch cannot by themselves establish that it is correct is the general principle (Smith et al., 2015). A natural response is to validate AI-generated fixes with further AI agents, one proposing a fix, another testing it, a third confirming it. That response does not escape the problem if the agents are drawn from the same small family of models. Multiplying verifiers whose errors are correlated does not multiply verification.

**Premise 4: Simultaneous deployment converts a shared defect into a systemic event.** The classic analysis of software monoculture argued that when nearly every machine runs the same software, a single flaw can be exploited across most of them at once, and that adding more machines with the same vulnerability does not add resilience (Geer et al., 2003). Because the security benefits of diversity accrue to the network as a whole while each adopter has reasons, such as the ready availability of support for popular products, to choose what everyone else uses, computing tends toward monoculture, a condition one analysis called a tragedy of the security commons (Stamp, 2004). Two recent events show the mechanism at scale. In July 2024, a single faulty content update to a widely deployed security product, pushed without staged rollout, affected roughly 8.5 million Windows devices worldwide; the vendor's remedies were to restore staged deployment, additional validation and independent review (CrowdStrike, 2024; Weston, 2024). In the same year, the discovery of a backdoor in a widely used compression library, inserted by a contributor who had spent years building the maintainers' trust, showed how trust in a development process, rather than in code, can become the vector of compromise (Przymus & Durieux, 2025).

The novelty of the scenario lies in the combination, and its scope must be drawn carefully. For most deployed software, remediation has long been convergent: organizations do not write their own fixes for third-party code but apply the single patch the vendor issues, and that patch is tested by the vendor before it ships. What is new concerns code that organizations maintain themselves, and the forks and configurations they patch on their own. In the world before AI-assisted remediation, a hundred organizations remediating the same class of flaw in their own code would write a hundred different fixes. Many would be poor, but their failures would be independent, and some would be robust. Diversity of remediation was itself a defense. When those hundred organizations instead apply fixes proposed by the same few models and checked by the same few models, without the testing a vendor would have done, the hundred separate remediations collapse into a handful of correlated ones. This dissertation calls the process *convergent remediation*. The defenders have not diversified their defense; they have standardized the attack surface, at the very moment they believed they were hardening it. An adversary who finds the flaw in one convergent fix has found it in all of them.

A further variant should be named. Everything above concerns honest error. If a widely used model were itself compromised, through poisoned training data or a backdoor of the kind demonstrated in the compression-library case, convergent remediation would distribute the compromise with the same efficiency. The intent-neutral version of the scenario requires no adversary inside the pipeline; the deliberate version requires only one.

**Counterarguments.** The scenario must be weighed against serious objections. Standardization has real security benefits: a single, well-maintained configuration is easier to defend than many poorly maintained ones, and monoculture combined with automated diversification can be a sound strategy (Birman & Schneider, 2009). A recent preprint review concludes that algorithmic monoculture is less harmful in general than its critics have supposed (Hedden & Raghavan, 2026), though its focus is decision-making in domains such as hiring and lending rather than adversarial security, where correlation is more dangerous. Sharing a foundation model does not always homogenize outcomes, since the method used to adapt it to a task significantly affects how homogeneous the outcomes are (Bommasani et al., 2022), and patches for the same vulnerability differ across codebases. Organizations deploy patches on different schedules, which limits simultaneity; the 2024 outage resulted from a single vendor's push, a different mechanism from many organizations independently applying similar fixes. Finally, correlated IT failures have so far been recoverable. The United Kingdom's financial regulator found that consumer harm from the 2024 outage was minimal (Financial Conduct Authority, 2024). These objections do not refute the scenario, but they establish its proper status: a candidate trigger, specified precisely enough to be monitored and prevented, not a prediction.

## 6.4 How the pieces could combine

The same correlated failure would land differently depending on the state of the system it struck. In an economy with robust employment, abundant compute and functioning institutions, a widespread security failure caused by convergent remediation would be costly and embarrassing, a hard week for the people who keep systems running, and then it would pass. In an economy already strained by entry-level displacement, by competition between AI infrastructure and human needs for power and water, and by a strategic race that has eroded every actor's willingness to pay for caution, the same event could be the kind of discrete, attributable failure that has historically made societies willing to renegotiate their basic economic arrangements.

This layered model, slow strain plus a discrete trigger, is the shape that great transitions have usually taken. The stock-market crash of 1929 became the Great Depression through a sequence of further failures, including waves of banking panics and a severe contraction of the money stock, that turned a financial shock into an economic collapse (Friedman & Schwartz, 1963; see also Bernanke, 2002). The causal claim is never that one event is sufficient. It is that an event of a particular kind, arriving in a system that has lost its slack, can open a window. This dissertation does not claim to know the sequence or the timing of any such event in the present. It claims only that the shape is familiar, and that an economy built on artificial intelligence and human data has a specific, already visible candidate for the trigger.

## 6.5 The darker branch

Every account of crisis as an opportunity for reform must confront the evidence that crises are equally opportunities for something worse. Any argument from crisis must answer it.

The evidence that economic crises can push societies toward extremism, rather than toward fairer institutions, is strong. A study of more than eight hundred elections in twenty advanced economies over nearly a century and a half found that after financial crises, government majorities shrink, polarization increases, and the vote share of the far right rises on average by about thirty percent, effects not observed after ordinary recessions (Funke et al., 2016). Analysis of the interwar period found that economic hardship, and particularly cumulative economic decline, was associated with votes for right-wing extremist parties (the link was much weaker for Communist parties), most strongly in countries with short democratic histories and low barriers to parliamentary representation (de Bromhead et al., 2013). The same historical literature that credits crises with the New Deal and Bretton Woods credits them with fascism. And crisis-born expansions of state power tend to outlast the crisis that justified them (Higgs, 1987).

A crisis in an economy built on artificial intelligence would carry a specific authoritarian risk, and it follows directly from the properties that make compute attractive as a basis for governance. Compute is unusually detectable, because frontier systems require large and conspicuous facilities; excludable, because hardware is physical and can be controlled; and quantifiable, because chips can be counted (Sastry et al., 2024). Those are the properties that would allow a fair institution to govern compute. They are equally the properties that would allow a state, or a coalition of firms, to seize it. A crisis in which the security of the world's software had visibly failed would supply a powerful justification for exactly such a seizure, in the name of restoring order. The darker branch of the transition is therefore not generic authoritarianism. It is the concentration of control over compute in the hands of whoever claims the emergency.

What can a design proposed in advance do about this? It cannot prevent a seizure. It can make the fair alternative available, specified and tested, so that it is among the ideas lying around when the window opens. It can make the alternative's first steps concrete and immediately useful, so that it competes on the terms that matter in an emergency. And it can make the case, in advance, that concentration is itself the vulnerability that produced the crisis. A correlated-patch catastrophe is a failure of monoculture, and the monoculture that matters is in verification, not merely in suppliers: Premise 2 suggests that errors can be correlated across providers, so that diversity of providers alone is not a remedy. What the framework's emphasis on distributed control, portability and polycentric governance supplies is the institutional ground for diversity where it counts, in independent testing by parties who do not share the same tools and the same blind spots. A response that concentrates control of compute further deepens the very monoculture that failed.

## 6.6 What happens to people during the transition

The second unanswered objection concerns the people who would live through a transition. Institutional designs typically describe their end state and are silent about the passage to it, and the passage is where the harm is greatest.

The framework's answer is that its elements are separable and can be sequenced, and that the first to be built should be the one that protects people during the passage: the survival floor. A guaranteed provision of the floor goods defined in Chapter 3, power, water, food, shelter, basic communication and emergency care, does not depend on the founding of a global convention or the adoption of a new currency. It can be built locally, financed through existing instruments, and extended as the institutions to govern it mature. The rural electrification program of the 1930s, which Chapter 4 discussed, offers a model: it was not part of a comprehensive redesign of the American economy but a targeted program, delivered through cooperatives and public credit, that extended a basic service to people the market had left behind (Kitchens & Fishback, 2015; Lewis & Severnini, 2020). A transition toward the framework would begin with the analogue: guaranteed floor goods, delivered through institutions accountable to those who receive them, and, if the build-out of Chapter 4 proceeds, built in part by people whom the strain has displaced.

The second element of the transition is containment. Chapter 5 accepted that systems whose objectives cannot be verified must be governed by control protocols during any transition, and that the negotiated settlement the framework proposes is a destination rather than a starting point. The third element is the pilot of Chapter 7, which exists to show, at small scale and before any crisis, whether the mechanism works. A design that has been tested is more likely to be adopted in a window than one that has not, and a design that has failed its test is one that should not be adopted at all.

## 6.7 What this chapter does and does not establish

Describing a transition does not make it more likely. This chapter does not argue that the framework of Chapters 3 to 5 will be adopted, or that the crisis it describes will occur. It sets the dissertation's own standard for honesty about adoption. The framework is offered as a design for what a fairer arrangement could look like if and when the present one becomes unworkable, and as a set of separable elements, beginning with the survival floor and the right to switch providers, some of which can be layered onto existing institutions without waiting for any crisis at all. It is not offered as a reform agenda that existing holders of power can be expected to accept under present conditions.

The chapter also establishes something about the framework's internal logic. The trigger it identifies is a failure of correlation, and the authoritarian response it fears is a further concentration. The remedy the framework proposes for both is the same: independent verification, portability of records and services, distributed control over compute, and institutions that no single party can capture. The case for Synthetic Exchange Theory does not rest on a crisis. But the crisis this chapter describes would be a failure of correlation, and the independent verification and distributed control that answer it are also among the framework's first steps.
