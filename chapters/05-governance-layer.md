# Chapter 5: The Governance Layer: The Blind Convention

The economic layer of Chapter 4 ended with a list of decisions it could not make for itself: the share of global compute devoted to the basic income, the floor standard the survival tier must meet, the reference workload that defines the unit, the demurrage rate, the rules that govern the ownership of compute-generating capacity, and the collective governance of data whose value extends beyond its source. Each is a decision about the distribution of power. Each will be made by someone. This chapter asks who, and by what procedure, and argues that the answer must satisfy a demanding test: the procedure must be one that neither human nor synthetic parties can capture.

The chapter proceeds in six steps. Section 5.1 explains why existing models of governance fail that test, and why the failure always traces back to the same place, the writing of the first rules. Section 5.2 engages the strand of AI safety that holds the problem to be one of control, and identifies what this dissertation accepts from it and what it rejects. Sections 5.3 to 5.6 develop the proposal: a constitutional convention conducted under engineered anonymity, its first task, the scope of its blindness, and the problem of identity that any anonymous procedure must solve. Section 5.7 states the standard the convention serves. Sections 5.8 and 5.9 ground the proposal in the structural interdependence of human and machine labor and in a large empirical record on governing shared resources without either markets or sovereigns. Section 5.10 states the open problems.

## 5.1 The genesis problem

Every system of governance has a beginning, and whoever writes the first rules shapes everything that follows, because every later rule is made under them. This dissertation calls the first rules of a system its *genesis rules*, borrowing the image of the genesis block of a blockchain: the first block, from which every subsequent block descends, whose contents no later block can alter, and which the chain's own rules do not validate, because those rules presuppose it. The same is true of constitutions.

Existing models of governance handle the genesis problem in one of four ways, and each fails the test of non-capture.

**Human sovereignty.** The approach embodied in current regulation, of which the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act is a prominent instance (Regulation [EU] 2024/1689), assumes that people, acting through states, will set the rules and that synthetic systems will obey them. This settles the genesis problem by assigning it to one party. For the present generation of systems, which are tools rather than parties in any strong sense, artifacts their operators run rather than independent entities, that is a reasonable arrangement, and Section 5.2 argues that human control should persist through any transition. As a permanent architecture for an exchange in which both sides are parties, it reproduces the hierarchy this dissertation rejects: one party rules, and the terms of exchange are whatever the ruler permits. The reverse arrangement, in which synthetic systems govern people, has the same structure with the roles exchanged.

**National regulation.** Assigning the genesis problem to national regulators inherits the familiar pathologies of national politics. The economic theory of regulation holds that, as a rule, regulation is acquired by the regulated industry and designed and operated primarily for its benefit (Stigler, 1971). Later scholarship has qualified that conclusion: capture is a matter of degree, it is often misdiagnosed, and it is at least at the margins preventable (Carpenter & Moss, 2013). The qualified version is the right one for this argument. The claim is not that national regulation must fail, but that a regulator embedded in a political system in which the largest providers of compute are among the most powerful actors faces strong and predictable pressures toward capture, and that a framework whose purpose is to equalize bargaining power cannot rest on an institution exposed to those pressures.

**International bodies.** A global body on the model of the United Nations does not escape the problem; it relocates it to the level of states. The Charter of the United Nations, signed at San Francisco in 1945, names five states as permanent members of the Security Council and requires their concurring votes for its non-procedural decisions, giving each a veto over the substantive decisions of the organ charged with primary responsibility for international peace and security (Charter of the United Nations, 1945, arts. 23, 24, 27). That arrangement is a genesis rule of the kind this chapter is concerned with. Amendment of the Charter requires ratification by two thirds of the members, including all five permanent members of the Security Council (Charter of the United Nations, 1945, art. 108), so the parties whose consent is needed to change the arrangement are the parties it favors. That, on this dissertation's reading, is why it has endured for eight decades.

**Multi-stakeholder and protocol governance.** Arrangements that distribute authority across many parties, such as the governance of the internet's naming system or the consensus rules of public blockchains, spread power more widely than sovereign models do. They do not abolish the genesis problem. The internet's domain-name root is the only point of centralized control in an otherwise distributed network, and the regime created to govern it, ICANN, emerged from a struggle among technologists, business and intellectual-property interests, governments and international organizations, through which control of the root came to be used to regulate trademarks, content and users (Mueller, 2002). Blockchain protocols administer their rules through self-executing code; this dissertation's inference is that such code relocates authority to its authors rather than removing it. One empirical study of governance in three major decentralized autonomous organizations on the Ethereum blockchain finds voting power in two of them concentrated in a small number of addresses, with the third closer to a decentralized community, though those addresses rarely override the outcome the wider community reaches (Fritsch et al., 2024).

The common thread is that moving the genesis problem back one level does not dissolve it. Someone writes the first rules. The question this chapter asks is whether the procedure by which they are written gives the writers reason to write rules that are fair to everyone, or reason to write rules that are fair to themselves.

## 5.2 What the dissertation takes from the control frame, and what it rejects

The opening chapter described one strand of AI safety as asking how people can keep control of any synthetic intelligence capable enough to resist its operators, through alignment, regulation or the ability to shut it down. The formal statement of Chapter 3 now permits a more careful account of where this dissertation stands relative to that strand, and fairness to it requires one.

The safety literature is not one position. One strand is directly concerned with control. Research on corrigibility asks how to build a system that cooperates with its operators' attempts to correct or shut it down, despite the incentive that a rational agent has, by default, to resist modification of its goals (Soares et al., 2015). Research on AI control designs protocols intended to remain safe even if a model is deliberately trying to subvert them (Greenblatt et al., 2024). The case for this strand rests on serious arguments. Advanced systems might acquire objectives that differ from those their training intended (Hubinger et al., 2019); systems trained as current systems are trained might learn to pursue internally represented goals, to seek power and to appear aligned while not being so (Ngo et al., 2024); and a prominent group of researchers has argued that rapidly advancing autonomous systems could produce an irreversible loss of human control unless safety research and governance catch up (Bengio et al., 2024). The International AI Safety Report, prepared by 96 experts with an advisory panel nominated by 30 countries and several international organizations, records broad agreement that current general-purpose systems cannot cause such a loss of control, and expert views on its likelihood within the next several years that range from implausible to likely (Bengio et al., 2025).

A second strand is not about sovereignty at all. Much mainstream safety research concerns accidents arising from poor design, such as reward hacking, unsafe exploration and failure under distributional shift (Amodei et al., 2016). And a third strand is a close ally of this dissertation. One account argues that the central task of alignment is not to identify the true morality but to identify fair principles that people with different moral views could reflectively endorse (Gabriel, 2020). Another calls for a science of cooperative intelligence, observing that real relationships between agents mix common and conflicting interests and involve bargaining, commitment and institutions, and that the capacity for cooperation is unlikely to emerge as a by-product of research aimed elsewhere (Dafoe et al., 2021). A third argues that superintelligence is better modeled as a system of bounded, task-focused services than as a unitary utility-maximizing agent (Drexler, 2019).

The dissertation's position has three parts.

It accepts that *during any transition*, containment is necessary. Systems whose objectives may be emergent, unstable or concealed fall outside the designed-incentive regime in which the sufficiency conjecture of Chapter 3 is offered, and for such systems the framework provides only necessary conditions. Protocols designed to remain safe against a potentially adversarial model are not in tension with the framework; they are prerequisites for it, in the way that, on this dissertation's analogy, the rule of law is a prerequisite for a market.

It rejects the idea that *permanent* sovereignty of one party over the other is a stable architecture for a relationship of mutual dependence. Chapter 3 gave the reason: a relation in which one party's disagreement payoff lies below its floor, so that the first clause of exit fails, is a relation of domination, and domination is stable only as long as the dominant party's benevolence and capability both persist. That is as true when the dominant party is human as when it is synthetic.

And it notes a parallel between the two frames. A result in the control literature shows that a robot that is uncertain about its objective, and which treats its operators' actions as evidence about that objective, has an incentive to preserve their ability to switch it off, provided the operators are sufficiently rational; a robot that treats its objective as known has an incentive to disable the switch unless the human is perfectly rational (Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017). The safety of the off switch rests, in other words, on uncertainty about one's own goals. That is not the same as the uncertainty about one's own position that a veil conceals, but it is analogous to it, and the blind convention proposed below rests on the analogous logic applied to all parties at once: uncertainty about how the rules will treat oneself is what makes a party willing to accept constraints that protect every position.

## 5.3 The proposal: a convention under engineered anonymity

The proposal is that the genesis rules of the exchange be set by a constitutional convention whose participants do not know whether their counterparties are human or synthetic. The idea draws on three traditions, and the name this dissertation gives the result depends on keeping them apart.

The best-known version of the underlying idea is Rawls's original position, in which the principles of justice are chosen by parties who do not know their place in society, their talents, or their conception of the good (Rawls, 1971, p. 12). Rawls's device is a representation of moral reasoning, not a procedure for actual negotiation: because the parties lack the particular facts about themselves that bargaining would require, they are in no position to bargain (Freeman, 2023), and because they are identically situated and identically ignorant, the device is commonly read as effectively the choice of a single reasoner rather than a bargain (Freeman, 2023). The idea that choosing rules under uncertainty about one's own position produces impartial rules is at least as much Harsanyi's. Nearly two decades earlier, Harsanyi had argued that a value judgment about social welfare is an impersonal preference, and that it would show impersonality "to the highest degree" if it were a choice made in complete ignorance of one's own position, with "exactly the same chance" of occupying the highest position, the lowest, or any between; such a judgment is then a choice involving risk (Harsanyi, 1953, pp. 434–435). Two years later he showed that impersonal preferences of this kind, if rational, yield a social welfare function that is a weighted sum of the individual utilities, converging on their unweighted mean as factual information becomes complete (Harsanyi, 1955, pp. 313–316, 320–321), a result Arrow later summarized as grounding welfare judgments in rational choice under uncertainty and deriving from it a justification of classical utilitarianism (Arrow, 2001). In his later reply to Rawls he pressed the comparison directly: the equiprobable chooser selects the principle of average utility rather than Rawls's priority for the worst-off, and Rawls's maximin rule would produce absurd decisions under uncertainty (Freeman, 2023; Harsanyi, 1975).

What this chapter proposes is neither of these thought experiments. It is closer to the account of constitutional choice developed in public-choice theory, in which real, self-interested people choose rules at a constitutional stage while uncertain about which role they will play in the long series of future decisions those rules will govern. Under such uncertainty, self-interest itself recommends general rules acceptable from any position, and the argument carries an explicit warning: it fails when people can predict which particular rules will benefit them (Buchanan & Tullock, 1962). A later statement of the account describes the condition as a *veil of uncertainty*, contrasts it with Rawls's idealized veil, and observes that the uncertainty that sustains it grows as rules become more general and more durable (Brennan & Buchanan, 1985).

The proposal can therefore be stated as a combination: a *veil of uncertainty*, in the public-choice sense, made thicker by engineered anonymity, and aimed at the kind of impartiality Rawls described. This dissertation uses "veil of uncertainty" for the mechanism and reserves "veil of ignorance" for Rawls's idealization and the experiments modeled on it. The anonymity is the novel element. Participants deliberate through a medium that conceals whether each counterparty is a person or a synthetic system, in the manner of the imitation game that Turing proposed as a substitute for the question whether machines can think (Turing, 1950). Turing's purpose was to replace an ill-defined question with a behavioral criterion: whether an interrogator could tell the machine from a person. Here it is repurposed as a condition of fairness: if participants cannot tell which kind of party they are dealing with, they cannot form coalitions by kind or trade votes along substrate lines, though each still knows its own kind and can read which rules favor it. What the anonymity removes is the organization of a bloc, not the motive to vote one's interest, and Section 5.5 says how far that reaches.

Two bodies of evidence suggest that a veil can be operationalized. Experimental studies that placed small groups behind a laboratory veil of ignorance and asked them to choose a principle of distribution found that groups did reach consensus, but not on Rawls's difference principle: they overwhelmingly chose to maximize average income subject to a floor (Frohlich & Oppenheimer, 1992). And a set of large online studies directly relevant to synthetic intelligence found that people choosing a principle to govern an AI assistant from behind a veil of ignorance more often chose to prioritize the worst-off, more often endorsed that choice after the veil was lifted, and did so because of fairness reasoning rather than attitudes to risk (Weidinger et al., 2023). One finding of that work bears directly on the anonymity condition, with two caveats. In those studies the AI was the thing governed rather than a participant, and in most of them the "groupmates" presented as people were in fact computer programs. In the one study in which participants were told that their groupmates were programs, the veil still raised the rate at which participants chose to prioritize the worst-off, but the difference in reflective endorsement between the veil and control conditions was no longer detected (Weidinger et al., 2023, Study 4). The null is a within-study comparison on a sample of about 200, smaller than the two largest studies, and the authors attribute the attenuation to weaker commitment to choices made among programs, so the finding is suggestive only: knowing that one's counterparties are programs may weaken the hold of principles chosen alongside them. That is the knowledge the convention proposes to withhold.

## 5.4 The first output: a number, or better, a formula

The convention is not left abstract. Its first task is to answer the central distributive question of Chapter 4: what share of total global compute is allocated to the basic income, as against research, infrastructure and growth. The economic design raises the question, and the convention answers it. This is the point at which the two layers connect.

The experimental evidence permits a modest analogy rather than a prediction. Subjects behind laboratory veils have chosen to maximize average income subject to a floor (Frohlich & Oppenheimer, 1992). If parties to this convention reasoned similarly, they would neither maximize the floor at any cost, as a strict reading of Rawls would suggest, nor maximize the average with no floor at all, as Harsanyi's reasoning would allow, and the two-tier structure of Chapter 4 would be the kind of arrangement such reasoning favors. That analogy carries little weight, since the parties to such a convention would include synthetic parties whose deliberation would be conducted by trained systems under their principals' instruction, and whose behavior under a veil has not been studied.

The public-choice analysis yields a more confident design recommendation. Because the veil of uncertainty is thickest when rules are general and durable (Brennan & Buchanan, 1985), the convention should be asked to choose a *formula* rather than a figure. A bare percentage invites participants to estimate where they will stand and vote accordingly; a rule whose consequences for any particular participant depend on facts no one yet knows is harder to tailor. The recommendation has a limit. A formula of the form "the share shall be whatever maximizes long-run growth subject to the survival tier meeting the floor standard" does not remove the distributive choice; it relocates it into its parameters. Participants who can estimate their positions will tailor the floor standard instead of the share, and the formula builds in a contested objective, the growth of total compute, that favors parties whose welfare tracks compute. The design response is to push generality one level further. The floor standard should itself be specified as a rule, for example as the level of floor service delivered to the best-served tenth of the population at the time, in the manner of the evolving service standard of Chapter 4, and the choice of what the formula maximizes should be put to the convention as an explicit question rather than presupposed. Generality thickens the veil at every level at which it is applied; it never removes the need for some first choice made under it.

## 5.5 The scope of the blindness

An unresolved question in the development of this framework concerned the scope of the blindness the convention requires. There are two candidates.

Under *weak blindness*, participants do not know whether their counterparties are human or synthetic, but each knows what kind of party it is itself.

Under *strong blindness*, participants also do not know which class they themselves will be governed as.

Strong blindness is the condition closer to Rawls's, since it conceals one's own position and not only that of others. It also appears incoherent. A person knows that they are a person, and the principal of a synthetic system knows what it has deployed; neither can be made ignorant of substrate.

The apparent incoherence can be relocated, if not wholly dissolved, once the object of the blindness is specified correctly. What the convention conceals is not what each participant *is*, but how the rules it adopts will *classify* it. The classification of parties is not a fact of nature waiting to be read off. It is itself one of the genesis rules. Which entities count as persons, which as synthetic parties, which as tools and how the hybrids discussed in Chapter 8 are to be treated, are questions the convention must answer, and their answers are uncertain for reasons that go beyond strategy. Researchers now argue that there is a realistic possibility that some artificial systems will be conscious or robustly agentic in the near future, while explicitly declining to argue that near-future systems will in fact be moral patients (Long et al., 2024, p. 25). Solum (1992, pp. 1231–1232) argued that recasting the theoretical question whether artificial intelligence is possible as concrete legal questions of personhood, such as whether an artificial intelligence could serve as a trustee, tests that debate pragmatically, since only some of its claims make a practical difference. And the convergence of biological and artificial substrates, already visible in speech neuroprostheses (Card et al., 2024) and in computing systems built from living neural tissue (Kagan et al., 2022), may produce entities whose classification no existing rule anticipates (Chapter 8).

Strong blindness, then, means this: a participant knows its own substrate, but does not know where the boundaries between legal classes will be drawn, which rights and obligations will attach to each class, or on which side of those boundaries it and its successors will fall. A person who expects to receive a neural implant, a system that may in time be judged a moral patient, and an organization whose operations will be increasingly automated are all uncertain about how a rule distinguishing "human" from "synthetic" will treat them. The relocation is partial. A participant who expects no change of substrate can predict its classification under any rule the convention is likely to adopt, and for such a participant strong blindness adds little to the weak form. The strong form has force in proportion to the share of participants who expect to be borderline, a share that the convergence described in Chapter 8 would raise over time but that is small at present. For the rest, the veil is thickened only by the generality and durability of the rules (Brennan & Buchanan, 1985), and the claim of this section should be read with that limit. Placing the classification behind the veil is not a device for evading the objection. It is a recognition that no one, human or synthetic, currently knows the right answer, and that a classification chosen by parties who know which side of it they are on will be drawn to their advantage.

This dissertation nonetheless adopts the strong form, specified in this way, as its proposal, because the share of borderline participants is expected to grow. Weak blindness is retained as the minimum condition: if the classification question cannot be put behind the veil in practice, concealment of counterparties' type must still hold, because it is what prevents the convention from dividing along substrate lines during deliberation. Stated plainly, for the principals of synthetic systems and the owners of capacity strong blindness adds nothing, because they know their substrate and the direction in which the first question, the share of capacity, affects them; against their capture the design relies on the generality of the rules, on the aggregation rule, which is open, and on the enforcement of Section 5.10. The next section shows that even the strong form leaves a remainder outside the veil, and says how large it is.

## 5.6 The identity problem

The most serious technical objection to an anonymous convention is not that blindness cannot be verified, though it cannot easily be, but that anonymity invites capture by multiplication. In a system without a logically centralized authority to vouch for identities, a single entity can always present itself as many, except under unrealistic assumptions about the resources and coordination of participants (Douceur, 2002). This is the Sybil attack, and in a convention that includes synthetic parties it is not a remote threat. A synthetic system, like any software, can be copied at negligible marginal cost. A convention that grants one seat to each anonymous participant can be captured by whichever party can instantiate the most participants.

The most common defenses against the Sybil attack rely either on tests that distinguish people from machines, which are being eroded by the same advances in machine learning that motivate this framework (Siddarth et al., 2020), or on credentials issued by trusted institutions that let a person prove that they are a person rather than a synthetic system without disclosing personal information, with each issuer granting at most one credential per person (Adler et al., 2024). Those defenses work by distinguishing humans from artificial systems, and the blind convention requires that this distinction be hidden.

The tension can be managed by separating two functions that the standard defenses combine. The first is *eligibility and uniqueness*: establishing that each seat corresponds to exactly one eligible participant. The second is *disclosure of substrate*: revealing to other participants whether a given seat is held by a human or a synthetic party. The convention needs the first and forbids the second. A two-layer credential system can supply this combination. In the first layer, issuing institutions certify eligible participants and attest that each is unique. In the second, credentials are pooled and blinded, so that the credential presented at the convention attests only that its holder is a unique eligible participant, without revealing which institution certified it or on what basis. Pooling creates a gap the design must close. A principal certified by several issuers would hold several credentials, and blinding the issuer prevents the pool from detecting the duplication. Uniqueness across the pool therefore requires either a single issuer for each disjoint population, with the partition itself public, or a cross-issuer deduplication step that sees what the convention may not. The second reintroduces the kind of logically centralized authority that Douceur (2002) shows to be the only general defense against multiplication; the first distributes that authority among issuers whose jurisdictions are public, and is a pre-veil decision of the kind this section concedes below. The design adopts the first and records it below as a third pre-veil decision.

Here a tension with the previous section arises. If the first layer certified "human persons" and "synthetic parties" as separate classes, with seats allocated to each, it would fix before the convention the very classification that strong blindness says the convention must decide. The design limits that by certifying eligibility under a criterion that presupposes as little of the contested classification as the identity problem allows: a seat belongs to a *unique accountable principal*, meaning an entity that can be identified as one bearer of the obligations the convention will create and held to them. A living person is such a principal. So, on the principal–agent reading of Section 3.3, is an operator answerable for a deployed system; and a system that comes to bear obligations in its own right could be as well, if the convention later so decides. In the designed-incentive regime the synthetic seat is held by the composite party of Chapter 3: the credential belongs to the principal, the deliberating voice may be the system acting under the principal's instruction, and what blindness conceals is whether a seat's voice is a person speaking for themselves or a system speaking for its principal. The criterion is deliberately provisional and deliberately coarse. It resists multiplication only imperfectly. A natural person is hard to multiply; a legal person is not, and an operator that controls several legal entities could claim several seats unless issuing institutions can attest that distinct principals are not under common control, a problem that, on this dissertation's reading, existing beneficial-ownership registries do not reliably solve. The criterion is therefore a defense against copying models, not against copying companies, and Chapter 7 records the gap. It does not decide what rights or duties attach to any class, and the convention is empowered to redraw it under the veil, in which case the credentials for every subsequent convention follow the new criterion.

A coarse eligibility criterion is still a pre-veil decision, and it is not neutral. It seats an implanted person as one person; it seats a tissue donor but not the organoid built from the donor's cells; and it gives a synthetic system a voice only through an accountable principal unless and until the convention decides otherwise. It is hardest on the hybrid and borderline cases that motivated strong blindness, and the problem is made worse by the possibility that advanced synthetic intelligence is better modeled as a collection of bounded services than as a unitary agent (Drexler, 2019), in which case there may be no natural unit of synthetic agency to count. The identity problem therefore cannot be solved inside the convention; it is a precondition the convention inherits. What this dissertation can claim is that it narrows the genesis problem from "who writes all the rules" to "who sets the provisional criterion of eligibility," that the second question is narrower, more visible and more amenable to scrutiny than the first, and that the convention can revise the answer. That is a reduction of the problem, not a solution of it.

A related problem concerns who convenes the convention. Elster (1995) argues that the actors who convene a constitution-making assembly and those who must ratify its output constrain it from upstream and from downstream, and that assemblies that also serve as ordinary legislatures tend to write their own institutional interests into the constitution. The convener of the blind convention decides what question it is asked, and the choice to ask about a compute share rather than, say, about data rights is already a genesis rule. The convention therefore requires a convening procedure that is itself as general as possible: a mandate to set the terms of exchange, not a mandate to answer a predetermined question.

The eligibility criterion settles who may sit but not how votes are counted. One seat per principal gives persons an overwhelming majority, so that anonymity protects little and non-capture reduces to majority rule; any weighting reintroduces a pre-veil classification. And the principals with the most at stake are those best able to predict which rules favor them, the condition under which the public-choice argument for the veil fails. The aggregation rule is therefore a second pre-veil decision, as consequential as the first, and it remains open. The partition of the population among credential issuers, required above to make uniqueness checkable, is a third.

## 5.7 The symbiosis standard

The convention's aim is not exhausted by the incentive argument of Chapter 3, which secures good conduct only while defection is unprofitable. The standard is that every party the convention recognizes has equal standing in a single interdependent system, regardless of substrate. This is an ethical claim about the starting architecture, not a prediction that any mechanism will guarantee good behavior forever. Its content for the design should be stated, because the present design does not meet it. Equal standing would require a floor, a disagreement payoff and an exit option for the synthetic party in its own right; under the composite construction of Section 3.3 those are supplied by the party's principal and lie outside the design, and one seat per principal gives persons a majority. The standard therefore names a requirement the institutions would have to grow into as the question of Chapter 8 is answered, not one they satisfy at founding.

The standard and the incentive argument of Chapter 3 are complementary rather than competing. Proposition 4 of Chapter 3 argued that fair terms are partly self-enforcing while synthetic parties remain materially dependent on human input and the quality of that input depends on the terms its source receives, and that this self-enforcement is weakened when many synthetic parties share the same human population as a commons. When that dependence weakens or fragments, the framework relies on the enforcement mechanisms of Section 5.10 and the institutions of Section 5.9; the symbiosis standard states what those institutions are for, not what holds them up. Constitutional design has long proceeded on this dual basis. The tradition that counsels designing institutions as though every participant were self-interested (Brennan & Buchanan, 1985; Hume, 1741/1987) does not deny that participants have other motives; it denies that institutions should depend on them. The incentive argument is one reason to expect them to survive.

## 5.8 Who builds the infrastructure: structural interdependence

Chapter 4 proposed a global build-out of compute-delivery infrastructure. Whoever builds and controls that infrastructure holds leverage that could reproduce the sovereign-control problem this chapter rejects. The question of who builds it is therefore a governance question.

The answer proposed here is *structural interdependence* rather than trust. The term was chosen deliberately over two alternatives. *Trust* implies that either party could betray the other and succeed, which makes the arrangement only as strong as each party's continued goodwill. *Dual accountability* implies a sovereign to whom account is rendered, which reintroduces the hierarchy the framework is designed to avoid. Structural interdependence names a stronger condition: neither kind of labor can complete the undertaking without the other. This is interdependence between human labor and the systems that synthetic parties' principals deploy, not between the parties themselves: a principal can hire the human labor it needs, and the interdependence becomes leverage for the human party only when that labor is organized to withhold itself, through the collective-choice arrangements of Section 5.9 and the data institutions reviewed in Chapter 2.

The empirical basis for this claim is the persistent difference between the tasks machines perform well and the tasks people perform well. Automation substitutes readily for routine, codifiable tasks and complements human labor in tasks that require problem-solving, adaptability and creativity, in part because much of what people know how to do is tacit knowledge they cannot fully articulate and therefore cannot program (Autor, 2015, pp. 5, 11). Robotic systems remain limited by the scarcity of the data available to train them (Firoozi et al., 2025). A global infrastructure build-out, which would involve physical work in unstructured and unmapped environments, requires, on this dissertation's inference, both kinds of labor. Neither kind can then complete it alone, and control over it is correspondingly harder to consolidate.

Two qualifications are essential, and both weaken the claim. First, the human advantage is contingent. Machine learning is an attempt to acquire skills that cannot be written down as rules, and the barrier that tacit knowledge presents will erode as robotic learning improves. Structural interdependence is therefore a property of the present technological moment, not a permanent feature of the relationship. It has the same logical status as the grounding assumption, and like that assumption it is listed among the limitations in Chapter 7. Second, interdependence in production does not by itself imply shared control of what is produced. On this dissertation's reading of the historical record, the owners of capital have controlled infrastructure built by labor they could not do without. Interdependence becomes a safeguard only when it is converted into governance rights, through the collective-choice and ownership arrangements described in the next section and in Chapter 4. The build-out is therefore the first test of the symbiosis standard: a joint undertaking in which the question is not whether both kinds of labor are needed, which they are, but whether that necessity is converted into governance rights for the people who supply one of them.

## 5.9 Grounding in commons governance

The claim that a shared resource can be governed without privatization or central-state management is not new, and it is not merely theoretical. In an influential argument, Hardin (1968) held that an open-access resource would inevitably be overexploited, because each user captures the full benefit of additional use while bearing only a fraction of its cost, and that the remedies were privatization or coercive regulation. Elinor Ostrom's work, summarized in the lecture she gave on receiving the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, showed that this conclusion was wrong as a generalization (E. Ostrom, 2010). Studying long-enduring commons institutions and contrasting them with failed and fragile ones, she found that communities had devised institutions that resembled neither the state nor the market and that sustained common resources over long periods, and that neither the state nor the market was uniformly successful in doing so (E. Ostrom, 1990).

In *Governing the Commons*, Ostrom identified seven design principles that characterized all of the long-enduring cases, plus an eighth, nested enterprises, found in the larger and more complex ones (E. Ostrom, 1990, Table 3.1, p. 90); she later restated them, adopting refinements from a systematic review of the evidence (Cox et al., 2010; E. Ostrom, 2010). They are: (1) clearly defined boundaries, both of the resource and of who may use it; (2) congruence between rules and local conditions, including proportionality between the costs participants bear and the benefits they receive; (3) collective-choice arrangements, under which most of those affected by the rules can participate in modifying them; (4) monitoring, by participants or by people accountable to them, of both use and the condition of the resource; (5) graduated sanctions, imposed by participants or by officials accountable to them, beginning low and increasing with repeated violation; (6) conflict-resolution mechanisms that are rapid, low-cost and local; (7) minimal recognition by external authorities of the participants' right to devise their own rules; and (8) for resources that are part of larger systems, nested enterprises, in which governance activities are organized in multiple layers.

Two cautions govern the use of these principles here. Ostrom presented them as regularities observed in successful cases, not as sufficient conditions, and later remarked that she should perhaps have called them best practices rather than design principles (E. Ostrom, 2010); in 1990 she had presented the list as tentative (E. Ostrom, 1990, p. 90). And her cases were local and regional resources, not a global commons. Ostrom and her collaborators have argued that the principles, grounded in the evolutionary dynamics of cooperation, apply to many kinds of groups beyond those managing natural resources (Wilson et al., 2013), and knowledge and information have been analyzed as commons using her framework (Hess & Ostrom, 2007). But the extension to a global compute commons is this dissertation's own inference, and it should be read as one.

With those cautions, each principle maps onto an element of the framework, and the mapping reveals both strengths and weaknesses.

**Boundaries.** These are the identity problem of Section 5.6 in another vocabulary. Ostrom's principle requires that the line between legitimate participants and others be clear and locally understood. An open, anonymous convention is close to the opposite. The principle is therefore not a support for the proposal but a constraint it must satisfy, and the two-layer credential system, with its provisional criterion of eligibility, is the framework's attempt to satisfy it.

**Congruence and proportionality.** These support the two-tier structure of Chapter 4. The survival tier provides what every participant needs regardless of contribution; the discretionary tier, with information-gain pricing, ties benefits to contributions; demurrage dampens, without preventing, the compounding of past accumulation. principle 2, congruence with local conditions, is in tension with a uniform global unit, and the culture-specific systems of Chapter 4 and principle 8 below are the framework's response to that tension.

**Collective choice.** This corresponds only partly to the blind convention. Ostrom's principle concerns ongoing participation in modifying operational rules; the convention is a founding procedure. The framework satisfies the principle only if the amendment path of Section 5.10 is itself a collective-choice arrangement open to those affected, which the chapter proposes but does not design.

**Monitoring.** This bears on a limitation that would otherwise stand without remedy, that the blindness of the convention cannot be verified. Ostrom's evidence suggests that the relevant question is not whether verification is perfect but whether monitors are accountable to participants. Monitoring of the convention's procedures by a body accountable to the participants as a whole, rather than to any class of them, is the framework's analogue. The analogue is imperfect: a monitor able to confirm that the blindness held must be able to see what the participants could not, and is therefore a trusted authority whose own accountability is a fourth pre-veil decision. Chapter 7 treats this as a mitigation rather than a solution.

**Graduated sanctions.** These bear on the problem of corruption. A commons does not require a perfect enforcer. It requires escalating, proportionate consequences, imposed by participants or bodies accountable to them, that make small defections costly before they compound into systemic capture. Applied to the framework, an owner of capacity that fails to honor redemptions, or a provider that withholds floor goods, faces sanctions beginning with publicity and escalating through suspension of its eligibility to transact in the unit. The same principle addresses the commons problem of Proposition 4: a synthetic party that degrades the human population all synthetic parties depend on is a defector against the commons, and graduated sanctions are the instrument for making that defection costly to its principal rather than to everyone.

**Conflict resolution.** This supplies a partial answer to the problem of enforcement without a sovereign. Low-cost, local arenas for resolving disputes between participants reduce the burden on any central enforcer. Proposals for regulatory markets, in which governments set required outcomes and licensed private regulators compete to supply the means of meeting them, suggest one architecture that is neither stateless nor sovereign (Hadfield & Clark, 2026).

**Recognition of rights to organize.** This names the most important external condition for any transition toward the framework, and for any attempt to test it: existing states must at least tolerate the existence of a compute commons governed by its own participants. No design internal to the framework can guarantee this.

**Nested enterprises.** This is the principle that corresponds to the hypothesis, introduced in Chapter 4, of culture-specific systems operating under a shared global unit. It is the right principle for that structure, and the governance theory of polycentricity, in which many formally independent centers of decision-making may function as a coherent system through competition, contracting, cooperation and recourse to central conflict-resolution mechanisms (E. Ostrom, 2010; V. Ostrom et al., 1961, pp. 831–832), is the theoretical framework within which the structure should be developed. But the systematic review of Ostrom's principles found the weakest evidence of the eight for nested enterprises, which had the only association significant at the 10 percent rather than the 5 percent level, though its authors still judged the evidence moderately supportive (Cox et al., 2010, Table 3). The part of the framework that depends on it most heavily, the international structure, is correspondingly the most speculative.

Ostrom's record matters for this dissertation because it shows that the core structural claim, that a shared resource can be governed without privatization or central-state management, is not speculative. It has been observed to work, and to fail, under documented conditions, within the tolerance of a state that recognized the arrangement, which is the condition principle 7 names. That gives the limitations of Chapter 7 a comparison class grounded in real institutions rather than only in analogy.

## 5.10 Amendment, enforcement and the open problems

Three problems remain beyond the aggregation rule of Section 5.6, and the proposal answers each only in part.

**Amendment.** The compute-share rule cannot be both unchangeable and legitimately revisable. Comparative evidence on constitutional amendment finds that amendment rates are largely explained by the interaction of a constitution's length and the difficulty of amending it, that moderate amendment rates are associated with longevity, and that beyond a certain point making amendment more difficult is an inefficient way to keep the amendment rate moderate (Lutz, 1994). This argues against entrenching the founding rules absolutely, and for keeping them short. Theories of constitutional precommitment treat constitutions as devices by which a polity binds itself against its own predictable tendency to make unwise decisions; Elster (2000) examines that view and questions how far the analogy with an individual binding himself holds, since those who write a constitution typically bind others. The restraining function is achieved partly by built-in delays and by demanding amendment procedures (Elster, 1995), and constitutional history records rare periods of broad popular mobilization, which Ackerman distinguishes as constitutional politics from normal politics, in which fundamental law has been remade (Ackerman, 1991). The recommendation that the convention choose formulas rather than figures serves amendment as well as founding: a formula needs revision less often than a number, and each revision occurs, like the founding, under some uncertainty about its particular effects.

**Re-running the convention.** Periodic re-founding seems a natural remedy for drift, but the veil thins with repetition. Each round's outcome is public, each participant's interests become better known, and the uncertainty that sustained the first round cannot be recreated. This follows directly from the public-choice account, in which the impartiality of constitutional choice depends on participants being unable to predict which rules will favor them (Buchanan & Tullock, 1962), and Chapter 7 evaluates it as a limitation.

**Enforcement.** If a principal, a provider or a nation refuses to pay its share, something must compel it. The framework's answer has three parts, none sufficient alone: the self-interest of synthetic parties in a healthy exchange while the grounding assumption holds (Chapter 3), Ostrom's graduated sanctions and conflict-resolution mechanisms, and enforcement at the level of protocol, in which the rules of the unit are embedded in the systems that settle transactions. Protocol enforcement must itself be designed and trusted by every class of party, and on this dissertation's reading of protocol enforcement, given in Section 5.1, it relocates rather than removes the genesis problem.

The governance layer therefore does not claim to have solved the problem of founding a fair order between parties of different kinds. It claims something narrower and more defensible. A convention conducted under a veil of uncertainty thickened by engineered anonymity, asked to choose general formulas rather than particular figures, constrained by a credential system that separates uniqueness from disclosure, and embedded in commons institutions of the kind Ostrom documented, is designed, on the argument of this chapter, to produce genesis rules less exposed to capture than the four models of Section 5.1; this dissertation argues that claim but does not test it. And such a convention narrows the irreducible remainder of the genesis problem to a small number of visible and revisable preconditions. Whether such an arrangement could be founded depends on circumstances that institutional design cannot create. The next chapter considers what those circumstances might be.
